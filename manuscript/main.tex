\section*{Introduction}\label{sec:orientation}
The main structural emphasis of this manuscript is a paravector form of space-time algebra organized by three operations: the conjugate \((\cdot)^*\), the reverse \((\cdot)^{\mathsf R}\), and the scalar paravector metric \(\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2\). Writing \(Z\in\C\oplus\C^3\) as a complex scalar plus complex vector, or equivalently as real scalar, imaginary scalar, real vector, and imaginary vector parts, makes several layers of structure visible at once. The same product then encodes scalar-vector couplings, real-imaginary couplings, norms, interval formulas, field terms, and wave operators in one algebraic framework.

The document proceeds from algebraic construction to physical interpretation. It begins with the sigma basis, the conjugate and reverse operators, the decomposition of complex paravectors, dot and cross products, general paravector product laws, and the resulting norm and scalar-paravector-metric identities. It then develops projector algebra, exponential and logarithmic formulas, generalized transform identities, and the matrix and auxiliary \(2\times2\) realizations. Only after that algebraic setup is fixed does the presentation turn to space-time kinematics, differential-operator notation, electromagnetism, and relativistic wave equations.

This perspective is consistent with the modern STA and paravector literature of Hestenes, Baylis, and Doran--Lasenby \cite{hestenes1966,baylis1999,doran2003}, while its algebraic lineage runs back through Hamilton's quaternion construction and Clifford's geometric extension \cite{hamilton1844,clifford1878}. The present treatment emphasizes keeping the scalar/vector and real/imaginary decompositions explicit so that familiar physical relations appear as compact consequences of the same algebraic products. A one-page Equation Reference Sheet follows this introduction and collects the main numbered formulas.

Bold symbols denote three-vectors, and the remaining symbols are defined where they first appear. Whenever a new symbol is introduced, the text states whether it is a scalar, vector, or paravector and whether it is real or complex. Complexification is carried by the pseudoscalar \(i\). Natural units \(\hbar=c=1\) and metric signature \((+,-,-,-)\) are used unless stated otherwise.

\item Section \ref{sec:qm} develops the relativistic wave-equation material, from frequency splitting and Klein--Gordon factorization to the Dirac two-component field of complex paravectors, the Pauli limit, and the energy/projector split.

\item After the references, Section \ref{sec:flags} records scope notes and cautions, and Section \ref{sec:verification} records verification and formula status.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Algebraic Foundations}\label{sec:algebra}\label{sec:foundations}\label{sec:functions}
This section fixes the basis, the conjugate and reverse operators, complex paravectors, dot and cross products, norms and metrics, paravector products, projector formulas, exponential and logarithmic formulas, generalized transforms, and the standard matrix realizations.

\subsection{Basis and pseudoscalar}
For \(n\in\{1,2,3\}\), the algebra is generated by the three basis elements \(\sigma_n\). Their products generate scalars, vectors, bivectors, and pseudoscalars.
The basis relations are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_n^2&=1,\qquad \sigma_n\sigma_m=-\sigma_m\sigma_n\quad (n\neq m),\\
i&:=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3,\qquad i^2=-1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:basis}
\end{equation}
The element \(i\) is a pseudoscalar because \(i\sigma_n=\sigma_n i\), and squaring \(i\) returns the scalar \(-1\):
\begin{align*}
(\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3)^2
&= \sigma_1\sigma_2(\sigma_3\sigma_1)\sigma_2\sigma_3,\notag\\
&= -\sigma_1(\sigma_2\sigma_1)(\sigma_3\sigma_2)\sigma_3,\notag\\
&= -\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2\sigma_3^2,\notag\\
&= -1.
\end{align*}
With the Kronecker delta and Levi-Civita symbol,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_n\sigma_m=\delta_{nm}+\sum_{k=1}^3\epsilon_{nmk}\,i\sigma_k.
\label{eq:sigma-product}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align*}
\delta_{nm} &:= \begin{cases}
1,& n=m,\\
0,& n\neq m,
\end{cases}\\[0.25em]
\epsilon_{nmk} &:= \begin{cases}
+1,& (n,m,k)\text{ even permutation of }(1,2,3),\\
-1,& (n,m,k)\text{ odd permutation of }(1,2,3),\\
0,& \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{align*}

\subsection{The Conjugate and Reverse Operators}
The two basic operators are
\begin{align}
(\cdot)^* &= \eqtext{conjugate that flips signs of } \sigma_n,
\label{eq:star-definition}\\
(\cdot)^{\mathrm R} &= \eqtext{reverse order of multiplied } \sigma_n,
\label{eq:reversion-definition}
\end{align}
In operator form,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
{(XY)}^{*}&=X^{*}Y^{*},\qquad
{(XY)}^{\mathrm R}=Y^{\mathrm R}X^{\mathrm R},\\
X^{*\mathrm R}&=(X^{\mathrm R})^{*}=(X^{*})^{\mathrm R}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:operator-rules}
\end{equation}
Applied to the generators and the pseudoscalar, these rules reduce to
\begin{alignat}{3}
\conj{\sigma_n} &{}= -\sigma_n, \qquad&
\rev{\sigma_n} &{}= \sigma_n, \qquad&
\crev{\sigma_n} &{}= -\sigma_n,
\label{eq:sigma-operator-signs}\\
\conj{i} &{}= -i, \qquad&
\rev{i} &{}= -i, \qquad&
\crev{i} &{}= i.
\label{eq:i-operator-signs}
\end{alignat}
For a two-generator bivector pair with \(n\neq m\),
\begin{align}
(\sigma_n\sigma_m)^*
&= \sigma_n^*\sigma_m^*
= (-\sigma_n)(-\sigma_m)
= \sigma_n\sigma_m,\notag\\
(\sigma_n\sigma_m)^{\mathrm R}
&= \sigma_m^{\mathrm R}\sigma_n^{\mathrm R}
= \sigma_m\sigma_n
= -\sigma_n\sigma_m,\notag\\
(\sigma_n\sigma_m)^{*\mathrm R}
&= (\sigma_m\sigma_n)^*
= \sigma_m^*\sigma_n^* \notag\\
&= (-\sigma_m)(-\sigma_n)
= \sigma_m\sigma_n
= -\sigma_n\sigma_m.
\label{eq:bivector-operator-signs}
\end{align}
Equation \eqref{eq:bivector-operator-signs} records the same sign pattern on the sigma-number-two elements. These rules agree with standard STA conventions \cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}.

The sigma number of a reduced basis product is the number of sigma generators that remain after simplification. Thus \(1\), \(\sigma_n\), \(\sigma_n\sigma_m\) with \(n\neq m\), and \(i\) have sigma numbers \(0\), \(1\), \(2\), and \(3\). Any unreduced product with sigma number larger than \(3\) simplifies to one of these four cases. Tables \ref{tab:sigma-number-components} and \ref{tab:sigma-number-signs} summarize the basis elements and operator signs by sigma number.

\subsection{Generic Complex Paravector}
Collect the three generators into the sigma-vector
\begin{equation}
\sigv := \{ \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3 \},
\label{eq:sigma-vector}
\end{equation}
which is a vector of algebraic objects rather than a vector of scalar components. Multiplication by the pseudoscalar gives the associated bivector triple
\begin{equation}
i\sigv := \{ i\sigma_1,\ i\sigma_2,\ i\sigma_3 \}
= \{ \sigma_2\sigma_3,\ \sigma_3\sigma_1,\ \sigma_1\sigma_2 \},
\label{eq:isigma-vector}
\end{equation}
because \(i=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3\) and each product \(i\sigma_n\) reduces to the complementary ordered two-generator product. Equation \eqref{eq:isigma-vector} therefore records the canonical sigma-number-two basis built from the generators.
With this basis fixed, for real scalars \(a,b\in\R\) and real vectors \(\mathbf A,\mathbf B\in\R^3\), with \(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv=A_1\sigma_1+A_2\sigma_2+A_3\sigma_3\), define the real paravectors \(X,Y\in\R\oplus\R^3\) by
\begin{equation}
X=a+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:real-paravector-x}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
Y=b+\mathbf B\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:real-paravector-y}
\end{equation}
We can define a complex paravector \(Z\in\C\oplus\C^3\) either as a real paravector plus an imaginary paravector with
\begin{equation}
Z=X+iY.
\label{eq:paravector-xy}
\end{equation}
or as a scalar plus a vector component with
\begin{align}
Z&=(a+ib)+(\mathbf A+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv
\label{eq:paravector-cartesian}\\
 &=S+\mathbf V\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:paravector}
\end{align}
where the complex scalar and vector components are given by
\begin{equation}
S=a+ib\in\C.
\label{eq:paravector-scalar}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf V=\mathbf A+i\mathbf B\in\C^3.
\label{eq:paravector-vector}
\end{equation}
Define the real, imaginary, scalar, and vector functions by
\begin{align}
\eqtext{real}(Z)&:=X,
\label{eq:real-function}\\
\eqtext{img}(Z)&:=Y.
\label{eq:img-function}\\
\eqtext{scalar}(Z)&:=S,
\label{eq:scalar-function}\\
\eqtext{vector}(Z)&:=\mathbf V.
\label{eq:vector-function}
\end{align}
For the scalar part and vector part of the paravector \(Z\), we also write \(Z_{S}:=\eqtext{scalar}(Z)\) and \(Z_{\mathbf V}:=\eqtext{vector}(Z)\).

The conjugate and reverse operators act on the generic complex paravector by
\begin{align}
\conj{Z} &= (a-ib)+(-\mathbf A+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:paravector-conjugate}\\
\rev{Z} &= X-iY=(a-ib)+(\mathbf A-i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:paravector-reverse}\\
\crev{Z} &= (a+ib)-(\mathbf A+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:paravector-reverse-conjugate}
\end{align}
For the real paravector \(X\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x}, these reduce to
\begin{align}
\conj{X} &= a-\mathbf A\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:real-paravector-conjugate}\\
\rev{X} &= X=a+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:real-paravector-reverse}\\
\crev{X} &= a-\mathbf A\cdot\sigv=\conj{X}.
\label{eq:real-paravector-combined}
\end{align}
Equations \eqref{eq:paravector-conjugate}--\eqref{eq:paravector-reverse-conjugate} are the generic operator formulas used throughout the later algebraic constructions, while Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-conjugate}--\eqref{eq:real-paravector-combined} record the purely real specialization used whenever the imaginary scalar and imaginary vector parts vanish.
Equations \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x}--\eqref{eq:vector-function} fix the real and complex paravector pieces and extractors used throughout the later algebra. \subsection{Dot products and cross products}
For real vectors \(\mathbf A,\mathbf B\in\R^3\), the dot product is defined by
\begin{align}
\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B
&:= \sum_{n=1}^3\sum_{m=1}^3\delta_{nm}A_nB_m \notag\\
&= A_1B_1+A_2B_2+A_3B_3.
\label{eq:vector-dot-norm}
\end{align}
Define the square of a vector by
\begin{equation}
\mathbf A^2:=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf A.
\label{eq:vector-square}
\end{equation}
\noindent For the cross product, the Levi-Civita form gives
\begin{equation*}
(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)_k
\,:= \sum_{n=1}^3\sum_{m=1}^3\epsilon_{nmk}A_nB_m,
\end{equation*}
which gives the vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf A\times\mathbf B=
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&A_2B_3-A_3B_2,\\
&A_3B_1-A_1B_3,\\
&A_1B_2-A_2B_1
\end{aligned}
\right\}.
\label{eq:cross-product}
\end{equation}
Dot products are commutative, whereas cross products are antisymmetric:
\begin{align*}
\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B &= +\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf A,\\
\mathbf A\times\mathbf B &= -\mathbf B\times\mathbf A.
\end{align*}
With Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:basis} and \eqref{eq:sigma-product},
\begin{align*}
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)
&= \sum_{n=1}^3\sum_{m=1}^3 A_n B_m \sigma_n \sigma_m \notag\\
&= \sum_{n=1}^3 A_n B_n \notag\\
&\quad + \sum_{n<m}(A_n B_m-A_m B_n)\sigma_n \sigma_m \notag\\
&= A_1B_1+A_2B_2+A_3B_3 \notag\\
&\quad + (A_2B_3-A_3B_2)i\sigma_1 \notag\\
&\quad - (A_1B_3-A_3B_1)i\sigma_2 \notag\\
&\quad + (A_1B_2-A_2B_1)i\sigma_3.
\end{align*}
This can be efficiently expressed in terms of the dot product and cross product as
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B+i(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:dot-cross}
\end{equation}
Table \ref{tab:components-vector-product} records the same product in component form. These same dot-product and cross-product definitions extend componentwise to complex vectors in \(\C^3\), and they apply equally to combinations of vectors, vector-valued functions, and vector operators whenever the component expressions are defined.

The reversed order flips only the antisymmetric term:
\begin{align*}
(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)
&= \mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B-i(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv,\\
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)+(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)
&= 2\,\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B,\\
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)-(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)
&= 2i(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
The same product immediately gives
\begin{align*}
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)^2 &= \mathbf A^2,\\
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)^{2n} &= (\mathbf A^2)^n,\\
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)^{2n+1} &= (\mathbf A^2)^n(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv),\\
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)\sigv &= \mathbf A+i\,\mathbf A\times\sigv.
\end{align*}
These are the basic sigma-product identities from which the later paravector formulas are built.

\subsection{Products}
With Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dot-cross} fixing the scalar/vector split for real vectors, the fully complex product follows as the master formula from which the simpler cases are recovered by restriction.

For \(Z\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:paravector} and \(Z'=S'+\mathbf V'\cdot\sigv\in\C\oplus\C^3\) defined analogously to Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:paravector} and \eqref{eq:paravector-vector},
\begin{equation}
ZZ'=(SS'+\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf V')+\big(S\mathbf V'+S'\mathbf V+i(\mathbf V\times\mathbf V')\big)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:paravector-product}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:paravector} and \eqref{eq:paravector-product} fix the generic complex paravector and its master multiplication rule: pure scalar, real vector, real paravector, and mixed products all follow by setting the unused parts to zero.

\subsection{Norms and Metrics}
Using Eq.\ \eqref{eq:vector-square}, the product formulas above now collect the norm and scalar paravector metric identities used later in the paper.
For a complex scalar \(S=a+ib\in\C\), define the scalar norm by
\begin{equation}
|S|:=\sqrt{SS^*}.
\label{eq:scalar-norm}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
|S|^2&=SS^*\\
&=a^2+b^2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
If \(S\in\R\), then Eq.\ \eqref{eq:scalar-norm} reduces to the ordinary absolute value \(|S|=\sqrt{S^2}\).
For a complex vector \(\mathbf V=\mathbf A+i\mathbf B\in\C^3\), define the vector norm by
\begin{equation}
\|\mathbf V\|:=\sqrt{\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf V^*}.
\label{eq:vector-norm}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\|\mathbf V\|^2&=\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf V^*\\
&=\mathbf A^2+\mathbf B^2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The quadratic product \(\mathbf V^2=\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf V\) is not the same object as the positive norm square \(\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf V^*\).
For the complex paravector \(Z\) from Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:paravector}--\eqref{eq:paravector-cartesian}, define the paravector norm by
\begin{align}
\|Z\|
&:= \sqrt{|S|^2+\|\mathbf V\|^2},\label{eq:euclidean-form}\\
\|Z\|^2
&= |S|^2+\|\mathbf V\|^2\notag\\
&= a^2+b^2+\mathbf A^2+\mathbf B^2\notag\\
&= \eqtext{scalar}(Z\rev{Z})
= \eqtext{scalar}(\rev{Z}Z).\notag
\end{align}
This norm is therefore a nonnegative real scalar, and it is the reverse operator \((\cdot)^{\mathrm R}\) that supplies the quadratic form. For the scalar paravector metric, use the complex paravector \(Z\) from Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:paravector}--\eqref{eq:paravector-cartesian} and the real paravector \(X\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x}. The scalar paravector metric \(\langle\!\langle\cdot\rangle\!\rangle\) is defined by the principal square root of a scalar: its argument lies in \(\C\) for complex paravectors and in \(\R\) for real paravectors. Define the complex scalar paravector metric by
\begin{align}
\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle
&:= \sqrt{S^2-\mathbf V^2},\label{eq:minkowski-form}\\
\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2
&= S^2-\mathbf V^2\notag\\
&= (a^2-b^2-\mathbf A^2+\mathbf B^2)+2i(ab-\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B)\notag\\
&= Z\crev{Z}
= \crev{Z}Z.\notag
\end{align}
The product order does not matter because \(\crev{Z}Z=Z\crev{Z}\) is scalar. Thus the metric is built from the reverse-conjugate operator \((\cdot)^{*\mathrm R}\), not from the reverse alone.
For the real paravector \(X\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x}, note that \(X^{*\mathrm{R}}=X^*\), so the real scalar paravector metric is
\begin{align}
\langle\!\langle X\rangle\!\rangle^2
&= a^2-\mathbf A^2\notag\\
&= XX^*=X^*X.\label{eq:real-paravector-metric}
\end{align}
For \(Z,Z'\in\C\oplus\C^3\), the scalar paravector metric is multiplicative:
\begin{align}
\langle\!\langle ZZ'\rangle\!\rangle^2
&= ZZ'(ZZ')^{*\mathrm{R}}
= ZZ'Z'^{*\mathrm{R}}Z^{*\mathrm{R}}
= Z\bigl(Z'Z'^{*\mathrm{R}}\bigr)Z^{*\mathrm{R}}\notag\\
&= Z\,\langle\!\langle Z'\rangle\!\rangle^2\,Z^{*\mathrm{R}}
= \langle\!\langle Z'\rangle\!\rangle^2\,ZZ^{*\mathrm{R}}
= \langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2\langle\!\langle Z'\rangle\!\rangle^2\notag\\
&= \langle\!\langle Z'Z\rangle\!\rangle^2.
\label{eq:metric-product}
\end{align}
Here \((ZZ')^{*\mathrm{R}}=Z'^{*\mathrm{R}}Z^{*\mathrm{R}}\), and \(\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2\) and \(\langle\!\langle Z'\rangle\!\rangle^2\) are scalars.
Under complex scalar multiplication, the norm and scalar paravector metric scale differently:
\begin{align}
\|r e^{i\theta} Z\|^2 &= r^2\|Z\|^2,
\label{eq:norm-scaling}\\
\langle\!\langle r e^{i\theta} Z\rangle\!\rangle^2
&= r^2 e^{2i\theta}\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2.
\label{eq:metric-scaling}
\end{align}
The norm is phase-insensitive, while the scalar paravector metric retains the phase square. Equations \eqref{eq:vector-square}--\eqref{eq:metric-scaling} therefore collect the full norm and scalar paravector metric structure used later in the paper.

\subsection{Inverse formulas}
The inverse formulas are the algebraic consequence of the scalar paravector metric identities from Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:minkowski-form} and \eqref{eq:metric-product}. For \(Z=S+\mathbf V\cdot\sigv\in\C\oplus\C^3\), when \(\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2\neq 0\), the inverse may be written in any of the equivalent forms
\begin{align}
Z^{-1}
&:= \frac{\crev{Z}}{\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2}
= \frac{\crev{Z}}{Z\crev{Z}}
= \frac{\crev{Z}}{\crev{Z}Z}\notag\\
&= \frac{S-\mathbf V\cdot\sigv}{S^2-\mathbf V^2}.
\label{eq:paravector-inverse}
\end{align}
If the scalar paravector metric vanishes, the inverse does not exist.

Differentiating the identity \(Z^{-1}Z=1\) gives
\begin{align}
\dd(Z^{-1}) &= -Z^{-1}(\dd Z)Z^{-1}.
\label{eq:paravector-inverse-diff}
\end{align}
For \(Z'=S'+\mathbf V'\cdot\sigv\in\C\oplus\C^3\), if \(\mathbf V\times\mathbf V'=\mathbf 0\), then
\begin{equation}
Z^{-1}Z'=Z'Z^{-1}.
\label{eq:inverse-commuting}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Projector Algebra}\label{sec:projectors}
Projectors are introduced next because they organize the later function formulas and ladder constructions. Once Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:sigma-product} and \eqref{eq:dot-cross} are fixed, the projector calculus gives the cleanest route from simple unit-direction elements to more general paravector decompositions.

If \(\mathbf A\in\R^3\) is written as \(\mathbf A=\theta\mathbf u\) with \(\theta=\|\mathbf A\|\), \(\mathbf u\in\R^3\), and \(\mathbf u^2=1\), define the idempotents
\begin{equation}
\Pi(\pm):=\frac{1}{2}(1\pm \mathbf u\cdot\sigv)
\label{eq:projectors}
\end{equation}
satisfying $\Pi(\pm)^2=\Pi(\pm)$, $\Pi(+)\Pi(-)=0$, $\Pi(+)+\Pi(-)=1$, and
\((\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)\Pi(\pm)=\pm\Pi(\pm)\).
Algebraically, \(\Pi(+)\) and \(\Pi(-)\) isolate the parts aligned and anti-aligned with the direction \(\mathbf u\). Their role here is purely structural: they split an object into two parts adapted to a chosen axis.

For a scalar-valued function \(f:\{\pm 1\}\to\C\),
\begin{equation}
f(\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)=f(+)\Pi(+)+f(-)\Pi(-).
\label{eq:functional-calculus}
\end{equation}
This gives a compact projector-based formula for roots, inverses, exponentials, logarithms, and related functions. Once the minimal polynomial of an STA element is known, functions of that element can be defined without leaving the algebraic framework. The detailed formulas begin with projector and eigenvalue identities and continue to ladder operations associated with a chosen axis.

About the \(z\) axis, define the ladder elements
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
a(\pm)&:=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_1\pm i\sigma_2),\qquad
[\sigma_3,a(\pm)]=\pm 2a(\pm),\\
a(\pm)^2&=0,\qquad
a(+)a(-)=\Pi(+),\qquad a(-)a(+)=\Pi(-).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ladder}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:ladder} records the nilpotent combinations of \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\) that exchange the two projector parts selected by Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projectors}. The projector eigenvalue formulas, mixed projector products, and ladder identities built from Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:projectors}, \eqref{eq:functional-calculus}, and \eqref{eq:ladder} are collected next.

\section{Space-Time Kinematics}\label{sec:spacetime}
The same paravector structure also carries a space-time interpretation: the scalar paravector metric becomes the Minkowski metric of the interval, and normalized time-like paravectors become proper velocities. This interpretation follows the relativistic kinematic development of Lorentz, Einstein, and Minkowski, recast here in paravector form \cite{lorentz1904,einstein1905,minkowski1908,hestenes1966,baylis1999}.

\subsection{Differential space-time and proper velocity}
Specializing the real paravector \(X\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x} to differentials gives
\begin{equation}
\dd X=\dd t+\dd\mathbf r\cdot\sigv
\label{eq:dX}
\end{equation}
and the interval is the differential version of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:minkowski-form}:
\begin{equation}
\dd s^2=\langle\!\langle \dd X\rangle\!\rangle^2=\langle\!\langle \dd t+\sigv\cdot\dd\mathbf r\rangle\!\rangle^2=\dd X\,\conj{\dd X}
=\dd t^2-\dd\mathbf r^2.
\label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
Here \(t,s,\gamma\in\R\), \(\mathbf r,\mathbf v,\mathbf P\in\R^3\), and \(X,U\in\R\oplus\R^3\). The scalar part \(\dd t\) is the temporal separation and the vector part \(\dd\mathbf r\cdot\sigv\) is the spatial separation. Equation \eqref{eq:interval} is therefore not an added metric axiom but the direct spacetime interpretation of the scalar paravector metric already introduced in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:minkowski-form}.

If \(\mathbf v=\dd\mathbf r/\dd t\in\R^3\) and \(\gamma=(1-\mathbf v^2)^{-1/2}\in\R\), then
\begin{equation}
U:=\frac{\dd X}{\dd s}=\gamma(1+\mathbf v\cdot\sigv),
\qquad
\langle\!\langle U\rangle\!\rangle^2=1.
\label{eq:proper-velocity}
\end{equation}
Multiplying Eq.\ \eqref{eq:proper-velocity} by the real rest mass \(m\in\R\) gives the proper momentum formula
\begin{equation}
mU=E+\mathbf P\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
E=m\gamma,\qquad \mathbf P=m\gamma\mathbf v,
\label{eq:proper-momentum}
\end{equation}
which immediately yields $E^2-\mathbf P^2=m^2$.
The normalization \(\langle\!\langle U\rangle\!\rangle^2=1\) is the essential point: proper velocity is the unit time-like paravector tangent to the worldline. Multiplying by \(m\) then produces the energy--momentum paravector in a way that makes the relativistic mass shell relation appear immediately.

\subsection{Proper acceleration and free motion}
The proper acceleration is the real paravector
\begin{equation}
A:=\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}=\frac{\dd^2 X}{\dd s^2},
\qquad
\eqtext{scalar}(\conj{U}A)=0.
\label{eq:proper-acceleration}
\end{equation}
The orthogonality condition in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:proper-acceleration} is the paravector analogue of the standard four-acceleration orthogonality relation. It is included here because the Lorentz-force law later in the paper should be read as a specific dynamical realization of this general kinematic structure.

A free-particle Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{free}}=-\frac{m}{\gamma},
\label{eq:free-lagrangian}
\end{equation}
whose Euler--Lagrange equations reproduce inertial motion and the usual nonrelativistic expansion
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{free}}=-m+\frac{1}{2}m\mathbf v^2+O(\mathbf v^4).
\label{eq:free-lagrangian-low}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:free-lagrangian-low} is worth displaying explicitly because it shows how the relativistic action reduces to the classical kinetic expression plus an additive rest-energy constant when \(\|\mathbf v\|\ll 1\).

\section{Differential Operators}\label{sec:differential}
The electromagnetic and wave-equation sections use one fixed set of differential conventions. They are collected here once so the later sections can refer back to the same numbered equations instead of redefining the operators in multiple places.

For spatial derivatives with respect to \(\mathbf r=(r_1,r_2,r_3)\), define
\begin{equation}
\delr:=\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial r_1},\frac{\partial}{\partial r_2},\frac{\partial}{\partial r_3}\right\}.
\label{eq:delr}
\end{equation}
The gradient notation is then
\begin{equation}
\gradv:=\delr
=\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial r_1},\frac{\partial}{\partial r_2},\frac{\partial}{\partial r_3}\right\}
=\{\partial_1,\partial_2,\partial_3\}.
\label{eq:gradient-notation}
\end{equation}
For shorthand, the coordinate symbol \(\mathbf r\) is dropped in the last form, so \(\partial_i:=\partial/\partial r_i\) for \(i\in\{1,2,3\}\). The corresponding spatial Laplacian is
\begin{equation}
\gradv^2:=\partial_1^2+\partial_2^2+\partial_3^2.
\label{eq:laplacian}
\end{equation}
The STA differential paravector and its conjugate are
\begin{equation}
\partial:=\partial_t+\sigv\cdot\gradv
=\partial_t+\sigma_1\partial_1+\sigma_2\partial_2+\sigma_3\partial_3.
\label{eq:partial-operator}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\conj{\partial}:=\partial_t-\sigv\cdot\gradv
=\partial_t-\sigma_1\partial_1-\sigma_2\partial_2-\sigma_3\partial_3.
\label{eq:partial-conjugate}
\end{equation}
Their product gives the d'Alembertian
\begin{align}
\Box&:=\langle\!\langle \partial\rangle\!\rangle^2
\label{eq:dalembertian-norm}\\
&=\partial\conj{\partial}\notag\\
&=\conj{\partial}\partial\notag\\
&=\partial_t^2-\gradv^2.
\label{eq:dalembertian}
\end{align}

\section{Electro-Magnetic Fields}\label{sec:em}
Electro-magnetic fields, gauge structure, and the Lorentz force admit compact STA forms. In physical content this is Maxwell theory \cite{maxwell1865}, while the paravector packaging follows the later STA presentations of Hestenes, Baylis, and Doran--Lasenby \cite{hestenes1966,baylis1999,doran2003}.

\subsection{Fields, potentials, and Maxwell equations}
The differential conventions are fixed once in Section \ref{sec:differential}; in particular, Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:delr}--\eqref{eq:dalembertian} define the spatial derivative vector, the shorthand \(\partial_i\), the paravector derivative, its conjugate, and the d'Alembertian. With those operators fixed, and with \(V,\rho,\lambda,S,q\in\R\), \(\mathbf E,\mathbf B,\mathbf A,\mathbf J\in\R^3\), the electromagnetic field vector and potential are written as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf F:=\mathbf E+i\mathbf B,
\qquad
F:=\mathbf F\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
\Phi=V+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:F-Phi}
\end{equation}
Here \(\Phi\in\R\oplus\R^3\), \(\mathbf F\in\C^3\) is the complex field vector, and \(F\in\varnothing\oplus\C^3\) is the corresponding STA field paravector. Equation \eqref{eq:F-Phi} packages electric and magnetic fields into one object \(\mathbf F\), scalar and vector potentials into one object \(\Phi\), and then lets the single symbol \(F\) carry the STA field notation used below.

\subsection{Boost transformation of electro-magnetic fields}
The same boost element \(L\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:boost} acts on the field paravector by
\begin{equation}
F'=\conj{L}FL.
\label{eq:field-transform}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:field-transform} is the direct field analogue of the event transform \(\conj{L}XL\). It shows that \(F\) is not an extra object appended to the mechanics, but part of the same algebraic structure: boosts act on the field paravector by the same two-sided rule that acts on space-time paravectors, and the usual parallel/perpendicular split of \(\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf B\) follows from the same boost algebra.

The identity
\begin{equation}
\partial\Phi = S+\conj{F},
\qquad
S:=\partial_t V+\gradv\cdot\mathbf A
\label{eq:potential-gradient}
\end{equation}
encodes
\begin{equation}
\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf A-\gradv V,
\qquad
\mathbf B=\gradv\times\mathbf A.
\label{eq:E-B-from-potential}
\end{equation}
The compact Maxwell equation is
\begin{equation}
\partial F=\rho-\mathbf J\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:maxwell}
\end{equation}
Splitting real/imaginary scalar/vector parts of \eqref{eq:maxwell} reproduces
\begin{align}
\gradv\cdot\mathbf E&=\rho,
&
\gradv\cdot\mathbf B&=0,
\label{eq:maxwell-scalars}\\
\gradv\times\mathbf B&=\mathbf J+\partial_t\mathbf E,
&
\gradv\times\mathbf E&=-\partial_t\mathbf B.
\label{eq:maxwell-vectors}
\end{align}
Equations \eqref{eq:maxwell-scalars} and \eqref{eq:maxwell-vectors} are standard STA/GA electrodynamics up to sign conventions fixed by Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:partial-operator}, \eqref{eq:partial-conjugate}, \eqref{eq:dalembertian}, and \eqref{eq:F-Phi}; component by component they recover Maxwell's field equations in the present signature and field convention \cite{maxwell1865,hestenes1966,baylis1999,jackson1998}.
Equation \eqref{eq:maxwell} avoids taking divergence and curl as separate primitive operators at this stage. They emerge automatically when the scalar and vector parts of the single STA equation are separated.

\subsection{Wave equations, gauge, and Lorentz force}
Applying $\conj{\partial}$ to \eqref{eq:maxwell} gives
\begin{equation}
\Box F=\conj{\partial}(\rho-\mathbf J\cdot\sigv),
\label{eq:wave-F}
\end{equation}
whose scalar part yields charge conservation,
\begin{equation}
\partial_t\rho+\gradv\cdot\mathbf J=0.
\label{eq:charge-conservation}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:charge-conservation} is the continuity equation written in the present notation.
For a real scalar gauge function \(\lambda\in\R\),
\begin{equation}
\Phi'=\Phi+\conj{\partial}\lambda,
\qquad
F'=F.
\label{eq:gauge}
\end{equation}
This gauge transformation shows why the potential \(\Phi\) is retained even when the field is physically primary: \(\Phi\) carries the redundancy, while \(F\) carries the gauge-invariant derivative content.

For a particle of real mass \(m\in\R\), real charge \(q\in\R\), and proper velocity \(U\in\R\oplus\R^3\), the proper-force formula is
\begin{equation}
m\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}=q\,\eqtext{real}\bigl(FU\bigr)
=q\gamma\Big(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf E+(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv\Big),
\label{eq:lorentz-force}
\end{equation}
which packages both relativistic power and the Lorentz three-force.
The real part in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:lorentz-force} extracts the physically relevant part of the product \(FU\). The scalar term gives power transfer and the vector term gives the ordinary Lorentz force, so the standard split between energy change and momentum change is preserved inside one STA formula.

\subsection{Lagrangian and energy-momentum remarks}
The electromagnetic particle Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=-\frac{m}{\gamma}-q(V-\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf A),
\label{eq:em-lagrangian}
\end{equation}
and its Euler--Lagrange equations return $\dd\mathbf P/\dd t=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$.
Here the free action from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:free-lagrangian} is supplemented by the potential term, and the field-strength form of the force law reappears after variation.

A natural paravector candidate for field energy density and flux is
\begin{equation}
T:=\frac{1}{2}F\rev{F}
=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf E^2+\mathbf B^2)+(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:energy-momentum-paravector}
\end{equation}
\flag{Caution.} Equation \eqref{eq:energy-momentum-paravector} is a useful frame-dependent energy-density/Poynting paravector, but by itself it is not the full electromagnetic stress-energy tensor. In STA language the full stress-energy object is more naturally a linear map such as \(T(a)=\frac{1}{2}F\,a\,\rev{F}\) \cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}.

\section{Relativistic Wave Equations and Spin Dynamics}\label{sec:qm}
The relativistic wave-equation development is a second application of the same algebra: first the scalar wave equation, then minimal coupling and factorization, and only then the two-component paravector factorization, the projector split, and the Pauli limit. The sequence follows the historical progression from Klein and Gordon to Dirac and Pauli, but it is rewritten throughout in the common paravector language established earlier \cite{klein1926,gordon1926,dirac1928,pauli1927}.

\subsection{Klein--Gordon and frequency splitting}
For a complex scalar field \(\Psi\in\C\),
\begin{equation}
(\partial\conj{\partial}+m^2)\Psi=0
\label{eq:kg-free}
\end{equation}
is the free Klein--Gordon equation \cite{klein1926,gordon1926}. Define also
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Omega&:=\langle\!\langle m+\sigv\cdot\gradv\rangle\!\rangle=\sqrt{m^2-\gradv^2},\\
(\partial_t\pm i\Omega)\Psi(\pm)&=0,\qquad
\Psi=\Psi(+)+\Psi(-),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:frequency-split}
\end{equation}
which is the positive/negative-frequency factorization of \eqref{eq:kg-free}.
This factorization is useful both conceptually and technically. It separates opposite-frequency branches of the scalar theory, and it also prepares the reader for the later factorizations in which second-order relativistic equations are rewritten as first-order systems.

\subsection{Minimal coupling and operator factorization}
Minimal coupling enters directly at the operator level. Rather than modifying each component equation separately, one defines energy and momentum operators that already incorporate the potential \(\Phi\) established in Section \ref{sec:em}.

Here \(\Psi(\pm)\in\C\), while \(\Omega\) is a scalar differential operator built from \(m\in\R\) and \(\gradv\). Define also the scalar differential operator \(E\) and vector differential operator \(\mathbf P\) by
\begin{equation}
E:=i\partial_t-qV,
\qquad
\mathbf P:=-i\gradv-q\mathbf A.
\label{eq:E-P-operators}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2)\Psi=0
\label{eq:kg-coupled}
\end{equation}
expands to the minimal-coupling Klein--Gordon operator
\begin{equation}
\Big(\Box+iqS-q^2\langle\!\langle\Phi\rangle\!\rangle^2+m^2+2iq(V\partial_t+\mathbf A\cdot\gradv)\Big)\Psi=0.
\label{eq:kg-expanded}
\end{equation}
\noindent The basic covariant-momentum commutators are
\begin{equation}
[E,P_m]=iqE_m,
\qquad
[P_m,P_n]=iq(\partial_m A_n-\partial_n A_m),
\label{eq:commutators}
\end{equation}
and using Eq.\ \eqref{eq:commutators}
one gets the useful factorization
\begin{equation}
(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)(E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)=E^2-\mathbf P^2-iqF.
\label{eq:EP-factorization}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:EP-factorization} is the bridge between the Klein--Gordon and Dirac-type formulas below. The square of a first-order operator built from \(E\) and \(\mathbf P\) produces the second-order relativistic operator together with the field term, which is the structural fact needed before introducing a Dirac-type equation.

\subsection{Dirac Two-Component Form, Gauge Covariance, and Pauli Limit}
Using the auxiliary \(2\times2\) map from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:W-map}, the free and coupled Dirac operators act on a two-component field \(\Psi=\{\Psi_1;\Psi_2\}\) whose components \(\Psi_1,\Psi_2\in\C\oplus\C^3\) are complex paravectors:
\begin{align}
D_0&:=W(i\partial)-m\I,
\qquad D_0\Psi=0,
\label{eq:dirac-free}\\
D&:=W(i\partial-q\conj{\Phi})-m\I,
\qquad D\Psi=0.
\label{eq:dirac-coupled}
\end{align}
Multiplying \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled} by $D':=W(i\partial-q\conj{\Phi})+m\I$ yields
\begin{equation}
D'D\Psi=0
\;\Rightarrow\;
\begin{cases}
(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2+iq\conj{F})\Psi_1=0,\\[2pt]
(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2+iqF)\Psi_2=0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:dirac-square}
\end{equation}
The calculation in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-square} explains why the two-component construction is more than formal packaging. Squaring the first-order system returns Klein--Gordon-type equations with the expected field couplings, so the Dirac form is seen as a controlled factorization of the relativistic wave operator.
For the ordering \(Z=i\partial-q\conj{\Phi}=E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P\) inside \(W(Z)\), the upper component carries \(+iq\conj{F}\) and the lower component carries \(+iqF\).

Gauge covariance combines the electromagnetic gauge law \eqref{eq:gauge} with the phase transformation of that same two-component field,
\begin{equation}
\Psi=e^{iq\lambda}\Psi',
\label{eq:dirac-gauge}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:dirac-gauge} leaves the form of \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled} unchanged.

For the low-energy reduction, using the operators from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:E-P-operators}, one obtains the leading-order Pauli equation for the same two-component field \(\Psi\),
\begin{equation}
2mE\Psi=(\mathbf P^2-q\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)\Psi.
\label{eq:pauli-operator}
\end{equation}
Solving Eq.\ \eqref{eq:pauli-operator} for \(i\partial_t\Psi\) gives the more familiar Schr\"odinger-type form
\begin{equation}
i\partial_t\Psi
\approx
\left(\frac{(-i\gradv-q\mathbf A)^2}{2m}+qV\right)\Psi
-\frac{q}{2m}(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)\Psi.
\label{eq:pauli}
\end{equation}
This matches the usual Zeeman term \cite{pauli1927,sakurai2017}, provided one remembers that \eqref{eq:pauli} is a leading-order $1/m$ approximation and that later specialization to uniform static $\mathbf B$ is used when decoupling the spin-up and spin-down parts.
The nonrelativistic theory therefore appears as an approximation internal to the relativistic one, not as an unrelated starting point. The Pauli equation is the leading low-energy shadow of the Dirac two-component field once the rest-energy scale has been separated and higher-order corrections are neglected.

If \(\mathbf B=\|\mathbf B\|\mathbf u\) with \(\mathbf B,\mathbf u\in\R^3\), \(\|\mathbf u\|=1\), and \(\mathbf B\) uniform and constant, decompose
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Psi&=\Psi(+)+\Psi(-),\qquad
\Psi(\pm)=\Pi(\pm)\Psi,\\
\Pi(\pm)&=\frac{1}{2}(1\pm \mathbf u\cdot\sigv).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pauli-spin-decomposition}
\end{equation}
With the projector decomposition \eqref{eq:pauli-spin-decomposition} in place, Eq.\ \eqref{eq:pauli} separates into
\begin{equation}
i\partial_t\Psi(\pm)=\left((\mathbf P^2\mp q\|\mathbf B\|)/(2m)+qV\right)\Psi(\pm).
\label{eq:pauli-spin-split}
\end{equation}
In this special case the projector language acquires an immediate physical meaning: the field direction selects a preferred axis, and the two projector parts evolve with opposite Zeeman shifts.
If the field direction or magnitude varies, the same projector family from Section \ref{sec:projectors} still organizes the state but need not decouple the dynamics. Writing
\begin{equation*}
H:=\frac{\mathbf P^2}{2m}+qV-\frac{q\lVert\mathbf B\rVert}{2m}\,\mathbf u\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
i\partial_t\Psi=H\Psi,
\end{equation*}
the projector-selected parts obey
\begin{align*}
i\partial_t\Psi(\pm)
&=i(\partial_t\Pi(\pm))\Psi+\Pi(\pm)(i\partial_t\Psi)\notag\\
&=H\Psi(\pm)+S(\pm),
\end{align*}
with coupling term
\begin{equation*}
S(\pm):=\bigl(i\partial_t\Pi(\pm)+[\Pi(\pm),H]\bigr)\Psi.
\end{equation*}
The uniform static-field split in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:pauli-spin-split} is precisely the case \(S(\pm)=0\).

\subsection{4-State Solution to the Dirac Equation}
For a free on-shell Dirac state, start from the unit momentum paravector
\begin{equation*}
U=\frac{dX}{ds}=\frac{E+\mathbf P\cdot\sigv}{m}.
\end{equation*}
On the mass shell,
\begin{equation*}
\langle\!\langle U\rangle\!\rangle^2
=\frac{E^2-\mathbf P^2}{m^2}
=1.
\end{equation*}
Using the auxiliary map from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:W-map},
\begin{equation*}
W(U)^2
=\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\langle\!\langle U\rangle\!\rangle^2,\langle\!\langle U\rangle\!\rangle^2\bigr)
=\I.
\end{equation*}
The corresponding energy projectors are
\begin{equation*}
\Lambda(\pm):=\frac{1}{2}(\I\pm W(U)),
\end{equation*}
where \(\Lambda(+)\) selects the positive-energy (matter) branch and \(\Lambda(-)\) the negative-energy (antimatter) branch. Since \(W(U)^2=\I\), they satisfy
\begin{equation*}
\Lambda(\pm)^2=\Lambda(\pm),
\qquad
\Lambda(+)\Lambda(-)=0,
\qquad
\Lambda(+)+\Lambda(-)=\I.
\end{equation*}
For an arbitrary spin axis \(\mathbf u\in\R^3\) with \(\mathbf u^2=1\), use also the spin projectors
\begin{equation*}
\Pi(\pm):=\frac{1}{2}(1\pm \mathbf u\cdot\sigv).
\end{equation*}
In the free operator picture, the unit momentum is represented by
\begin{equation*}
U\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{i\partial}{m}.
\end{equation*}
The mass-normalized positive-energy projector condition may therefore be written as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\left(W\!\left(\frac{i\partial}{m}\right)-\I\right)\Psi&=0,\\
(W(U)-\I)\Psi&=0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:normalized-dirac}
\end{equation}
\flag{Equation \eqref{eq:normalized-dirac} is not a replacement for the Dirac equation; it is the free on-shell projector relation obtained after normalizing momentum by $m$ so that $W(U)^2=\I$.}

Combining the energy split with the spin split gives the four sectors
\begin{equation}
\Psi(\epsilon,s):=\Lambda(\epsilon)\Pi(s)\Psi,
\qquad
\epsilon,s\in\{+,-\},
\label{eq:energy-spin-projectors}
\end{equation}
where \(\epsilon\) labels the energy sector and \(s\) the spin along \(\mathbf u\). The full Dirac state therefore decomposes as
\begin{equation*}
\Psi=\Psi(+,+)+\Psi(+,-)+\Psi(-,+)+\Psi(-,-).
\end{equation*}
This is the four-state decomposition of the free Dirac solution: two energy sectors and, within each, two spin sectors.

For a normalized complex paravector state \(\psi\in\C\oplus\C^3\), write the scalar projections
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\langle \psi \mid \psi \rangle &:= \eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\psi}\psi)=1,\\
\langle \psi \mid M \mid \psi \rangle &:= \eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\psi}M\psi).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Here \(M\) may be any algebra element with a well-defined product on \(\psi\). The bracket notation is only a compact mnemonic for scalar extraction on normalized paravector states; the underlying objects remain paravectors throughout.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}\label{sec:synthesis}
The algebraic rules fixed in Section \ref{sec:algebra} generate interval geometry, covariant transformations, electromagnetism, and relativistic wave equations without changing the underlying language. The later physical equations follow from the earlier algebraic setup rather than appearing as separate formalisms placed side by side.

The main structural point is the complex paravector split introduced in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:paravector}, \eqref{eq:real-function}, and \eqref{eq:img-function}. Once a quantity is resolved into real scalar, imaginary scalar, real vector, and imaginary vector parts, the same algebraic product can be read component by component across several parts of physics. In the space-time sections this split yields the interval and proper-velocity formulas. In electromagnetism it packages \(V\) with \(\mathbf A\), and \(\mathbf E\) with \(\mathbf B\), into one differential framework. In the wave-equation sections it reappears in the paired field couplings of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-square}, where the two component fields carry the conjugate pair \(+iq\conj{F}\) and \(+iqF\). The benefit is not only compact notation. The same bookkeeping keeps the scalar/vector content visible while also showing which formulas come in natural complementary pairs. Within the flat-space theory developed here, it also avoids explicit tensor notation through the main line of argument.

Electromagnetism is the clearest example. Equation \eqref{eq:maxwell} produces the sourced equations from the real scalar and real vector parts, while the complementary imaginary scalar and imaginary vector parts give the magnetic-divergence and Faraday relations in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:maxwell-scalars} and \eqref{eq:maxwell-vectors}. Tables \ref{tab:components-generic-derivative} and \ref{tab:components-generic-iderivative} show that this is not an isolated coincidence: once the full real/imaginary scalar/vector decomposition is kept, the derivative algebra naturally generates partner formulas in which electric and magnetic roles are exchanged. The same pattern appears in the force product. Equation \eqref{eq:lorentz-force} keeps the real part because it gives the observed power and Lorentz-force terms, while its imaginary companion is
\begin{equation*}
\eqtext{img}(FU)=\gamma\Big(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf B+(\mathbf B+\mathbf E\times\mathbf v)\cdot\sigv\Big),
\end{equation*}
which is the complementary electric-magnetic partner of the real-force expression, with the cross-product orientation adjusted by the algebraic product order.

Not every complementary component carries an accepted physical interpretation. Some are standard and necessary, such as the homogeneous Maxwell equations. Others are better read as algebraically natural partner relations that may encode dual descriptions, hidden symmetries, or possible extensions that have not been fully developed. That distinction matters. The complex scalar/vector paravector framework is rich enough to place established relativistic physics and these additional companion relations inside one common algebraic system. The remaining task is to determine which of these companion formulas are alternate forms of known results and which, if any, carry genuinely new physical content.

The extra companion formulas are therefore worth retaining in view, but they should be interpreted with discipline. Some are already standard parts of accepted theory, while others remain algebraically suggestive rather than physically established.

\subsection{Toward General Relativity}
The natural gravitational extension is already suggested by Section \ref{sec:transform-algebra}. There the basis was allowed to depend on a transform element \(T(x)\), giving the local sigma basis
\begin{equation*}
\sigma_n'(x):=T(x)^{-1}\sigma_n T(x),
\qquad
d\sigma_n'=[\sigma_n',T^{-1}dT].
\end{equation*}
To move toward general relativity, one would promote that moving basis from a calculational device to the actual local spacetime frame. The interval would then still be built from the same scalar paravector metric as before, but now with position-dependent basis elements:
\begin{equation*}
dX=dt+d\mathbf r\cdot\sigv '(x),
\qquad
ds^2=\langle\!\langle dX\rangle\!\rangle^2.
\end{equation*}
So the metric is still encoded by the same paravector product used throughout this paper; the new ingredient is that the sigma basis is no longer fixed globally.

The corresponding connection is the differential transform element already introduced in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:general-transform}:
\begin{equation*}
d\Theta:=T^{-1}dT.
\end{equation*}
The transformed-basis calculation in Section \ref{sec:transform-algebra} shows that only the parts of \(d\Theta\) that fail to commute with the basis move the frame. In the decomposition
\begin{equation*}
\Omega=T^{-1}(dT/dt)=\Omega_s+\tfrac12 (k+i\omega)\cdot\sigv ',
\end{equation*}
the real-vector part \(k\) is the infinitesimal boost data and the imaginary-vector part \(\omega\) is the infinitesimal rotation data. A curved extension would therefore treat these local boost and rotation pieces as the gravitational connection written in the document's own sigma language, rather than introducing separate Christoffel symbols from the start.

For any chosen coordinate direction \(d_a\), define the directional connection element
\begin{equation*}
\Omega_a:=T^{-1}(d_a T).
\end{equation*}
Then, for a transported paravector \(Z'=T^{-1}ZT\), the same transport identity already derived for \(X'=T^{-1}XT\) suggests the local derivative law
\begin{equation*}
D_a Z':=T^{-1}(d_a Z)T+[Z',\Omega_a].
\end{equation*}
Curvature is then the failure of these local derivatives to commute:
\begin{equation*}
[D_a,D_b]Z'=[Z',F_{ab}],
\end{equation*}
with curvature element
\begin{equation*}
F_{ab}:=d_a\Omega_b-d_b\Omega_a+[\Omega_a,\Omega_b].
\end{equation*}
This is the same algebraic pattern that already appeared in the moving-frame commutator formulas, now interpreted as genuine spacetime curvature. In other words, curvature would be the obstruction to patching the local transforms \(T(x)\) into one global flat frame.

The Einstein equation would then be most naturally written not as one bare paravector product, but as an equality between linear maps on real paravectors \(A\in\R\oplus\R^3\):
\begin{equation*}
G(A):=\operatorname{Ric}(A)-\tfrac12 A\,R
=8\pi G_N\,\mathcal T_{\mathrm m}(A).
\end{equation*}
Here \(\operatorname{Ric}(A)\) is the Ricci contraction of the curvature built from the local basis \(\{1,\sigv '(x)\}\), \(R\) is the associated scalar curvature, and \(\mathcal T_{\mathrm m}(A)\) is the matter stress-energy map. This says the same thing as the usual Einstein equation, but it packages the contractions through the scalar paravector metric, commutators, and linear maps instead of explicit tensor indices \cite{lasenby1998,doran2003}.

So the answer remains qualified yes, but in a form closer to the present manuscript: general relativity can be written largely without tensor indexing by extending the already-developed similarity transforms, moving-basis formulas, commutators, and scalar paravector metric to local frames. What cannot be avoided is the need for linear maps. Curvature and stress-energy are not captured by one ordinary product of a single paravector; they require the local transform field \(T(x)\), the connection elements \(\Omega_a\), and the induced curvature and source maps.

\subsection{Remaining Work}
The gravitational extension sketched above is not developed in the present paper. Every calculation proved here still assumes a fixed flat-space sigma basis, so curvature, covariant transport, and Einstein dynamics are only prospective at this stage. To turn that sketch into a full development, the interval, geodesic law, Maxwell system, and Dirac system would all have to be rewritten in local frame form, and the source discussion around Eq.\ \eqref{eq:energy-momentum-paravector} would have to be replaced by the full stress-energy map required by the Einstein equation. In short, the next gravitational step is not a small correction to the present notation; it is a local-frame version of the whole flat-space construction.

Several other developments are also still missing. The present document does not yet develop non-Abelian gauge structure, so the algebra has not been tested here against the internal symmetries of weak and strong interactions. It does not yet treat many-particle or entangled quantum states, where one needs more than a single paravector or a single \(2\times2\) auxiliary system. It also does not yet give a full account of constitutive laws for anisotropic media, dissipative systems, or continuum mechanics, where higher-rank linear response objects usually appear. On the mathematical side, operator domains, boundary terms, and regularity hypotheses still need to be stated more systematically whenever differential operators are factored or commuted.

Nothing in the present calculations shows a direct incompatibility between STA and the standard relativistic theories treated here. The limitations are instead ones of scope and expressiveness in the current presentation. A single paravector layer does not by itself encode arbitrary higher-rank multilinear structure, bundle data, or global topological patching. Those features may still be representable in STA, but they require additional constructions beyond what has been developed in this document.

\subsection{Advantages and Limits of Avoiding Tensors}
The main advantage of the present approach is clarity of geometric meaning. The same algebraic product carries scalar contraction, oriented cross-product structure, projector calculus, and transform laws, so many long index derivations collapse to a few lines without losing component information. That compression is not merely cosmetic. It is what makes it possible to place kinematics, electromagnetism, and relativistic wave equations inside one algebraic framework while still recovering the scalar and vector parts explicitly at each step.

The cost is that tensor content has not truly disappeared; it has been repackaged. In flat-space problems that repackaging is often a gain because it removes index bookkeeping and keeps the geometry visible. In curved-space problems the story is more mixed. Once one introduces local frames, connections, curvature, and source maps, the theory again has genuinely multilinear structure even if it is written in STA form. The practical drawback is therefore twofold: first, some problems are not naturally expressed by paravectors alone; second, most of the surrounding literature, software, and standard formulations remain tensor-based, so translation back and forth is unavoidable.

For that reason the tensor-free character of this formulation is best treated as a strong feature, not as a universal replacement principle. In the regime developed here, the algebra gives a compact and coherent reformulation with real conceptual advantages. Beyond that regime, especially in general relativity and in theories built on richer internal or multilinear structure, the more useful question is how much tensor content can be absorbed into the algebra without losing precision, generality, or contact with standard formulations.

\section{Scope Notes and Cautions}\label{sec:flags}
The list below marks formulas or claims that still require interpretation, correction, or stronger hypotheses than the surrounding exposition alone makes explicit.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \flag{The spin-axis commutator estimate in Section \ref{sec:projectors} should not be confused with the full Robertson or Robertson--Schr\"odinger state-variance inequality.}
\item \flag{Any diagonalized matrix form of $\dd s^2$ must retain explicit Lorentz-signature information; the Euclidean shorthand $\dd s^2=\dd\mathbf x'\cdot\dd\mathbf x'$ is not a Lorentzian identity.}
\item \flag{In quaternion intermediate expansions, the linear term carries the factor $a$, as in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:quaternion-square}.}
\item \flag{The line $T=\frac12F\rev{F}$ should be read as a frame-conditioned energy-density/Poynting paravector, not the full electromagnetic stress-energy tensor.}
\item \flag{Equation \eqref{eq:normalized-dirac} is an on-shell projector statement for normalized free states, not the general Dirac equation.}
\item \flag{An earlier sign variant of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-square} with $-iqF^*$ in the upper component is inconsistent with the operator ordering in $W(i\partial-q\conj{\Phi})$; the corrected sign is $+iqF^*$.}
\item \flag{Several operator equalities stated in differential form implicitly assume smooth test functions and benign boundary behavior; those hypotheses should be stated whenever commutators of differential operators are used rigorously.}
\end{enumerate}
