A Pauli paravector contains one complex scalar and one complex three-vector; its multiplication rule packages dot and cross products, the inverse divisor, the space-time interval, rotations, boosts, Maxwell's equations, and the Klein--Gordon and Dirac systems. Spin-axis projectors, ladder elements, and the quaternion rotation basis arise from the same product, and the leading Pauli equation produces the expected magnetic spin separation. Events, fields, and wave functions retain the different transformation laws required by their physical roles. The main construction is intrinsic to the paravector algebra; optional matrix applications are identified where they are used, and the standard two-by-two coordinate realization is isolated in Appendix A. No new physical law is proposed. A notation-light, self-contained reorganization is supplied so that connections usually divided among vector, indexed-array, and matrix descriptions remain visible.}

\section*{Introduction}\label{sec:orientation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}
\begingroup
\setlength{\abovedisplayskip}{5pt plus 1pt minus 1pt}
\setlength{\belowdisplayskip}{5pt plus 1pt minus 1pt}
\setlength{\abovedisplayshortskip}{3pt plus 1pt}
\setlength{\belowdisplayshortskip}{4pt plus 1pt minus 1pt}
Shared geometry is often hidden when space-time, electromagnetic fields, and
quantum states are assigned different mathematical languages. Their
rotations, boosts, intervals, and order-sensitive differential products are
placed here in one small algebra. Notation light enough for plain-text
transcription is retained, while scalar/vector and real/imaginary structure
remains visible.

Three generators \(\pv{\sigma}_n\) are used. A generator is an element from
which other elements are built by addition and multiplication. Each
generator squares to \(1\), while the product of two different generators
changes sign when its order is exchanged. Their full product
\(i=\pv{\sigma}_1\pv{\sigma}_2\pv{\sigma}_3\) commutes with them and satisfies
\(i^2=-1\). It is conventionally called a pseudoscalar
\cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}. Every algebra element is written uniquely as
\[
\pv{Z}=S+\vct{V}\cdot\sigv,
\qquad S\in\C,\quad \vct{V}\in\C^3.
\]
Elements of this form are called Pauli paravectors and are referred to below
simply as paravectors. Their product is supplied by the sigma-basis rule,
not componentwise multiplication. An optional one-to-one
product-preserving translation into two-by-two complex matrices is supplied
in Appendix \ref{sec:matrix-appendix}; it is conventionally called a faithful
representation \cite{hestenes1966,baylis1999,doran2003}. No intrinsic result
depends on that translation; optional matrix applications are identified
explicitly where they are used.

The product yields dot and cross products, while multiplication by the
adjugate supplies the determinant used in an inverse. For real events that
determinant is the space-time interval. Exponentials generate rotations and
boosts; one equation contains all four Maxwell equations; and ordered
first-order products produce the Dirac and leading Pauli equations
\cite{lorentz1904,maxwell1865,dirac1928,pauli1927}. Events are transformed by
a two-sided Hermitian law, fields by similarity, and one Dirac field by a
fixed algebraic projection. Spin-axis projectors, ladder elements, and the
magnetic separation of the two Pauli spin parts are obtained from the same
product. These distinctions and spin structures are derived rather than
suppressed.

Established flat-space physics is reorganized rather than altered. Here
flat means that coordinates exist in which the interval coefficients are
constant. Position-dependent coefficients produced only by a coordinate
relabeling do not change that geometry, and gravity is absent from the
model. The construction is grounded in the space-time-algebra and
paravector literature \cite{hestenes1966,baylis1999,doran2003}, with roots in
quaternions and geometric algebra \cite{hamilton1844,clifford1878}. Natural
units set the reduced Planck constant and the speed of light to \(1\).
Rationalized Heaviside--Lorentz units absorb the vacuum constants and factors
of \(4\pi\) \cite{jackson1998}. The signature \((+,-,-,-)\) gives
\(t^2-\vct{r}^{\,2}\). Ordinary three-vectors are bold and their components
are displayed in braces:
\begin{equation}
\vct{A}:=\vthree{A_1}{A_2}{A_3}.
\label{eq:three-vector-convention}
\end{equation}
Operator order and the derivative product rule are retained explicitly. The
notation \(O(x^n)\) collects terms of order \(x^n\) and beyond.
The prime mark is defined as a value label:
\begin{equation}
x' := \eqtext{a distinct value carrying the same declared type and role as }x.
\label{eq:prime-convention}
\end{equation}
When a transformation has been specified, the prime labels the transformed
value. In other uses it only distinguishes two values that share the same
meaning and algebraic properties. It never denotes a derivative; derivatives
are written explicitly with \(\partial\) or \(\dd\).

Wave-function roles are identified by upright text subscripts, as in
\(\psi_{\eqtext{KG}}\), \(\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_split}}(\pm)\), and
\(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}}\). Parentheses attached directly
to a wave function contain only actual inputs \(t\), \(\vct{r}\), and
\(+\) or \(-\); unneeded inputs are omitted.

The next page collects the principal formulas. Algebra, space-time,
transforms, differential calculus, electromagnetism, wave mechanics,
probability, and spin are developed in Sections
\ref{sec:algebra}--\ref{sec:qm}. Conclusions, open work, and symbolic checks
are separated in Sections \ref{sec:conclusions}--\ref{sec:verification}.
Quaternion relabeling accompanies rotations; Appendix
\ref{sec:matrix-appendix} isolates the two-way translation between
paravectors and ordinary two-by-two complex matrices.

\section{Algebraic Foundations}\label{sec:algebra}\label{sec:foundations}\label{sec:functions}
The algebra is fixed before physical meanings are assigned to particular
elements. Product grouping does not matter:
\((\pv{X}\pv{Y})\pv{Z}=\pv{X}(\pv{Y}\pv{Z})\). This property is called
associativity. Multiplication by \(1\) leaves every element unchanged. Every
complex coefficient used in the master product commutes with every algebra
element; such a coefficient is called central. Only later calculations
containing differential operators require the written order to be retained.
These standard algebra terms are used in precisely these senses
\cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}.

\subsection{Basis and pseudoscalar}
For \(n\in\{1,2,3\}\), every algebra element is built from real-number
multiples of products of the three basis elements \(\pv{\sigma}_n\).
Products containing zero, one, two, or three different generators provide
the four basis types. The product containing all three generators is called
the pseudoscalar, following the standard Pauli-algebra terminology
\cite{hestenes1966,baylis1999,doran2003}. Altogether, eight independent real
coefficients are required. The resulting algebra is commonly called the real
Pauli algebra or the three-dimensional real Clifford algebra.
The basis relations are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\pv{\sigma}_n^2&=1,\qquad \pv{\sigma}_n\pv{\sigma}_m=-\pv{\sigma}_m\pv{\sigma}_n\quad (n\neq m),\\
i&:=\pv{\sigma}_1\pv{\sigma}_2\pv{\sigma}_3,\qquad i^2=-1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:basis}
\end{equation}
The full three-generator product commutes with each generator,
\(i\pv{\sigma}_n=\pv{\sigma}_n i\), and its square is the scalar \(-1\):
\begin{align*}
(\pv{\sigma}_1\pv{\sigma}_2\pv{\sigma}_3)^2
&= \pv{\sigma}_1\pv{\sigma}_2(\pv{\sigma}_3\pv{\sigma}_1)\pv{\sigma}_2\pv{\sigma}_3,\notag\\
&= -\pv{\sigma}_1(\pv{\sigma}_2\pv{\sigma}_1)(\pv{\sigma}_3\pv{\sigma}_2)\pv{\sigma}_3,\notag\\
&= -\pv{\sigma}_1^2\pv{\sigma}_2^2\pv{\sigma}_3^2,\notag\\
&= -1.
\end{align*}
With the Kronecker delta and Levi-Civita symbol
\cite{hestenes1966,doran2003},
\begin{equation}
\pv{\sigma}_n\pv{\sigma}_m=\delta_{nm}+\sum_{k=1}^3\epsilon_{nmk}\,i\pv{\sigma}_k,
\label{eq:sigma-product}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align*}
\delta_{nm} &:= \begin{cases}
1,& n=m,\\
0,& n\neq m,
\end{cases}\\[0.25em]
\epsilon_{nmk} &:= \begin{cases}
+1,& (n,m,k)\text{ even permutation of }(1,2,3),\\
-1,& (n,m,k)\text{ odd permutation of }(1,2,3),\\
0,& \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{align*}
The cyclic orderings \((1,2,3)\), \((2,3,1)\), and \((3,1,2)\) are the even
permutations. Exchanging any two entries gives an odd permutation. The
display is therefore also a complete definition of the signs used for
\(\epsilon_{nmk}\).

\subsection{The Conjugate and Hermitian Operators}
The sigma-conjugate operator is defined by
\begin{equation}
(\cdot)^* := \eqtext{flip the signs of } \pv{\sigma}_n.
\label{eq:star-definition}
\end{equation}
The Hermitian operator is defined by
\begin{equation}
(\cdot)^{\Hop} := \eqtext{reverse the order of multiplied } \pv{\sigma}_n.
\label{eq:hermitian-definition}
\end{equation}
Within the present algebra, the Hermitian operation is defined solely by
reversing sigma-product order. Appendix \ref{sec:matrix-appendix} shows that
this intrinsic operation becomes the familiar Hermitian operation after the
optional matrix translation is made. Both intrinsic operations distribute
over addition and multiplication by real
numbers. Star preserves product order, whereas \(\Hop\) reverses it. Because
both send \(i\) to \(-i\), each also conjugates a complex coefficient:
applying either operation to \(c\pv{Z}\) produces \(c^*\) times the operated
element. This property is conventionally called conjugate linearity. Their
combined operation preserves complex coefficients and reverses product order
\cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}. These statements are expressed directly by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
{(\pv{X}\pv{Y})}^{*}&=\pv{X}^{*}\pv{Y}^{*},\qquad
{(\pv{X}\pv{Y})}^{\Hop}=\pv{Y}^{\Hop}\pv{X}^{\Hop},\\
\pv{X}^{*\Hop}&=(\pv{X}^{\Hop})^{*}=(\pv{X}^{*})^{\Hop},\qquad
(\pv{X}^*)^*=(\pv{X}^{\Hop})^{\Hop}=\pv{X}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:operator-rules}
\end{equation}
When applied to the generators and the pseudoscalar, these rules are reduced to
\begin{alignat}{3}
\conj{\pv{\sigma}_n} &{}= -\pv{\sigma}_n, \qquad&
\herm{\pv{\sigma}_n} &{}= \pv{\sigma}_n, \qquad&
\cherm{\pv{\sigma}_n} &{}= -\pv{\sigma}_n,
\label{eq:sigma-operator-signs}\\
\conj{i} &{}= -i, \qquad&
\herm{i} &{}= -i, \qquad&
\cherm{i} &{}= i.
\label{eq:i-operator-signs}
\end{alignat}
For a product of two different generators, with \(n\neq m\),
\begin{align}
(\pv{\sigma}_n\pv{\sigma}_m)^*
&= \pv{\sigma}_n^*\pv{\sigma}_m^*
= (-\pv{\sigma}_n)(-\pv{\sigma}_m)
= \pv{\sigma}_n\pv{\sigma}_m,\notag\\
(\pv{\sigma}_n\pv{\sigma}_m)^{\Hop}
&= \pv{\sigma}_m^{\Hop}\pv{\sigma}_n^{\Hop}
= \pv{\sigma}_m\pv{\sigma}_n
= -\pv{\sigma}_n\pv{\sigma}_m,\notag\\
(\pv{\sigma}_n\pv{\sigma}_m)^{*\Hop}
&= (\pv{\sigma}_m\pv{\sigma}_n)^*
= \pv{\sigma}_m^*\pv{\sigma}_n^* \notag\\
&= (-\pv{\sigma}_m)(-\pv{\sigma}_n)
= \pv{\sigma}_m\pv{\sigma}_n
= -\pv{\sigma}_n\pv{\sigma}_m.
\label{eq:bivector-operator-signs}
\end{align}
The same sign pattern on the sigma-number-two elements is recorded in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:bivector-operator-signs}. Each basic operation returns the
original element when applied twice, as shown in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:operator-rules};
an operation with this property is called an involution. The word
``Hermitian'' names the operation; it does not assert that every operated
element is unchanged. An element is self-Hermitian precisely when
\(\pv{Z}^{\Hop}=\pv{Z}\). When this algebra is compared
with products containing an even number of basis vectors in the usual
space-time algebra, reversal of a
space-time product corresponds to the combined operation
\((\cdot)^{*\Hop}\), not to \((\cdot)^{\Hop}\) alone
\cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}. This comparison introduces no additional rule.

The sigma number of a reduced basis product is defined as the number of sigma
generators that remain after repeated use of
\(\pv{\sigma}_n^2=1\) and
\(\pv{\sigma}_n\pv{\sigma}_m=-\pv{\sigma}_m\pv{\sigma}_n\).
Sigma numbers \(0\), \(1\), \(2\), and \(3\) are assigned respectively to
\(1\), \(\pv{\sigma}_n\),
\(\pv{\sigma}_n\pv{\sigma}_m\) with \(n\neq m\), and \(i\). A product not
yet simplified by these rules is called unreduced. Every such product is
reduced to one of the four cases. The basis elements and operator signs are
summarized by sigma number in Tables \ref{tab:sigma-number-components} and
\ref{tab:sigma-number-signs}.

\subsection{Generic Complex Paravector}
The three generators are collected into the sigma-vector
\begin{equation}
\sigv := \vthree{\pv{\sigma}_1}{\pv{\sigma}_2}{\pv{\sigma}_3},
\label{eq:sigma-vector}
\end{equation}
which is a vector of algebraic objects rather than a vector of scalar
components. The associated three products of two different generators are
obtained by multiplication by the pseudoscalar:
\begin{equation}
i\sigv := \vthree{i\pv{\sigma}_1}{i\pv{\sigma}_2}{i\pv{\sigma}_3}
= \vthree{\pv{\sigma}_2\pv{\sigma}_3}
{\pv{\sigma}_3\pv{\sigma}_1}
{\pv{\sigma}_1\pv{\sigma}_2},
\label{eq:isigma-vector}
\end{equation}
because \(i=\pv{\sigma}_1\pv{\sigma}_2\pv{\sigma}_3\). For each \(n\), the
two indices other than \(n\) remain in the displayed order after reduction.
Equation \eqref{eq:isigma-vector} therefore fixes the chosen ordered basis
for the sigma-number-two products.
The real and complex Pauli-paravector spaces are defined directly by the
allowed scalar and vector coefficients. The unqualified symbol
\(\Pspace\) denotes whichever of the two spaces is applicable when the
coefficient type is immaterial or already fixed by context:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\RPspace
&:=\left\{a+\vct{A}\cdot\sigv\ \middle|\;
a\in\R,\ \vct{A}\in\R^3\right\},\\
\CPspace
&:=\left\{S+\vct{V}\cdot\sigv\ \middle|\;
S\in\C,\ \vct{V}\in\C^3\right\},\\
\Pspace
&:=\eqtext{either }\RPspace\eqtext{ or }\CPspace
\eqtext{, as fixed by context}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:paravector-spaces}
\end{equation}
Thus every member of \(\RPspace\) is constructed from one real scalar and
one real three-vector. Every member of \(\CPspace\) is constructed in the
same way with a complex scalar and a complex three-vector. The
\(\R\) or \(\C\) subscript identifies the coefficient type. An element with
a real or zero scalar and a complex vector is therefore still in
\(\CPspace\). Every
member of \(\RPspace\) is also a member of \(\CPspace\). A product of two
members of \(\RPspace\) can nevertheless
leave that set because of the imaginary cross-product term in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dot-cross}. In standard algebra language,
\(\RPspace\) is therefore not closed under the product, whereas
\(\CPspace\) is. Equivalently,
\(\pv{X}\in\RPspace\) exactly when \(\herm{\pv{X}}=\pv{X}\).

With this basis fixed, for real scalars \(a,b\in\R\) and real vectors
\(\vct{A},\vct{B}\in\R^3\), with
\(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv=A_1\pv{\sigma}_1+A_2\pv{\sigma}_2+A_3\pv{\sigma}_3\),
the real paravectors \(\pv{X},\pv{Y}\in\RPspace\) are defined by
\begin{align}
\pv{X}&:=a+\vct{A}\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:real-paravector-x}\\
\pv{Y}&:=b+\vct{B}\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:real-paravector-y}
\end{align}
The complex scalar and vector components are defined by
\begin{align}
S&:=a+ib\in\C,
\label{eq:paravector-scalar}\\
\vct{V}&:=\vct{A}+i\vct{B}\in\C^3.
\label{eq:paravector-vector}
\end{align}
Every algebra element can then be defined as a complex paravector
\(\pv{Z}\in\CPspace\), written as a real paravector plus an imaginary
paravector:
\begin{equation}
\pv{Z}:=\pv{X}+i\pv{Y}.
\label{eq:paravector-xy}
\end{equation}
The equivalent real-component and scalar--vector forms are
\begin{align}
\pv{Z}&=(a+ib)+(\vct{A}+i\vct{B})\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:paravector-cartesian}\\
\pv{Z}&=S+\vct{V}\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:paravector}
\end{align}
On coefficients alone, the actions inherited from either basic operation are
\(
S^*=S^{\Hop}=a-ib
\)
and
\(
\vct{V}^*=\vct{V}^{\Hop}=\vct{A}-i\vct{B}
\).
When either operation acts on the full algebra term
\(\vct{V}\cdot\sigv\), its action on the sigma basis is included as well,
according to Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:operator-rules} and
\eqref{eq:sigma-operator-signs}.
The real function is defined by
\begin{equation}
\eqfunc{real}(\pv{Z}):=\pv{X}=\frac{\pv{Z}+\herm{\pv{Z}}}{2}.
\label{eq:real-function}
\end{equation}
The imaginary function is defined by
\begin{equation}
\eqfunc{img}(\pv{Z}):=\pv{Y}=\frac{\pv{Z}-\herm{\pv{Z}}}{2i}.
\label{eq:img-function}
\end{equation}
The scalar function is defined by
\begin{equation}
\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{Z}):=S=\frac{\pv{Z}+\cherm{\pv{Z}}}{2}.
\label{eq:scalar-function}
\end{equation}
The vector function is defined by
\begin{equation}
\eqfunc{vector}(\pv{Z}):=\vct{V}.
\label{eq:vector-function}
\end{equation}
The algebra element carried by that vector is recovered as
\begin{equation}
\eqfunc{vector}(\pv{Z})\cdot\sigv
=\frac{\pv{Z}-\cherm{\pv{Z}}}{2}.
\label{eq:vector-recovery}
\end{equation}
The zero three-vector and the two paravector identity elements can now be
defined without introducing another kind of object:
\begin{equation}
\vct{0}:=\vthree{0}{0}{0},
\qquad
\pv{0}:=0+\vct{0}\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
\pv{1}:=1+\vct{0}\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:paravector-identities}
\end{equation}
Their scalar and vector parts are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{0})&=0,
&
\eqfunc{vector}(\pv{0})&=\vct{0},\\
\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{1})&=1,
&
\eqfunc{vector}(\pv{1})&=\vct{0}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:identity-components}
\end{equation}
Their defining actions on every paravector \(\pv{Z}\) are
\begin{equation}
\pv{Z}+\pv{0}=\pv{0}+\pv{Z}=\pv{Z},
\qquad
\pv{Z}\pv{1}=\pv{1}\pv{Z}=\pv{Z}.
\label{eq:algebra-identities}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\pv{0}\) is the additive identity and \(\pv{1}\) is the
multiplicative identity. An inverse, when it exists, is defined by a product
equal to \(\pv{1}\).
The actions of the conjugate and Hermitian operators on the generic complex paravector are
\begin{align}
\conj{\pv{Z}} &= (a-ib)+(-\vct{A}+i\vct{B})\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:paravector-conjugate}\\
\herm{\pv{Z}} &= \pv{X}-i\pv{Y}=(a-ib)+(\vct{A}-i\vct{B})\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:paravector-reverse}\\
\cherm{\pv{Z}} &= (a+ib)-(\vct{A}+i\vct{B})\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:paravector-reverse-conjugate}
\end{align}
For the real paravector \(\pv{X}\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x}, these are reduced to
\begin{align}
\conj{\pv{X}} &= a-\vct{A}\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:real-paravector-conjugate}\\
\herm{\pv{X}} &= \pv{X}=a+\vct{A}\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:real-paravector-reverse}\\
\cherm{\pv{X}} &= a-\vct{A}\cdot\sigv=\conj{\pv{X}}.
\label{eq:real-paravector-combined}
\end{align}
Equations \eqref{eq:paravector-conjugate}--\eqref{eq:paravector-reverse-conjugate}
are the generic formulas used below. In particular, conjugation
of the complex coefficients is already encoded by \(\Hop\); no overbar
or additional intrinsic operation is needed. Equations
\eqref{eq:real-paravector-conjugate}--\eqref{eq:real-paravector-combined}
are the real specialization.

fixed by Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x}--\eqref{eq:vector-function}. The

\subsection{Dot products and cross products}
For real vectors \(\vct{A},\vct{B}\in\R^3\), the dot product is defined by
\begin{align}
\vct{A}\cdot\vct{B}
&:= \sum_{n=1}^3\sum_{m=1}^3\delta_{nm}A_nB_m \notag\\
&= A_1B_1+A_2B_2+A_3B_3.
\label{eq:vector-dot-norm}
\end{align}
The square of a vector is defined by
\begin{equation}
\vct{A}^2:=\vct{A}\cdot\vct{A}.
\label{eq:vector-square}
\end{equation}
\noindent For the cross product, the Levi-Civita form is
\begin{equation}
(\vct{A}\times\vct{B})_k
\,:= \sum_{n=1}^3\sum_{m=1}^3\epsilon_{nmk}A_nB_m,
\label{eq:cross-product-definition}
\end{equation}
from which the vector is obtained:
\begin{equation}
\vct{A}\times\vct{B}=
\vthreeeq
{A_2B_3-A_3B_2}
{A_3B_1-A_1B_3}
{A_1B_2-A_2B_1}.
\label{eq:cross-product}
\end{equation}
Dot products are commutative, whereas cross products are antisymmetric:
\begin{align*}
\vct{A}\cdot\vct{B} &= +\vct{B}\cdot\vct{A},\\
\vct{A}\times\vct{B} &= -\vct{B}\times\vct{A}.
\end{align*}
For a fixed unit vector \(\vct{u}\), where \(\vct{u}^2=1\), the parallel
component function is defined by
\begin{equation}
\eqfunc{par}(\vct{A}):=(\vct{A}\cdot\vct{u})\vct{u}.
\label{eq:par-function}
\end{equation}
The perpendicular component function is defined by
\begin{equation}
\eqfunc{prp}(\vct{A}):=\vct{A}-\eqfunc{par}(\vct{A}).
\label{eq:prp-function}
\end{equation}
With Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:basis} and \eqref{eq:sigma-product},
\begin{align*}
(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)(\vct{B}\cdot\sigv)
&= \sum_{n=1}^3\sum_{m=1}^3 A_n B_m \pv{\sigma}_n \pv{\sigma}_m \notag\\
&= \sum_{n=1}^3 A_n B_n \notag\\
&\quad + \sum_{n<m}(A_n B_m-A_m B_n)\pv{\sigma}_n \pv{\sigma}_m \notag\\
&= A_1B_1+A_2B_2+A_3B_3 \notag\\
&\quad + (A_2B_3-A_3B_2)i\pv{\sigma}_1 \notag\\
&\quad - (A_1B_3-A_3B_1)i\pv{\sigma}_2 \notag\\
&\quad + (A_1B_2-A_2B_1)i\pv{\sigma}_3.
\end{align*}
This can be efficiently expressed in terms of the dot product and cross product as
\begin{equation}
(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)(\vct{B}\cdot\sigv)=\vct{A}\cdot\vct{B}+i(\vct{A}\times\vct{B})\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:dot-cross}
\end{equation}
The same product is recorded in component form in Table
\ref{tab:components-vector-product}. For vectors in \(\C^3\), the dot and
cross products are extended linearly in each input, without complex
conjugation. Formula \eqref{eq:dot-cross} may also be used for commuting
scalar-valued functions. A separate ordered calculation is required when a
coefficient contains a derivative, because the ordinary derivative product
rule acts on everything to its right.

Only the antisymmetric term is flipped by the reversed order:
\begin{align*}
(\vct{B}\cdot\sigv)(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)
&= \vct{A}\cdot\vct{B}-i(\vct{A}\times\vct{B})\cdot\sigv,\\
(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)(\vct{B}\cdot\sigv)+(\vct{B}\cdot\sigv)(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)
&= 2\,\vct{A}\cdot\vct{B},\\
(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)(\vct{B}\cdot\sigv)-(\vct{B}\cdot\sigv)(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)
&= 2i(\vct{A}\times\vct{B})\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
The following identities are obtained immediately from the same product:
\begin{align*}
(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)^2 &= \vct{A}^2,\\
(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)^{2n} &= (\vct{A}^2)^n,\\
(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)^{2n+1} &= (\vct{A}^2)^n(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv),\\
(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)\sigv &= \vct{A}-i\,\vct{A}\times\sigv.
\end{align*}
These are the basic sigma-product identities from which the later paravector formulas are built.

\subsection{Products}
With the scalar/vector split for real vectors fixed by Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dot-cross}, the fully complex product is obtained as the master formula from which the simpler cases are recovered by restriction.

For \(\pv{Z}\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:paravector} and \(\pv{Z}'=S'+\vct{V}'\cdot\sigv\) defined analogously to Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:paravector} and \eqref{eq:paravector-vector},
\begin{equation}
\pv{Z}\pv{Z}'=(SS'+\vct{V}\cdot\vct{V}')+\big(S\vct{V}'+S'\vct{V}+i(\vct{V}\times\vct{V}')\big)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:paravector-product}
\end{equation}
The generic complex paravector and its master multiplication rule are fixed by Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:paravector} and \eqref{eq:paravector-product}: pure scalar, real vector, real paravector, and mixed products are all obtained by setting the unused parts to zero.

\subsection{Positive Norm, Adjugate, and Determinant}
Two distinct expressions formed from two copies of a paravector are used: a
positive coefficient norm and a generally complex determinant.
For a complex scalar \(S=a+ib\in\C\), the scalar norm is defined by
\begin{equation}
|S|:=\sqrt{SS^*}.
\label{eq:scalar-norm}
\end{equation}
Its squared value is
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
|S|^2&=SS^*\\
&=a^2+b^2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
If \(S\in\R\), then the ordinary absolute value \(|S|=\sqrt{S^2}\) is obtained from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:scalar-norm}.
For a complex vector \(\vct{V}=\vct{A}+i\vct{B}\in\C^3\), the vector norm is defined by
\begin{equation}
\|\vct{V}\|:=\sqrt{\vct{V}\cdot\vct{V}^*}.
\label{eq:vector-norm}
\end{equation}
The corresponding vector-norm square is
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\|\vct{V}\|^2&=\vct{V}\cdot\vct{V}^*\\
&=\vct{A}^2+\vct{B}^2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The quadratic product \(\vct{V}^2=\vct{V}\cdot\vct{V}\) is not the same object as the positive norm square \(\vct{V}\cdot\vct{V}^*\).
For the complex paravector \(\pv{Z}\) from Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:paravector}--\eqref{eq:paravector-cartesian}, the paravector norm is defined by
\begin{equation}
\|\pv{Z}\|:=\sqrt{|S|^2+\|\vct{V}\|^2}.
\label{eq:euclidean-form}
\end{equation}
Its square is reduced directly to the coefficient norm:
\begin{align}
\|\pv{Z}\|^2
&= |S|^2+\|\vct{V}\|^2\notag\\
&= a^2+b^2+\vct{A}^2+\vct{B}^2\notag\\
&= \eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{Z}\herm{\pv{Z}})
= \eqfunc{scalar}(\herm{\pv{Z}}\pv{Z}).
\label{eq:euclidean-norm-expansion}
\end{align}
This is a nonnegative real norm: it is the sum of the squared magnitudes of the scalar and vector coefficients.

The determinant is obtained through construction of the multiplicative inverse. For
\(\pv{Z}=S+\vct{V}\cdot\sigv\), the adjugate is obtained by reversing the sign of the vector part:
\begin{equation}
\adjf{\pv{Z}}
:=\cherm{\pv{Z}}
=S-\vct{V}\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:adjugate}
\end{equation}
Because the two basic operations commute and each returns the original
element when applied twice, the following useful consistency chain is also
obtained from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:adjugate}:
\begin{equation}
\herm{\pv{Z}}
=\conj{\bigl(\cherm{\pv{Z}}\bigr)}
=\cherm{\bigl(\conj{\pv{Z}}\bigr)}
=\conj{\adjf{\pv{Z}}}
=\adjf{\conj{\pv{Z}}}.
\label{eq:hermitian-adjugate-identity}
\end{equation}
In the compact superscript notation, this is
\(\pv{Z}^{\Hop}=(\pv{Z}^{*\Hop})^*
=(\pv{Z}^*)^{*\Hop}
=\adjf{\pv{Z}}^*=\adjf{\pv{Z}^*}\).
Because \(S\) is central and
\((\vct{V}\cdot\sigv)^2=\vct{V}^2\), the vector terms are canceled by multiplication by the adjugate:
\begin{align}
\pv{Z}\adjf{\pv{Z}}
&=(S+\vct{V}\cdot\sigv)(S-\vct{V}\cdot\sigv)\notag\\
&=S^2-\vct{V}^2\notag\\
&=\adjf{\pv{Z}}\pv{Z}.
\label{eq:adjugate-product}
\end{align}
The remaining central scalar is exactly the divisor required to normalize the product to the multiplicative identity \(1\). The determinant function is therefore defined by
\begin{equation}
\detf{\pv{Z}}:=\adjf{\pv{Z}}\pv{Z}.
\label{eq:determinant-function}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:adjugate-product} shows that the order of the two factors
does not matter. The determinant is reduced to
\begin{equation}
\detf{\pv{Z}}=\pv{Z}\adjf{\pv{Z}}=S^2-\vct{V}^2.
\label{eq:minkowski-form}
\end{equation}
In real and imaginary components, the same result is
\begin{equation}
\detf{\pv{Z}}
=(a^2-b^2-\vct{A}^2+\vct{B}^2)
+2i(ab-\vct{A}\cdot\vct{B}).
\label{eq:determinant-components}
\end{equation}
The name determinant is assigned because this central scalar is exactly the
divisor required by the inverse in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:paravector-inverse}. Its
agreement with the ordinary two-by-two determinant is verified independently
in Appendix \ref{sec:matrix-appendix}. No square root is included in the
definition.

For the real paravector \(\pv{X}\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x}, \(\cherm{\pv{X}}=\conj{\pv{X}}\), and the determinant is restricted to the Minkowski quadratic form
\begin{align}
\detf{\pv{X}}
&= a^2-\vct{A}^2\notag\\
&= \pv{X}\conj{\pv{X}}
=\conj{\pv{X}}\pv{X}.
\label{eq:real-paravector-metric}
\end{align}
Product order is reversed by the adjugate, so for complex paravectors \(\pv{Z}\) and \(\pv{Z}'\),
\begin{align}
\adjf{\adjf{\pv{Z}}}&=\pv{Z},\notag\\
\adjf{\pv{Z}\pv{Z}'}
&=\adjf{\pv{Z}'}\adjf{\pv{Z}},\notag\\
\detf{\adjf{\pv{Z}}}&=\detf{\pv{Z}}.
\label{eq:adjugate-identities}
\end{align}
Consequently, the determinant of a product is the product of the two
determinants, even when the two paravectors do not commute:
\begin{align}
\detf{\pv{Z}\pv{Z}'}
&=\pv{Z}\pv{Z}'\adjf{\pv{Z}'}\adjf{\pv{Z}}\notag\\
&=\pv{Z}\detf{\pv{Z}'}\adjf{\pv{Z}}\notag\\
&=\detf{\pv{Z}'}\detf{\pv{Z}}\notag\\
&=\detf{\pv{Z}}\detf{\pv{Z}'}
=\detf{\pv{Z}'\pv{Z}}.
\label{eq:metric-product}
\end{align}
For a real \(\theta\), the factor \(\exp(i\theta)\) has magnitude one and is
called a phase factor. Under multiplication by this factor, the positive norm
and determinant are transformed as
\begin{align*}
\|\exp(i\theta)\pv{Z}\|^2 &= \|\pv{Z}\|^2,\\
\detf{\exp(i\theta)\pv{Z}}
&= \exp(2i\theta)\detf{\pv{Z}}.
\end{align*}
More generally, under complex scalar multiplication by \(r\exp(i\theta)\) with \(r\in\R\), the two quadratic constructions are scaled differently:
\begin{align}
\|r\exp(i\theta)\pv{Z}\|^2 &= r^2\|\pv{Z}\|^2,
\label{eq:norm-scaling}\\
\detf{r\exp(i\theta)\pv{Z}}
&= r^2\exp(2i\theta)\detf{\pv{Z}}.
\label{eq:metric-scaling}
\end{align}
The positive norm is unchanged by a phase factor, while the determinant
retains that phase factor squared. The term ``Minkowski metric'' below is reserved for the
real-paravector expression in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-metric}
\cite{minkowski1908}.

\subsection{Inverse formulas}
The inverse construction is completed by Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:adjugate-product} and \eqref{eq:minkowski-form}. For \(\pv{Z}=S+\vct{V}\cdot\sigv\), when \(\detf{\pv{Z}}\neq0\), the inverse is defined by
\begin{equation}
\pv{Z}^{-1}:=\frac{\adjf{\pv{Z}}}{\detf{\pv{Z}}}.
\label{eq:paravector-inverse}
\end{equation}
The equivalent explicit forms are
\begin{equation}
\pv{Z}^{-1}
=\frac{\cherm{\pv{Z}}}{\pv{Z}\cherm{\pv{Z}}}
=\frac{\cherm{\pv{Z}}}{\cherm{\pv{Z}}\pv{Z}}
=\frac{S-\vct{V}\cdot\sigv}{S^2-\vct{V}^2}.
\label{eq:paravector-inverse-explicit}
\end{equation}
If \(\detf{\pv{Z}}=0\), the inverse does not exist.

By differentiation of the identity \(\pv{Z}^{-1}\pv{Z}=1\), the following result is obtained:
\begin{align}
\dd(\pv{Z}^{-1}) &= -\pv{Z}^{-1}(\dd \pv{Z})\pv{Z}^{-1}.
\label{eq:paravector-inverse-diff}
\end{align}
For \(\pv{Z}'=S'+\vct{V}'\cdot\sigv\), if \(\vct{V}\times\vct{V}'=\vct{0}\), then
\begin{equation}
\pv{Z}^{-1}\pv{Z}'=\pv{Z}'\pv{Z}^{-1}.
\label{eq:inverse-commuting}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Projector Algebra}\label{sec:projectors}
A projector is an element that is unchanged when squared. Projectors are
introduced next so that the later function formulas and the elements that
exchange their two parts can be organized. Once Eqs.\
\eqref{eq:sigma-product} and \eqref{eq:dot-cross} are fixed, simple
unit-direction elements can be used to split a paravector into two parts.

An algebraic unit direction is any \(\vct{u}\in\C^3\) satisfying
\(\vct{u}^2=1\). For a real physical axis obtained from a nonzero
\(\vct{A}\in\R^3\), the direction is defined before the projector by
\begin{equation}
\vct{u}:=\frac{\vct{A}}{\|\vct{A}\|},
\qquad
\vct{u}^2=1.
\label{eq:real-unit-axis}
\end{equation}
The projector function for the chosen direction is then defined by
\begin{equation}
\pv{\Pi}(\pm):=\frac{1}{2}(1\pm \vct{u}\cdot\sigv).
\label{eq:projectors}
\end{equation}
The corresponding left-projected part of any algebra element \(\pv{Z}\) is
defined by
\begin{equation}
\pv{Z}(\pm):=\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\pv{Z}.
\label{eq:projected-part-function}
\end{equation}
The identities \(\pv{\Pi}(\pm)^2=\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\),
\(\pv{\Pi}(+)\pv{\Pi}(-)=0\), \(\pv{\Pi}(+)+\pv{\Pi}(-)=1\),
\(\pv{Z}=\pv{Z}(+)+\pv{Z}(-)\), and
\((\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)\pv{\Pi}(\pm)=\pm\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\) are satisfied.
Thus each projector is unchanged when squared, the two projectors annihilate
one another, and their sum is the identity. These properties are
conventionally called idempotence, orthogonality, and completeness,
respectively \cite{sakurai2017}.
Algebraically, the parts aligned and anti-aligned with the direction \(\vct{u}\) are isolated by \(\pv{\Pi}(+)\) and \(\pv{\Pi}(-)\). Their role is purely structural: an object is split into two parts adapted to a chosen axis.

For a scalar-valued function \(f:\{+1,-1\}\to\C\),
\begin{equation}
f(\vct{u}\cdot\sigv):=f(+1)\pv{\Pi}(+)+f(-1)\pv{\Pi}(-).
\label{eq:functional-calculus}
\end{equation}
A compact projector-based formula for roots, inverses, exponentials,
logarithms, and related functions is thereby obtained. If an element satisfies
a polynomial equation, that equation can be used to replace its higher powers
by lower powers. The lowest-degree such equation is called its minimal
polynomial. When its two scalar roots are distinct, the function is obtained
by evaluating it at those roots. When the roots coincide, the limiting formula
also contains the derivative of the function. These statements are derived
explicitly below rather than assumed.

For the ladder construction, the chosen direction is specialized to the
third axis:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\vct{u}&:=\vct{e}_3=\vthree{0}{0}{1},
\qquad
\vct{u}^2=1,
\\
\vct{u}\cdot\sigv&=\pv{\sigma}_3,
\qquad
\pv{\Pi}(\pm)
=\frac12(1\pm\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)
=\frac12(1\pm\pv{\sigma}_3).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ladder-axis}
\end{equation}
The ladder elements about this axis are defined by
\begin{equation}
\pv{a}(\pm):=\frac{1}{2}(\pv{\sigma}_1\pm i\pv{\sigma}_2).
\label{eq:ladder}
\end{equation}
The associated ordered-difference, zero-square, and product identities are
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\com{\pv{\sigma}_3}{\pv{a}(\pm)}=\pm 2\pv{a}(\pm),\qquad
\pv{a}(\pm)^2=0,\\
\pv{a}(+)\pv{a}(-)=\pv{\Pi}(+),\qquad
\pv{a}(-)\pv{a}(+)=\pv{\Pi}(-).
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:ladder-properties}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:ladder-properties} shows that each ladder element has
square zero; such an element is conventionally called nilpotent. These
combinations of \(\pv{\sigma}_1\) and \(\pv{\sigma}_2\) exchange the two
projector parts selected by Eq.\ \eqref{eq:ladder-axis}. This third-axis
specialization applies only to the ladder identities; the supporting
function formulas are restored to an arbitrary chosen unit direction. The corresponding
projector values, mixed products, and ladder identities built from Eqs.\
\eqref{eq:projectors}, \eqref{eq:functional-calculus}, and
\eqref{eq:ladder} are collected next.

\subsection{Generalized Transform Algebra}\label{sec:transform-algebra}
With the projector and exponential formulas fixed, the remaining purely
mathematical transform material can be collected in one place. The general
law is given first, followed by basis changes and then by the rotation and
boost elements built from the earlier exponentials:

\begin{align}
\pv{T}\pv{Z}'&=\pv{Z}\pv{T},
\label{eq:transform-intertwining}\\
\pv{Z}'&=\pv{T}^{-1}\pv{Z}\pv{T}.
\label{eq:general-transform}
\end{align}
Here \(\pv{Z}\) is a generic complex paravector and \(\pv{T}\) is an algebra
element with an inverse. Equation \eqref{eq:transform-intertwining} requires
that applying the transformed element and then \(\pv{T}\) give the same result
as applying \(\pv{T}\) and then the original element. This compatibility
condition is conventionally called an intertwining relation. Multiplication
by \(\pv{T}^{-1}\) gives the unique result
\eqref{eq:general-transform}. A transform of the form
\(\pv{T}^{-1}\pv{Z}\pv{T}\) is called a similarity transform. It is used below
for basis changes, rotations, and electromagnetic fields
\cite{hestenes1966,baylis1999,doran2003}. A different two-sided law is needed
for a real event paravector; its two factors are related by \(\Hop\), and
that law is derived and named when it is introduced.

\section{Space-Time Kinematics}\label{sec:spacetime}
The relations describing motion before forces are introduced are
conventionally called kinematics. On real event paravectors, the squared
space-time interval is obtained from the determinant, and a four-velocity is
represented by a real paravector with determinant \(1\). Standard
special-relativistic kinematics is thereby expressed in paravector form
\cite{lorentz1904,einstein1905,minkowski1908,hestenes1966,baylis1999}.

\subsection{Differential space-time and four-velocity}
The differential event is obtained by specializing the real paravector
\(\pv{X}\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x}:
\begin{equation}
\dd\pv{X}:=\dd t+\dd\vct{r}\cdot\sigv
\label{eq:dX}
\end{equation}
The interval is the differential version of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:minkowski-form}:
\begin{equation}
\dd s^2=\detf{\dd\pv{X}}
=\dd\pv{X}\,\conj{\dd\pv{X}}
=\dd t^2-\dd\vct{r}^2.
\label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
Here \(t,s,\gamma\in\R\), \(\vct{r},\vct{v},\vct{P}\in\R^3\), and
\(\pv{X},\pv{U}\) are real paravectors. Equation \eqref{eq:interval} is the
real-event restriction of the determinant in Eq.\
\eqref{eq:minkowski-form}. A worldline is the path of an object through
space-time. It is called timelike here when
\(\detf{\dd\pv{X}}>0\), and future-directed when \(\dd t>0\). Along such a
path, the positive increment
\(\dd s=\sqrt{\dd t^2-\dd\vct{r}^2}>0\) is the time measured by a clock
moving with the object and is conventionally called proper time
\cite{einstein1905,minkowski1908}.

The coordinate velocity and Lorentz factor are defined by
\(\vct{v}:=\dd\vct{r}/\dd t\in\R^3\) and
\(\gamma:=(1-\vct{v}^2)^{-1/2}\in\R\). The four-velocity is defined by
\begin{equation}
\pv{U}:=\frac{\dd\pv{X}}{\dd s}
=\gamma(1+\vct{v}\cdot\sigv).
\label{eq:proper-velocity}
\end{equation}
Its normalization is
\begin{equation}
\detf{\pv{U}}=1.
\label{eq:proper-velocity-normalization}
\end{equation}
The energy and spatial momentum of a particle with real rest mass \(m>0\)
are defined by
\begin{align}
E&:=m\gamma,
\label{eq:particle-energy}\\
\vct{P}&:=m\gamma\vct{v}.
\label{eq:particle-momentum}
\end{align}
The four-momentum is therefore
\begin{equation}
m\pv{U}=E+\vct{P}\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:proper-momentum}
\end{equation}
from which $E^2-\vct{P}^2=m^2$ is obtained immediately.
The normalized tangent \(\pv{U}\) is conventionally called the
four-velocity; only its spatial part \(\gamma\vct{v}\) is often denoted by
``proper velocity.'' The four-momentum of a massive particle is obtained by
multiplication by \(m>0\). A paravector with zero determinant is
conventionally called null. For a massless particle \(\dd s=0\), so
\(\pv{U}\) is undefined and a null momentum paravector must instead be
introduced directly.

\subsection{Four-acceleration and free motion}
The four-acceleration is the real paravector
\begin{equation}
\pv{A}:=\frac{\dd \pv{U}}{\dd s}
=\frac{\dd^2 \pv{X}}{\dd s^2}.
\label{eq:proper-acceleration}
\end{equation}
It obeys
\begin{equation}
\eqfunc{scalar}(\conj{\pv{U}}\pv{A})=0.
\label{eq:proper-acceleration-orthogonality}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:proper-acceleration-orthogonality} states that the scalar
part of the product of velocity and acceleration is zero. This is the
standard four-acceleration orthogonality relation. The proper-acceleration
magnitude \(\sqrt{-\detf{\pv{A}}}\) is unchanged by a Lorentz transform; the
paravector \(\pv{A}\) itself is not unchanged.

A Lagrangian is a scalar function whose time integral is made stationary;
its derivatives give the equations of motion through the
Euler--Lagrange equation \cite{einstein1905,jackson1998}. For a free particle,
it is defined here by
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L:=-\frac{m}{\gamma},
\label{eq:free-lagrangian}
\end{equation}
from whose Euler--Lagrange equation constant-velocity motion and the usual
low-speed expansion are reproduced:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=-m+\frac{1}{2}m\vct{v}^2+O(\vct{v}^4).
\label{eq:free-lagrangian-low}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:free-lagrangian-low} is displayed explicitly to exhibit the reduction of the relativistic action to the classical kinetic expression plus an additive rest-energy constant in the low-speed limit.

\section{Differential Operators}\label{sec:differential}
One fixed set of differential conventions is used in the electromagnetic and wave-equation sections. The conventions are collected here so that the same numbered equations can be referenced later without repeated operator definitions.

For spatial derivatives with respect to
\(\vct{r}=\vthree{r_1}{r_2}{r_3}\), the derivatives are defined by
\(\partial_t:=\partial/\partial t\) and
\(\partial_n:=\partial/\partial r_n\) for \(n\in\{1,2,3\}\). The spatial
derivative vector is defined by
\begin{equation}
\delr:=\vthreeeq
{\dfrac{\partial}{\partial r_1}}
{\dfrac{\partial}{\partial r_2}}
{\dfrac{\partial}{\partial r_3}}
=\vthree{\partial_1}{\partial_2}{\partial_3}.
\label{eq:gradient-notation}
\end{equation}
The corresponding spatial Laplacian is
\begin{equation}
\gradv^2:=\partial_1^2+\partial_2^2+\partial_3^2.
\label{eq:laplacian}
\end{equation}
The differential paravector and its conjugate are
\begin{align}
\pv{\partial}&:=\partial_t+\sigv\cdot\gradv
=\partial_t+\pv{\sigma}_1\partial_1+\pv{\sigma}_2\partial_2+\pv{\sigma}_3\partial_3,
\label{eq:partial-operator}\\
\conj{\pv{\partial}}&:=\partial_t-\sigv\cdot\gradv
=\partial_t-\pv{\sigma}_1\partial_1-\pv{\sigma}_2\partial_2-\pv{\sigma}_3\partial_3.
\label{eq:partial-conjugate}
\end{align}
Because the coordinate derivatives commute on smooth fields, a scalar
second-order wave operator is obtained from their ordered products:
\begin{equation}
\Box:=\pv{\partial}\conj{\pv{\partial}}.
\label{eq:dalembertian}
\end{equation}
The reversed order gives the same scalar operator:
\begin{equation}
\Box=\conj{\pv{\partial}}\pv{\partial}
=\partial_t^2-\gradv^2.
\label{eq:dalembertian-expansion}
\end{equation}
This operator is conventionally called the d'Alembertian
\cite{klein1926,gordon1926}. The ordinary two-term derivative product rule
must be corrected when the left field does not commute with the sigma basis.
For smooth paravector fields \(\pv{Z}\) and \(\pv{Z}'\), the following result
is obtained by direct expansion:
\begin{equation}
\pv{\partial}(\pv{Z}\pv{Z}')
=(\pv{\partial}\pv{Z})\pv{Z}'
+\pv{Z}(\pv{\partial}\pv{Z}')
+\sum_{n=1}^{3}\com{\pv{\sigma}_n}{\pv{Z}}\,\partial_n\pv{Z}'.
\label{eq:paravector-leibniz}
\end{equation}
The derivatives act on fields written to their right, with parentheses
delimiting their scope. The final term is generated when \(\pv{Z}\) is moved
through the left-acting basis element \(\pv{\sigma}_n\). It is zero for a
scalar \(\pv{Z}\), or
whenever \(\com{\pv{\sigma}_n}{\pv{Z}}=0\) for every contributing direction,
and the ordinary product rule is then recovered.

\section{Electromagnetic Fields}\label{sec:em}
Electromagnetic fields, their potential freedom, and the Lorentz force can be
expressed in compact paravector forms. Different potentials that give the
same electric and magnetic fields are said to be related by a gauge change.
In physical content, Maxwell theory is recovered in rationalized
Heaviside--Lorentz units \cite{maxwell1865,jackson1998}; the packaging is
drawn from later space-time-algebra presentations
\cite{hestenes1966,baylis1999,doran2003}.

\subsection{Fields, potentials, and Maxwell equations}
The differential conventions are fixed once in Section
\ref{sec:differential}; in particular, the spatial derivative vector, the
paravector derivative, its conjugate, and the d'Alembertian are defined by
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:gradient-notation}--\eqref{eq:dalembertian}. The quantities
\(V,\rho,S,\lambda\) are real scalar fields of time and position:
\(V\) is the scalar potential, \(\rho\) is charge density, \(S\) is defined
below, and \(\lambda\) labels a change of potential. The quantities
\(\vct{E},\vct{B},\vct{A},\vct{J}\) are real three-vector fields: electric
field, magnetic field, vector potential, and electric-current density,
respectively. The charge \(q\) is a real constant. With these meanings fixed,
the electromagnetic field vector is defined by
\begin{equation}
\vct{F}:=\vct{E}+i\vct{B}.
\label{eq:field-vector}
\end{equation}
The corresponding pure-vector paravector is defined by
\begin{equation}
\pv{F}:=\vct{F}\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:F-Phi}
\end{equation}
The real potential paravector is defined separately by
\begin{equation}
\pv{\Phi}:=V+\vct{A}\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:potential-paravector}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\pv{\Phi}\in\RPspace\), while
\(\pv{F}\in\CPspace\) is restricted to have zero scalar part. Electric and
magnetic fields are packaged into \(\pv{F}\) by Eq.\ \eqref{eq:F-Phi}, and
scalar and vector potentials are packaged into \(\pv{\Phi}\).

The scalar part generated by the potential is defined by
\begin{equation}
S:=\partial_t V+\gradv\cdot\vct{A}.
\label{eq:potential-scalar}
\end{equation}
The relations in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:E-B-from-potential} are then encoded by
\begin{equation}
\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}=S+\conj{\pv{F}}.
\label{eq:potential-gradient}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\vct{E}=-\partial_t\vct{A}-\gradv V,
\qquad
\vct{B}=\gradv\times\vct{A}.
\label{eq:E-B-from-potential}
\end{equation}
For a vector field, \(\gradv\cdot\vct{A}\) measures its local outward
flow and is conventionally called the divergence, while
\(\gradv\times\vct{A}\) measures its local circulation and is conventionally
called the curl.
The compact Maxwell equation is
\begin{equation}
\pv{\partial}\pv{F}=\rho-\vct{J}\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:maxwell}
\end{equation}
By splitting the real/imaginary scalar/vector parts of \eqref{eq:maxwell}, the following equations are reproduced:
\begin{align}
\gradv\cdot\vct{E}&=\rho,
&
\gradv\cdot\vct{B}&=0,
\label{eq:maxwell-scalars}\\
\gradv\times\vct{B}&=\vct{J}+\partial_t\vct{E},
&
\gradv\times\vct{E}&=-\partial_t\vct{B}.
\label{eq:maxwell-vectors}
\end{align}
Agreement with standard space-time-algebra treatments is obtained in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:maxwell-scalars} and \eqref{eq:maxwell-vectors}, subject to the sign conventions fixed by Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:partial-operator}, \eqref{eq:partial-conjugate}, \eqref{eq:dalembertian}, and \eqref{eq:F-Phi}. Maxwell's field equations are recovered component by component in the present signature and field convention \cite{maxwell1865,hestenes1966,baylis1999,jackson1998}.
Separate primitive divergence and curl operators are avoided at this stage in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:maxwell}. They are produced automatically when the scalar and vector parts of the single paravector equation are separated.

\subsection{Boost transformation of electromagnetic fields}
The field paravector is transformed by the same boost element \(\pv{L}\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:boost-element}:
\begin{equation}
\pv{F}'=\pv{L}^{-1}\pv{F}\pv{L}
=\conj{\pv{L}}\pv{F}\pv{L}.
\label{eq:field-transform}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:field-transform} is the similarity form
\(\pv{L}^{-1}\pv{F}\pv{L}\). It is deliberately different from the
Hermitian event congruence
\(\pv{X}'=\pv{L}^{-1}\pv{X}(\pv{L}^{-1})^{\Hop}\).
The event and field laws were derived from different algebraic actions in
Section \ref{sec:transform-algebra}; the usual mixing of
\(\eqfunc{par}(\vct{E})\), \(\eqfunc{prp}(\vct{E})\),
\(\eqfunc{par}(\vct{B})\), and \(\eqfunc{prp}(\vct{B})\) is obtained from
the field similarity.

\subsection{Wave equations, gauge, and Lorentz force}
Two wave-equation consequences of Maxwell's equation are obtained by
applying \(\conj{\pv{\partial}}\) to Eq.\ \eqref{eq:maxwell} and
\(\pv{\partial}\) to its star-conjugate equation:
\begin{align}
\Box\pv{F}=\conj{\pv{\partial}}(\rho-\vct{J}\cdot\sigv),
\label{eq:wave-F}
\\
\Box\conj{\pv{F}}
=\pv{\partial}(\rho+\vct{J}\cdot\sigv).
\label{eq:wave-F-conjugate}
\end{align}
Because \(\Box\) is scalar and \(\pv{F}\) has zero scalar part, the left side
of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:wave-F} has zero scalar part. Its right-side scalar part
therefore gives charge conservation:
\begin{equation}
\partial_t\rho+\gradv\cdot\vct{J}=0.
\label{eq:charge-conservation}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:charge-conservation} is the continuity equation written in the present notation.
It states that a local change of charge density is balanced by charge flowing
through the surrounding boundary \cite{jackson1998}.
Different potentials may describe the same electric and magnetic fields.
Such potentials are said to differ by a gauge change
\cite{jackson1998}. For a real scalar gauge function \(\lambda\), a function
input is used so that confusion with a Lorentz-frame prime is avoided:
\begin{equation}
\pv{\Phi}(\lambda):=\pv{\Phi}+\conj{\pv{\partial}}\lambda.
\label{eq:gauge}
\end{equation}
The scalar and vector potential functions contained in this paravector are
defined by
\begin{align}
V(\lambda)&:=V+\partial_t\lambda,
\label{eq:gauge-scalar-potential}\\
\vct{A}(\lambda)&:=\vct{A}-\gradv\lambda.
\label{eq:gauge-vector-potential}
\end{align}
The scalar part generated by the transformed potential is defined by
\begin{align}
S(\lambda)
&:=\partial_tV(\lambda)+\gradv\cdot\vct{A}(\lambda)\notag\\
&=S+\bigl(\partial_t^2-\gradv^2\bigr)\lambda\notag\\
&=S+\Box\lambda.
\label{eq:gauge-scalar-part}
\end{align}
The corresponding field function is defined from the transformed potential
by the same relation as Eq.\ \eqref{eq:potential-gradient}:
\begin{align}
\conj{\pv{F}(\lambda)}
&:=\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}(\lambda)-S(\lambda)\notag\\
&=\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}+\Box\lambda-S-\Box\lambda\notag\\
&=\conj{\pv{F}}.
\label{eq:gauge-field-from-potential}
\end{align}
Application of the star operation to the final equality gives
\begin{equation}
\pv{F}(\lambda)=\pv{F}.
\label{eq:gauge-field}
\end{equation}
The potential \(\pv{\Phi}\) is retained even when the field is physically
primary because many potentials related by Eq.\ \eqref{eq:gauge} give the
same \(\pv{F}\). A quantity unchanged by this replacement is called gauge
invariant; the derivation ending at Eq.\ \eqref{eq:gauge-field} shows that
\(\pv{F}\) has
this property \cite{jackson1998}.

For a particle of real mass \(m>0\), real charge \(q\), and four-velocity \(\pv{U}\), the four-force formula is
\begin{equation}
m\frac{\dd \pv{U}}{\dd s}=q\,\eqfunc{real}\bigl(\pv{F}\pv{U}\bigr)
=q\gamma\Big(\vct{v}\cdot\vct{E}+(\vct{E}+\vct{v}\times\vct{B})\cdot\sigv\Big),
\label{eq:lorentz-force}
\end{equation}
in which both relativistic power and the Lorentz three-force are packaged. Since \(\dd/\dd s=\gamma\,\dd/\dd t\), the equations \(\dd E/\dd t=q\vct{v}\cdot\vct{E}\) and \(\dd\vct{P}/\dd t=q(\vct{E}+\vct{v}\times\vct{B})\) are obtained by division by \(\gamma\).

\subsection{Lagrangian and energy-momentum remarks}
Here potential coupling means adding
\(-q(V-\vct{v}\cdot\vct{A})\) to the free-particle Lagrangian. The resulting
function is defined by
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L:=-\frac{m}{\gamma}-q(V-\vct{v}\cdot\vct{A}),
\label{eq:em-lagrangian}
\end{equation}
and
\(\dd\vct{P}/\dd t=q(\vct{E}+\vct{v}\times\vct{B})\) is recovered by
substitution into the Euler--Lagrange equation. Thus the potential form gives
the same force written directly with \(\vct{E}\) and \(\vct{B}\)
\cite{jackson1998}.

The frame-dependent field energy-density and flux paravector is defined by
\begin{equation}
\pv{T}:=\frac{1}{2}\pv{F}\herm{\pv{F}}.
\label{eq:energy-momentum-paravector}
\end{equation}
Its scalar and vector parts are
\begin{equation}
\pv{T}
=\frac{1}{2}(\vct{E}^2+\vct{B}^2)
+(\vct{E}\times\vct{B})\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:energy-momentum-components}
\end{equation}
The first term in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:energy-momentum-components} is the field
energy per unit volume. The vector \(\vct{E}\times\vct{B}\) is the field
energy flux and is conventionally called the Poynting vector
\cite{jackson1998}. These two quantities are packaged into one
frame-dependent paravector. The supporting subsection explains how the
energy and momentum seen along any chosen observer direction are obtained
from a map conventionally called the electromagnetic stress--energy map.

\section{Relativistic Wave Equations and Spin Dynamics}\label{sec:qm}
The standard relativistic wave equations are obtained from the same ordered
product rules. Three allowed wave-function forms are distinguished: a complex
scalar for the Klein--Gordon equation; a pair of two-amplitude right-projected
paravectors for the two blocks of a Dirac field; and the two-amplitude field
that remains in the slow-motion Pauli equation. With these forms separated,
unused algebra coefficients cannot be mistaken for independent physical
wave-function components
\cite{klein1926,gordon1926,dirac1928,pauli1927}.
Every spatial integral in this section is evaluated at fixed time. Unless a
region is named, the integration is extended over all real values of the three spatial
components; the measure is written directly as
\(\dd r_1\,\dd r_2\,\dd r_3\).

\subsection{Klein--Gordon field and frequency splitting}
The relativistic energy--momentum relation
\(E^2-\vct{P}^2=m^2\) supplies the physical starting point. Wave mechanics is
then introduced through the established replacement of energy and momentum
by the displayed differential operators; that replacement is physical input
and is not obtained from the determinant identity by algebra alone
\cite{klein1926,gordon1926,greiner2000}. For a complex scalar field
\(\psi_{\eqtext{KG}}\in\C\), the free Klein--Gordon equation is
\begin{equation}
(\pv{\partial}\conj{\pv{\partial}}+m^2)\psi_{\eqtext{KG}}
=(\Box+m^2)\psi_{\eqtext{KG}}=0.
\label{eq:kg-free}
\end{equation}
The spatial domain means the chosen set of fields together with the decay,
periodic, or zero-boundary conditions imposed on them. On such a domain, the
positive frequency operator is defined by
\begin{equation}
\Omega:=\sqrt{m^2-\gradv^2}.
\label{eq:frequency-operator}
\end{equation}
Here a spatial Fourier mode means a plane-wave part with a definite spatial
wave vector \(\vct{k}\). The operator \(\Omega\) multiplies that part by the
positive real number \(\sqrt{m^2+\vct{k}^2}\)
\cite{greiner2000}. When \(\Omega^{-1}\) exists
on the chosen data,
the two frequency functions are defined by
\begin{equation}
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_split}}(\pm)
:=\frac12\left(
\psi_{\eqtext{KG}}\pm
i\Omega^{-1}\partial_t\psi_{\eqtext{KG}}
\right).
\label{eq:frequency-functions}
\end{equation}
They satisfy
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
(\partial_t+i\Omega)\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_split}}(+)=0,\qquad
(\partial_t-i\Omega)\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_split}}(-)=0,\\
\psi_{\eqtext{KG}}
=\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_split}}(+)+\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_split}}(-).
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:frequency-split}
\end{equation}
Thus
\[
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_split}}(t,\pm)
=\exp(\mp i\Omega t)
\left.\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_split}}(t,\pm)\right|_{t=0}.
\]
When \(m=0\) and \(\vct{k}=\vct{0}\), the multiplier is zero and its inverse
does not exist; this massless zero mode is treated separately. The scalar
\(\lvert\psi_{\eqtext{KG}}\rvert^2\) is not the relativistic
Klein--Gordon probability density;
derivatives are contained in the conserved current, which is not positive on
the full positive/negative-frequency solution space.
Frequency is labeled in this subsection by the sign input of
\(\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_split}}(\pm)\). That input is unrelated to the
plus/minus input of the spin-axis projectors in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projectors}.

\subsection{Potential coupling and ordered products}
The established prescription called minimal coupling replaces the free
energy and momentum operators by the following potential-dependent
operators \cite{jackson1998,greiner2000}. They are gauge covariant: when the
potential and field are changed together as displayed below, their action
acquires only the same phase as the field. With the real potential
\(\pv{\Phi}=V+\vct{A}\cdot\sigv\), the energy operator is defined by
\begin{equation}
E:=i\partial_t-qV.
\label{eq:energy-operator}
\end{equation}
The momentum operator is defined by
\begin{equation}
\vct{P}:=-i\gradv-q\vct{A}.
\label{eq:momentum-operator}
\end{equation}
The scalar equation with the stated electromagnetic potential is
\begin{equation}
(-E^2+\vct{P}^2+m^2)\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}=0,
\label{eq:kg-coupled}
\end{equation}
or, after applying the derivative product rule,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\Bigl(\Box+iqS-q^2\detf{\pv{\Phi}}+m^2\\
&\qquad
2iq(V\partial_t+\vct{A}\cdot\gradv)\Bigr)\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}=0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:kg-expanded}
\end{equation}
The unchanged form of the scalar equation after a simultaneous gauge change
of the potential and phase change of the field is called gauge covariance.
Under Eq.\ \eqref{eq:gauge}, the transformed scalar field is defined by
\begin{equation}
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}'
:=\exp(-iq\lambda)\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}.
\label{eq:scalar-gauge-field}
\end{equation}
The gauge-dependent energy and momentum operators are defined by
\begin{align}
E(\lambda)&:=i\partial_t-q\,V(\lambda),
\label{eq:gauge-energy-operator}\\
\vct{P}(\lambda)&:=-i\gradv-q\,\vct{A}(\lambda).
\label{eq:gauge-momentum-operator}
\end{align}
The following relations are obtained by direct application of the product
rule:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&E(\lambda)\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}'\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
=\exp(-iq\lambda)E\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}},\\
&\vct{P}(\lambda)\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}'\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
=\exp(-iq\lambda)\vct{P}\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:kg-gauge}
\end{equation}
so the same form of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:kg-coupled} is retained in every gauge.
For smooth fields,
\begin{align}
\com{E}{P_m}&=iqE_m,\notag\\
\com{P_m}{P_n}&=iq(\partial_mA_n-\partial_nA_m).
\label{eq:commutators}
\end{align}
Consequently, the written order must be retained:
\begin{align}
(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})(E-\sigv\cdot\vct{P})
 &=E^2-\vct{P}^2-iq\pv{F},
\label{eq:EP-factorization}\\
(E-\sigv\cdot\vct{P})(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})
 &=E^2-\vct{P}^2-iq\conj{\pv{F}}.
\label{eq:EP-factorization-conjugate}
\end{align}
Here
\[
\begin{aligned}
\conj{\pv{F}}&=(-\vct{E}+i\vct{B})\cdot\sigv,\\
-iq\pv{F}&=(-iq\vct{E}+q\vct{B})\cdot\sigv,\\
-iq\conj{\pv{F}}&=(iq\vct{E}+q\vct{B})\cdot\sigv.
\end{aligned}
\]
These are ordered star--\(\Hop\) products of differential operators, not
applications of the scalar-coefficient determinant formula. In a background
field the full products do not reproduce only the scalar Klein--Gordon
operator: the displayed \(\pv{F}\) or \(\conj{\pv{F}}\) term remains. The
scalar equation is recovered by taking the scalar part or by subtracting that
remaining term explicitly. The same remaining terms are converted into the
established spin--field terms when the Dirac system is squared; they are not
additional scalar-wave components.

\subsection{Klein--Gordon current and pairing}
For \(m>0\), let
\(\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}\) and
\(\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}\) be two scalar solutions of
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:kg-coupled} in the same real potential. A current constructed
from these two solutions is called a mixed current; its inputs need not be
the same. Its density function is defined by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\rho\!\left(
\begin{gathered}
\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}},\\[-0.2em]
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}
\end{gathered}
\right)\\
&\quad:=\frac{1}{2m}\Bigl(
\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}^*
E\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}\\
&\qquad\qquad
+(E\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}})^*
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}
\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:kg-mixed-current}
\end{equation}
The associated spatial-current function is defined by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\vct{j}\!\left(
\begin{gathered}
\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}},\\[-0.2em]
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}
\end{gathered}
\right)\\
&\quad:=\frac{1}{2m}\Bigl(
\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}^*
\vct{P}\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}\\
&\qquad\qquad
+(\vct{P}\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}})^*
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}
\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:kg-mixed-spatial-current}
\end{equation}
Star here is ordinary complex conjugation because both fields are scalars.
Gauge invariance of this mixed current and its pairing is established
immediately by Eq.\ \eqref{eq:kg-gauge} when the same phase is acquired by both solutions.
The local identity is obtained from the product rules:
\begin{align}
&\partial_t\rho\!\left(
\begin{gathered}
\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}},\\[-0.2em]
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}
\end{gathered}
\right)\notag\\
&\quad+\gradv\cdot\vct{j}\!\left(
\begin{gathered}
\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}},\\[-0.2em]
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}
\end{gathered}
\right)\notag\\
&=\frac{i}{2m}\Bigl(
\left((E^2-\vct{P}^2)
\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}\right)^*
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}\notag\\
&\qquad
-\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}^*
(E^2-\vct{P}^2)
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}
\Bigr)\notag\\
&=\frac{i}{2m}\Bigl(
m^2\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}^*
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}\notag\\
&\qquad
-\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}^*
m^2\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}
\Bigr)=0.
\label{eq:kg-continuity-proof}
\end{align}
The ordinary conserved Klein--Gordon current is obtained by using the same
field for both inputs; its electric current is obtained by multiplication by
\(q\). A pairing assigns one scalar to two solutions. The mixed-solution
pairing is defined here by the spatial integral
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\left\langle
\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}
\mathrel{\big|}
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}
\right\rangle\\
&\quad:=\iiint\rho\!\left(
\begin{gathered}
\phi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}},\\[-0.2em]
\psi_{\eqtext{KG\_with\_potential}}
\end{gathered}
\right)\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad\qquad\dd r_1\,\dd r_2\,\dd r_3.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:kg-pairing}
\end{equation}
For free positive-frequency solutions, the pairing is
\[
\left\langle\phi_{\eqtext{KG}}\mathrel{\big|}
\psi_{\eqtext{KG}}\right\rangle
=\frac1m\iiint
\phi_{\eqtext{KG}}^*\Omega\psi_{\eqtext{KG}}\,
\dd r_1\,\dd r_2\,\dd r_3,
\]
which is positive for equal nonzero fields. The local density can nevertheless
be negative at some points in a positive-frequency superposition. Therefore
a positive local position probability is not supplied by
\(\rho(\psi_{\eqtext{KG}},\psi_{\eqtext{KG}})\) in the scalar theory.
On the full positive- and
negative-frequency solution space, even the integrated pairing can be
positive, zero, or negative and therefore cannot serve as a positive norm.

\subsection{Fixed right-projector space for one Dirac field}
An unrestricted complex paravector contains four complex coefficients,
whereas each of the two parts of one Dirac field contains only two. The
required two-coefficient space is selected intrinsically by one fixed
right-projector. Its third-axis unit direction and positive projector are
defined, in that order, by
\begin{align}
\vct{u}
&:=\vct{e}_3=\vthree{0}{0}{1},
\qquad \vct{u}^2=1,
\label{eq:fixed-column-axis}\\
\pv{\Pi}(+)
&:=\frac12(1+\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)
=\frac12(1+\pv{\sigma}_3).
\label{eq:fixed-column-carrier}
\end{align}
Each paravector block of one Dirac field is required to satisfy
\begin{equation}
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},n}
=\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},n}\pv{\Pi}(+),
\qquad n\in\{1,2\}.
\label{eq:fixed-column-constraint}
\end{equation}
Every operator below acts from the left, so associativity preserves this
condition:
\begin{equation}
(\pv{Z}\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},n})\pv{\Pi}(+)
=\pv{Z}\bigl(
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},n}\pv{\Pi}(+)
\bigr)
=\pv{Z}\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},n}.
\label{eq:fixed-projector-preservation}
\end{equation}
The plus input identifies the positive part of this permanently chosen
third axis; this right carrier is not itself a spin measurement. Using the
ladder element \(\pv{a}(-)\) from
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:ladder}, every block satisfying
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:fixed-column-constraint} has the unique form
\begin{equation}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}
=a\pv{\Pi}(+)+b\pv{a}(-),
\qquad a,b\in\C.
\label{eq:fixed-projector-amplitudes}
\end{equation}
Indeed, a paravector
\(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}=S+\vct{V}\cdot\sigv\) satisfies the
restriction exactly when
\(V_3=S\) and \(V_2=-iV_1\), with \(a=2S\) and \(b=2V_1\).
Thus exactly two complex amplitudes are retained. Its optional ordinary-matrix
translation is supplied in Section \ref{sec:matrix-state-pairings}.

For any projected block \(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}\), the positive density
function is
defined by
\begin{equation}
\rho(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}})
:=2\,\eqfunc{scalar}\left(
\herm{\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}}\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}
\right).
\label{eq:projected-block-density-function}
\end{equation}
For the expansion in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:fixed-projector-amplitudes}, the
projector and ladder products give
\(\rho(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}})=|a|^2+|b|^2\). The density is therefore
real and
nonnegative without use of an auxiliary translation.

The two blocks are treated as the ordered pair
\(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}
:=(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},1},\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},2})\).
The free Dirac equation is defined by the two coupled paravector equations
\begin{equation}
i\pv{\partial}\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},2}
=m\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},1},
\qquad
i\conj{\pv{\partial}}\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},1}
=m\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},2}.
\label{eq:dirac-free}
\end{equation}
With the real electromagnetic potential, the coupled Dirac equation is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}})
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}
&=m\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1},\\
(i\conj{\pv{\partial}}-q\pv{\Phi})
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1}
&=m\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-coupled}
\end{equation}
Using the energy and momentum operators from Eqs.\
\eqref{eq:energy-operator} and \eqref{eq:momentum-operator}, the same system is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
(E-\sigv\cdot\vct{P})
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}
&=m\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1},\\
(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1}
&=m\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-coupled-blocks}
\end{equation}
To display every scalar component, the two blocks are written intrinsically
as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1}\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
=a\pv{\Pi}(+)+b\pv{a}(-),\\
&\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
=c\pv{\Pi}(+)+d\pv{a}(-).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-amplitude-expansion}
\end{equation}
The actions of the three sigma generators on
\(\pv{\Pi}(+)\) and \(\pv{a}(-)\), obtained from
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:ladder-properties}, reduce
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled-blocks} to
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
(E-P_3)c-(P_1-iP_2)d&=ma,\\
-(P_1+iP_2)c+(E+P_3)d&=mb,\\
(E+P_3)a+(P_1-iP_2)b&=mc,\\
(P_1+iP_2)a+(E-P_3)b&=md.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-four-components}
\end{equation}
These are the four established first-order Dirac component equations
\cite{dirac1928,greiner2000}; only the defined sigma products were used.
The free system is recovered by setting \(q=0\).

A transformation preserves the form of an equation when the primed
quantities satisfy the same relation as the unprimed quantities. For a
constant transform \(\pv{T}\) with determinant one, this condition fixes the
two block transformations:
\begin{align}
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},1}'(t',\vct{r}')
&=\pv{T}^{\Hop}\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},1}(t,\vct{r}),
\label{eq:dirac-transform}\\
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},2}'(t',\vct{r}')
&=\pv{T}^{-1}\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},2}(t,\vct{r}).
\label{eq:dirac-transform-lower}
\end{align}
As shown by the supporting derivation of
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-transform} and \eqref{eq:dirac-transform-lower}, the
first block is therefore acted upon by \(\pv{L}\) under a boost, whereas the
second is acted upon by \(\conj{\pv{L}}\); both are acted upon by
\(\pv{R}^{\Hop}\) under a rotation. This is the wave-function law, distinct from
both event congruence and field similarity.

The potential-coupled ordered pair is
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}}
&:=\Bigl(\\[-0.4em]
&\quad\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1},\\[-0.2em]
&\quad\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}
\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
For this ordered pair, the Dirac function is defined by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\mathcal{D}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})
:=\Bigl(\\[-0.3em]
&\quad
(E-\sigv\cdot\vct{P})
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
-m\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1},\\
&\quad
(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1}\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
-m\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}
\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-potential-operator}
\end{equation}
Thus Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled} is equivalently
\(\mathcal{D}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})
=(\pv{0},\pv{0})\). For a generic ordered pair
\((\pv{Y}_1,\pv{Y}_2)\), the companion function with the mass sign reversed
is defined by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{D}'(\pv{Y}_1,\pv{Y}_2)
:=\bigl(&
(E-\sigv\cdot\vct{P})\pv{Y}_2+m\pv{Y}_1,\\
&
(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})\pv{Y}_1+m\pv{Y}_2
\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-mass-conjugate-operator}
\end{equation}
The prime in \(\mathcal{D}'\) labels the explicitly stated alternative mass
sign, in accordance with Eq.\ \eqref{eq:prime-convention}; it is not a
derivative or a Hermitian operation. This companion function is applied to
\(\mathcal D\) evaluated on the potential-coupled Dirac field. Use of the
ordered factorizations
\eqref{eq:EP-factorization}--\eqref{eq:EP-factorization-conjugate} then gives
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&(-E^2+\vct{P}^2+m^2+iq\conj{\pv{F}})\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1}=0,\\[2pt]
&(-E^2+\vct{P}^2+m^2+iq\pv{F})\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}=0.
\end{aligned}
\right.
\label{eq:dirac-square}
\end{equation}
With
\(iq\conj{\pv{F}}=(-iq\vct{E}-q\vct{B})\cdot\sigv\) and
\(iq\pv{F}=(iq\vct{E}-q\vct{B})\cdot\sigv\), the same equations are written as
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&\bigl(-E^2+\vct{P}^2+m^2
-q\vct{B}\cdot\sigv\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad-iq\vct{E}\cdot\sigv\bigr)
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1}=0,\\[2pt]
&\bigl(-E^2+\vct{P}^2+m^2
-q\vct{B}\cdot\sigv\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad+iq\vct{E}\cdot\sigv\bigr)
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}=0.
\end{aligned}
\right.
\label{eq:dirac-square-components}
\end{equation}
Thus the expected Klein--Gordon operator, the same magnetic spin coupling in
both blocks, and the opposite electric spin couplings of the two blocks are
obtained component by component by squaring the first-order system.

Under the gauge law \eqref{eq:gauge},
\begin{equation}
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}}'
:=\exp(-iq\lambda)\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}}.
\label{eq:dirac-gauge}
\end{equation}
The form of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled} is then unchanged.

The Hermitian product supplies a nonnegative density whose spatial integral
is the squared norm at a fixed time. Its vector part is not a probability
current. With the fixed-projector convention above, the Dirac density function
is defined by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\rho(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})\\[-0.2em]
&\quad:=2\sum_{n=1}^{2}
\eqfunc{scalar}\!\Bigl(\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
\herm{\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},n}}
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},n}
\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-density}
\end{equation}
The spatial-current function is defined by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\vct{j}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})\\[-0.2em]
&\quad:=2\,\eqfunc{vector}\!\Bigl(\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}
\herm{\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}}\\[-0.1em]
&\qquad
-\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1}
\herm{\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1}}
\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-spatial-current}
\end{equation}
The full current paravector is defined by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\pv{J}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})\\[-0.2em]
&\quad:=\rho(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
+\vct{j}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})
\cdot\sigv.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-current-paravector}
\end{equation}
Its density and spatial-current components are real, so the current in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-current-paravector} belongs to \(\RPspace\).
The two definitions satisfy the continuity equation
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\partial_t\rho(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})\\[-0.2em]
&\quad+\gradv\cdot
\vct{j}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})=0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-current}
\end{equation}
The normalization condition is the spatial integral
\[
\iiint\rho(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})\,
\dd r_1\,\dd r_2\,\dd r_3=1,
\]
not an equation imposed separately at every position. By
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:fixed-projector-amplitudes},
\(\rho(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})\) is the sum of four
absolute squares. The established Dirac spatial current is supplied by the
displayed opposite-order products, rather than by the vector part of a single
Hermitian norm product \cite{dirac1928,greiner2000}.
The real current paravector from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-current-paravector}
obeys the event congruence, as shown in the supporting derivation.
The component balances are obtained directly from the two time-evolution
equations and the amplitude expansion
\eqref{eq:dirac-amplitude-expansion}:
\begin{align}
\partial_t(|a|^2+|b|^2)
&=2\gradv\cdot\eqfunc{vector}\!\Bigl(\notag\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1}
\herm{\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},1}}
\Bigr)\notag\\
&\quad+im\bigl(c^*a+d^*b-a^*c-b^*d\bigr),\notag\\
\partial_t(|c|^2+|d|^2)
&=-2\gradv\cdot\eqfunc{vector}\!\Bigl(\notag\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}
\herm{\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},2}}
\Bigr)\notag\\
&\quad+im\bigl(a^*c+b^*d-c^*a-d^*b\bigr).
\label{eq:dirac-continuity-components}
\end{align}
By addition of the two lines, the mass exchange cancels and the exact
component density is
\begin{equation}
\rho(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})
=|a|^2+|b|^2+|c|^2+|d|^2.
\label{eq:dirac-density-components}
\end{equation}
The exact spatial-current components are
\begin{equation}
\vct{j}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})
=\vthreeeq
{c^*d+d^*c-a^*b-b^*a}
{i(d^*c-c^*d-b^*a+a^*b)}
{|c|^2-|d|^2-|a|^2+|b|^2}.
\label{eq:dirac-current-components}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:dirac-density-components} and
\eqref{eq:dirac-current-components} give the continuity equation
\eqref{eq:dirac-current}. Neither
block norm is conserved separately: norm is exchanged between them by the
mass terms.

\subsection{Probabilities and measurement averages}
A pairing assigns one complex scalar to two fields. For projected one-block
fields, the pairing is defined intrinsically by
\begin{equation}
\left\langle
\pv{\phi}_{\eqtext{block}}
\mathrel{\big|}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}
\right\rangle
:=2\iiint\eqfunc{scalar}\left(
\herm{\pv{\phi}_{\eqtext{block}}}\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}
\right)\,
\dd r_1\,\dd r_2\,\dd r_3.
\label{eq:adjoint-paravector-braket}
\end{equation}
For the amplitude forms
\(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}
=a\pv{\Pi}(+)+b\pv{a}(-)\) and
\(\pv{\phi}_{\eqtext{block}}
=c\pv{\Pi}(+)+d\pv{a}(-)\), the projector and ladder products
give
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\herm{\pv{\phi}_{\eqtext{block}}}\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}
&=(c^*a+d^*b)\pv{\Pi}(+),\\
&2\,\eqfunc{scalar}\!\left(
\herm{\pv{\phi}_{\eqtext{block}}}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}
\right)\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad=c^*a+d^*b.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:projected-amplitude-pairing}
\end{equation}
Thus Eq.\ \eqref{eq:adjoint-paravector-braket} is exactly the established
two-amplitude state pairing \cite{sakurai2017}. Its optional ordinary-matrix
translation is supplied in Section \ref{sec:matrix-state-pairings}.
For two projected two-block fields, the complete Dirac pairing is defined by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\left\langle
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_left}}
\mathrel{\big|}
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_right}}
\right\rangle\\
&\quad:=2\iiint\sum_{n=1}^{2}
\eqfunc{scalar}\!\left(
 \herm{\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_left},n}}
 \bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_right},n}
\right)\,\dd r_1\,\dd r_2\,\dd r_3.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-pairing}
\end{equation}
The two inputs are identified by their
\(\eqtext{Dirac\_left}\) and \(\eqtext{Dirac\_right}\) role labels; no new
kind of field is introduced. When both inputs are the same, the shorter
notation
\(\langle\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}\mid\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}\rangle\)
is used.
The factor \(2\) converts the scalar part of
\(\pv{\Pi}(+)\), which is \(1/2\), into the ordinary
two-amplitude pairing. For a specified operator \(\mathcal{O}\) acting on
ordered field pairs, the operator pairing is defined by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\left\langle
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_left}}
\mathrel{\big|}
\mathcal{O}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_right}})
\right\rangle\\
&:=2\iiint\sum_{n=1}^{2}
\eqfunc{scalar}\!\left(
\herm{\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_left},n}}
\bigl(\mathcal{O}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_right}})\bigr)_n
\right)\\
&\qquad\dd r_1\,\dd r_2\,\dd r_3.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-operator-pairing}
\end{equation}
The resulting scalar need not be real. Its normalized value is
\begin{equation}
\frac{
\langle\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}\mid
\mathcal{O}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}})\rangle
}{
\langle\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}\mid\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}\rangle
}.
\label{eq:dirac-expectation}
\end{equation}
When the same state is used on both sides and
\(\mathcal{O}\) is Hermitian under this spatial pairing, the real
normalized value is called the expectation value of the operator
\cite{sakurai2017}. Hermiticity under the pairing means
\begin{equation}
\left\langle
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_left}}
\mathrel{\big|}
\mathcal{O}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_right}})
\right\rangle
=
\left\langle
\mathcal{O}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_left}})
\mathrel{\big|}
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_right}}
\right\rangle.
\label{eq:dirac-pairing-hermitian}
\end{equation}
For derivatives, this equality also requires the stated
integration-by-parts boundary conditions and a common set of allowed fields.
If a Hermitian operator is also unchanged when applied twice, the probability
of the outcome selected by that projector is given by the same ratio.

For a normalized Dirac field, the probability of finding the system in a
specified spatial region at the chosen time is defined by
\begin{equation}
p(\eqtext{region})
:=\iiint\rho(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}})\,
\dd r_1\,\dd r_2\,\dd r_3,
\label{eq:region-probability}
\end{equation}
where the integration is restricted to the specified region.
The density is nonnegative, but it is the time component of the real current
paravector: its scalar and vector components mix under a Lorentz transform in
the same way as those of an event. The density alone is therefore not
unchanged by a Lorentz transform. The normalization is kept time-independent
by conservation when the boundary flux vanishes.

For measurement along a nonzero real axis vector \(\vct{V}\), the local unit
direction is \(\vct{u}:=\vct{V}/\|\vct{V}\|\), as in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-unit-axis}. For a projected Pauli block
\(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{axis\_state}}\), the factor \(2\) is canceled from
normalized ratios. The
probability function supplied by \(\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\) for this direction is
therefore defined by
\begin{equation}
p(\pm)
:=\frac{
\left\langle
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{axis\_state}}(\pm)
\mathrel{\big|}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{axis\_state}}(\pm)
\right\rangle
}{
\left\langle
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{axis\_state}}
\mathrel{\big|}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{axis\_state}}
\right\rangle
}.
\label{eq:pauli-projector-probability}
\end{equation}
By Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:projectors} and \eqref{eq:projected-part-function}, each
projected field is obtained by left multiplication:
\[
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{axis\_state}}(\pm)
=\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{axis\_state}}.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
p(+)+p(-)=1.
\label{eq:pauli-probability-sum}
\end{equation}
The expectation value of \(\vct{u}\cdot\sigv\) is \(p(+)-p(-)\); the physical
spin component along \(\vct{u}\) is one half of this value in the natural
units used here. The corresponding free two-block energy and momentum-axis
probability is defined after those fields have been constructed below.

The Klein--Gordon field is instead paired by Eq.\ \eqref{eq:kg-pairing}.
On the set of positive-frequency solutions, the normalized operator pairing
is
\[
\frac{
\langle\psi_{\eqtext{KG}}\mid
\mathcal{O}(\psi_{\eqtext{KG}})\rangle
}{
\langle\psi_{\eqtext{KG}}\mid\psi_{\eqtext{KG}}\rangle
}
\]
only when that set is preserved and the operator is Hermitian under the
Klein--Gordon pairing. The density
\(\rho(\psi_{\eqtext{KG}},\psi_{\eqtext{KG}})\) is not thereby
converted into a position-probability density at each position.
Likewise, the nonscalar parts of a generic two-sided paravector product are
only algebraic results. They do not represent additional measurements unless
a field equation and a measurement rule assign them that meaning.

\subsection{Slow-Motion Pauli Limit}
The reduction of the Dirac equation at energies small compared with \(m\) is
conventionally called the Pauli limit \cite{pauli1927,sakurai2017}. A single
block by itself is not the slow-motion large component. The rapid
factor \(\exp(-imt)\) produced by the rest energy is called the rest phase.
The construction in this subsection assumes \(m>0\).
First, the slowly varying blocks are defined by removing that factor:
\begin{equation}
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_slow},n}
:=\exp(imt)\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential},n}.
\label{eq:slow-field-definition}
\end{equation}
The large component is then defined by
\begin{equation}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}
:=\frac{
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_slow},1}
+\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_slow},2}
}{\sqrt{2}}.
\label{eq:large-small}
\end{equation}
The small component is defined independently by
\begin{equation}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_small}}
:=\frac{
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_slow},2}
-\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_slow},1}
}{\sqrt{2}}.
\label{eq:small-component}
\end{equation}
The words ``large'' and ``small'' describe their relative magnitudes only for
positive-energy states at energies small compared with \(m\); they do not
define new algebraic types.
Equation \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled-blocks} is then transformed exactly into
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
E\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}
&=(\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_small}},\\
(E+2m)\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_small}}
&=(\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:large-small-coupled}
\end{equation}
At energies small compared with \(m\),
\[
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_small}}
=\frac{\vct{P}\cdot\sigv}{2m}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}+O(m^{-2}).
\]
By use of
\[
(\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)^2
=\vct{P}^2-q\,\vct{B}\cdot\sigv,
\]
the following result is obtained:
\begin{equation}
E\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}
=\frac{\vct{P}^2-q\,\vct{B}\cdot\sigv}{2m}\,
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}
+O(m^{-2}).
\label{eq:pauli-operator}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
i\partial_t\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}
&=\left(\frac{(-i\gradv-q\vct{A})^2}{2m}+qV\right)
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}\\
&\quad-\frac{q}{2m}(\vct{B}\cdot\sigv)
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}+O(m^{-2}).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pauli}
\end{equation}
At the next order, the electric field produces a spin--orbit term, which
couples spin to motion through that field, and a Darwin term, which is
proportional to the local divergence of the electric field
\cite{sakurai2017}. Time-dependent corrections also occur. No term from that
order is retained selectively here.
The exact change of variables in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:large-small} and
\eqref{eq:small-component} gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\rho(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})\\[-0.2em]
&\quad=\rho(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}})
+\rho(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_small}}),\\
&\vct{j}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})\\[-0.2em]
&\quad=2\,\eqfunc{vector}\!\Bigl(\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}
\herm{\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_small}}}\\[-0.1em]
&\qquad
+\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_small}}
\herm{\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}}
\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:large-small-current-change}
\end{equation}
The projected-block density from
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projected-block-density-function} is therefore
\begin{equation}
\rho(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}})
=2\,\eqfunc{scalar}\bigl(
 \herm{\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}}
 \pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}\bigr).
\label{eq:pauli-density}
\end{equation}
Writing \(P_1,P_2,P_3\) for the three components of the momentum operator in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:momentum-operator}, the Pauli spatial-current function is
defined intrinsically by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\vct{j}(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}})
&:=\frac{1}{m}
\vthreeeq
{\begin{gathered}
\eqfunc{scalar}\!\left(
\herm{\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}}
P_1\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}\right.\\[-0.2em]
\left.{}+\herm{(P_1\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}})}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}
\right)
\end{gathered}}
{\begin{gathered}
\eqfunc{scalar}\!\left(
\herm{\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}}
P_2\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}\right.\\[-0.2em]
\left.{}+\herm{(P_2\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}})}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}
\right)
\end{gathered}}
{\begin{gathered}
\eqfunc{scalar}\!\left(
\herm{\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}}
P_3\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}\right.\\[-0.2em]
\left.{}+\herm{(P_3\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}})}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}
\right)
\end{gathered}}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{m}\gradv\times
\eqfunc{vector}\!\left(
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}
\herm{\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}}
\right).
\label{eq:pauli-current}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Because \(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_small}}=O(m^{-1})\), the original Dirac
density and current are reduced to
\(\rho(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})
=\rho(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}})+O(m^{-2})\) and
\(\vct{j}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_with\_potential}})
=\vct{j}(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}})+O(m^{-2})\).
The two Hermitian-conjugate contributions inside the first vector summand of
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:pauli-current} form the convection current associated with
motion through the potential-dependent momentum. The curl summand is the
magnetization current associated with spin; its divergence is zero. Their
standard sum is thereby obtained. The equation
\(\partial_t\rho(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}})
+\gradv\cdot\vct{j}(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}})=0\)
is obeyed through the retained order.

Where \(\vct{B}\ne0\), the local magnetic-field axis is defined by
\begin{equation}
\vct{u}:=\frac{\vct{B}}{\|\vct{B}\|}.
\label{eq:magnetic-axis}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:projectors} is then specialized as
\(\pv{\Pi}(\pm)=\tfrac12(1\pm\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)\).
The two spin-axis parts are defined by
\begin{equation}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_split}}(\pm)
:=\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}.
\label{eq:pauli-spin-decomposition}
\end{equation}
They reconstruct the large component:
\begin{equation}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_large}}
=\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_split}}(+)
+\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_split}}(-).
\label{eq:pauli-spin-reconstruction}
\end{equation}
If \(\vct{B}\) is also uniform and constant, then
\begin{align}
i\partial_t\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_split}}(\pm)
&=\left(\frac{\vct{P}^2}{2m}+qV\right)
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_split}}(\pm)\notag\\
&\quad\mp\frac{q\|\vct{B}\|}{2m}
\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{Pauli\_split}}(\pm)+O(m^{-2}).
\label{eq:pauli-spin-split}
\end{align}
For a varying axis, the two projected fields are coupled by derivatives of
\(\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\) and its commutator with the time-evolution operator on the
right of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:pauli}, so they no longer evolve independently.

\subsection{Free energy and momentum-axis split}
A free momentum mode is a plane-wave solution with a definite momentum
\(\vct{p}\). For such a mode, the positive energy function is defined by
\begin{equation}
E(\vct{p}):=\sqrt{m^2+\vct{p}^2}.
\label{eq:free-energy-function}
\end{equation}
The split below is defined when \(E(\vct{p})>0\); the massless
zero-momentum case has no positive-versus-negative energy split of this form.
The Hamiltonian function on an ordered field pair is defined by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\mathcal{H}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}})
:=\Bigl(\\[-0.3em]
&\quad
-(\sigv\cdot\vct{p})\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},1}
+m\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},2},\\[-0.1em]
&\quad
m\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},1}
+(\sigv\cdot\vct{p})\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},2}
\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled-blocks} then gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
i\partial_t\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}
&=\mathcal{H}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}),\\
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{H}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}))
&=E(\vct{p})^2\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-hamiltonian-action}
\end{equation}
The function \(\mathcal H\) is called the Hamiltonian because it supplies
\(i\partial_t\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}\). It is not a new field: its two
components are exactly the two free Dirac evolution equations. The
calligraphic symbol
\(\mathcal H\) is distinct from the superscript
\(\Hop\), which is reserved for the Hermitian operation
\cite{dirac1928,greiner2000}.

The two energy parts of the field are defined directly, without naming
another operator:
\begin{equation}
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy}}(\pm)
:=\frac12\left(
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}
\pm\frac{\mathcal{H}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}})}{E(\vct{p})}
\right).
\label{eq:energy-projected-field}
\end{equation}
Its two component blocks are defined explicitly by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy},1}(\pm)\\[-0.2em]
&\quad:=\frac12\Biggl(
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},1}\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad\pm\frac{
-(\sigv\cdot\vct{p})\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},1}
+m\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},2}
}{E(\vct{p})}
\Biggr),\\
&\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy},2}(\pm)\\[-0.2em]
&\quad:=\frac12\Biggl(
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},2}\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad\pm\frac{
m\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},1}
+(\sigv\cdot\vct{p})\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac},2}
}{E(\vct{p})}
\Biggr),\\
&\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy}}(\pm)\\[-0.2em]
&\quad:=\Bigl(
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy},1}(\pm),\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy},2}(\pm)
\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:energy-projected-components}
\end{equation}
An eigenvalue is the scalar that multiplies a nonzero state when an operator
acts on it \cite{sakurai2017}. The energy-multiplier and reconstruction
identities are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{H}(\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy}}(\pm))
&=\pm E(\vct{p})\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy}}(\pm),\\
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}
&=\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy}}(+)
+\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy}}(-).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:energy-projection-properties}
\end{equation}
The plus and minus inputs select the positive- and negative-energy parts of
the same free field.

For \(\vct{p}\ne0\), the momentum direction is defined by
\begin{equation}
\vct{u}:=\frac{\vct{p}}{\|\vct{p}\|}.
\label{eq:momentum-axis}
\end{equation}
For this direction, the existing axis projector from
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projectors} is
\begin{equation}
\pv{\Pi}(\pm)
=\frac12(1\pm\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)
=\frac12\left(1\pm\frac{\vct{p}\cdot\sigv}{\|\vct{p}\|}\right).
\label{eq:momentum-axis-projector}
\end{equation}
The sign input below labels the momentum-axis part. Positive and negative
energy are identified by separate field names, so the two independent signs
cannot be confused.
The simultaneous energy and momentum-axis fields are defined by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_positive\_energy\_axis}}(\pm)\\[-0.2em]
&\quad:=\Bigl(
\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy},1}(+),\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy},2}(+)
\Bigr),\\
&\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_negative\_energy\_axis}}(\pm)\\[-0.2em]
&\quad:=\Bigl(
\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy},1}(-),\\[-0.2em]
&\qquad
\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_energy},2}(-)
\Bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:energy-spin-projectors}
\end{equation}
The two sign choices satisfy
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
(\vct{p}\cdot\sigv)\pv{\Pi}(\pm)
&=\pm\|\vct{p}\|\pv{\Pi}(\pm),\\
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}
&=\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_positive\_energy\_axis}}(+)\\
&\quad+\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_positive\_energy\_axis}}(-)\\
&\quad+\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_negative\_energy\_axis}}(+)\\
&\quad+\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_negative\_energy\_axis}}(-).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:energy-spin-reconstruction}
\end{equation}
The plus or minus axis input is the scalar by which
\(\vct{u}\cdot\sigv\) multiplies the selected field. In the natural units
used here, the corresponding physical spin component along momentum is
\(+1/2\) or \(-1/2\). This spin component along the momentum direction is
conventionally called helicity; it is undefined when
\(\vct{p}=\vct{0}\) \cite{sakurai2017}.
The Hamiltonian is Hermitian, and the pairing of the two energy parts is
zero; they are therefore called orthogonal. The
already established \(\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\) projectors are Hermitian,
idempotent, and complementary. Because the momentum axis is used, commutation
of their left action with \(\mathcal H\) is obtained, so the four
displayed fields are simultaneous energy and momentum-axis parts. At rest any spin axis may
be chosen. Negative-frequency classical solutions are not interpreted as
antiparticles in this single-particle treatment. That interpretation belongs
to quantum field theory, in which particle number may change, and is not
developed here \cite{greiner2000}.

The joint probability function is now defined without referring to a field
before its construction:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
p(+,\pm)
&:=
\frac{
\begin{gathered}
\left\langle
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_positive\_energy\_axis}}(\pm)
\mathrel{\big|}\right.\\[-0.2em]
\left.
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_positive\_energy\_axis}}(\pm)
\right\rangle
\end{gathered}
}{
\langle\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}\mid\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}\rangle
},
\\
p(-,\pm)
&:=
\frac{
\begin{gathered}
\left\langle
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_negative\_energy\_axis}}(\pm)
\mathrel{\big|}\right.\\[-0.2em]
\left.
\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac\_negative\_energy\_axis}}(\pm)
\right\rangle
\end{gathered}
}{
\langle\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}\mid\bPsi_{\eqtext{Dirac}}\rangle
}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-projector-probability}
\end{equation}
Completeness gives
\begin{equation}
p(+,+)+p(+,-)+p(-,+)+p(-,-)=1.
\label{eq:dirac-probability-sum}
\end{equation}
For a free field with a finite spatial norm, this split is applied to each
momentum mode before the spatial pairing is evaluated. An isolated plane wave
does not have a finite spatial norm. It must instead be normalized in a
finite volume or by the established normalization convention for continuous
momentum values \cite{sakurai2017}.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}\label{sec:synthesis}
The algebra used here is the real Pauli algebra, written as one complex scalar plus one complex three-vector. Its economy is supplied by a structural fact rather than by a new physical postulate: the complex unit is supplied by the central pseudoscalar, dot and cross products are combined by the sigma product, and the determinant is obtained by multiplication by the adjugate. On real event paravectors, that determinant is the Minkowski interval.

Within this framework, spatial rotations, Lorentz boosts, four-velocity and
four-momentum, Maxwell's equations, the Lorentz force, scalar relativistic
waves, the two first-order Dirac equations whose product gives the
second-order equation, and the leading Pauli equation are derived in one
notation. The distinctions between object types are essential. Events are
transformed by the determinant-preserving two-sided event law, the
electromagnetic field is transformed by an inverse-pair similarity, and one
Dirac field is restricted to a fixed right-projector space. Once those
distinctions are enforced, agreement between the compact notation and the
established component equations is obtained.

Several complementary structures are also exposed by the reformulation. The
four Maxwell equations are obtained from the real and imaginary parts of
\(\pv{\partial}\pv{F}\); the two established spin--field terms
\(\pv{F}\) and \(\conj{\pv{F}}\) are obtained from the two ordered momentum
products; and elementary functions, Pauli spin parts, and free
spin-along-momentum parts are organized by the same axis projectors. These
are connections among established equations, not evidence for an additional
interaction.

In particular, no independent measured components are added by the
paravector wave-function forms. Each Dirac or Pauli block is reduced by the
fixed-projector condition to two complex amplitudes. The scalar
\(2\,\eqfunc{scalar}(
\herm{\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}}\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}
)\) is their nonnegative
squared magnitude, while the opposite-order product
\(\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}
\herm{\pv{\psi}_{\eqtext{block}}}\) retains their spin-direction
information. The
two squared field terms give the two established Dirac spin couplings. Other
nonscalar parts of two-sided products
remain algebraic until a separately derived measurement rule assigns them a
physical meaning.

\subsection{Scope of the Established Results}
The results in this paper are established under the assumptions of flat
Minkowski space, smooth fields, and sets of fields on which the displayed
differential operators and square roots are defined. The electromagnetic
discussion is classical. The wave equations describe one particle and do
not include particle creation or annihilation. An antiparticle
interpretation of a negative-frequency solution requires the separate
quantum-field construction and is not made here. Likewise,
\(\pv{F}\herm{\pv{F}}/2\) is the energy-density and flux paravector seen in
one frame; the complete electromagnetic energy--momentum description is the
linear function
\(\pv{T}(\pv{a})=\pv{F}\pv{a}\herm{\pv{F}}/2\) on real paravectors
\(\pv{a}\).

The displayed components may be changed by a nonlinear coordinate map or an
algebraic change of basis in flat space, but physical curvature is not
thereby created. In a curved extension, a local basis would depend on
position, a rule would be needed to compare bases at neighboring events, and
the failure of that comparison to return unchanged around a small loop would
measure curvature. Such a comparison rule is conventionally called a
connection. Field equations would also have to retain their form under the
corresponding local basis changes \cite{doran2003}. None of that additional
structure is derived here. Complex space-time, unseen-matter proposals, and
claims about the universe as a whole are also outside the established
results.

These and other uncompleted directions are recorded in the following section
without being treated as results.
