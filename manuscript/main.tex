\section{Introduction}\label{sec:orientation}
Space-time algebra extends the familiar scalar and vector calculus of three-dimensional space into a unified algebra adapted to rotations, Lorentz transformations, and relativistic field equations. In this manuscript the basic object is the paravector, a real scalar plus a real vector, and the central claim is organizational as much as technical: the same algebraic product can be carried from elementary vector identities to kinematics, electromagnetism, and relativistic wave equations without changing mathematical language from one topic to the next.

The exposition is arranged so that each layer depends only on material already established. Section \ref{sec:algebra} fixes the algebra itself; Sections \ref{sec:spacetime} and \ref{sec:transforms} reinterpret that algebra as space-time geometry and covariant motion; Section \ref{sec:em} develops the electromagnetic sector; and Section \ref{sec:qm} uses the same machinery for Klein--Gordon, Dirac, and Pauli-type equations. A compact formula compendium is deferred to Appendix \ref{app:summary}, while representation theory, verification tables, historical notes, and cautions are kept in later appendices so they do not interrupt the main line of argument.

This perspective is consistent with the modern STA and paravector literature of Hestenes, Baylis, and Doran--Lasenby \cite{hestenes1966,baylis1999,doran2003}, but the notation is kept deliberately lean. Each major section is developed in two passes: a compact forward exposition that introduces only the formulas needed for the next step, followed by a local supporting development that records intermediate identities, tables, and special cases near the formulas they support. Readers interested in the shortest route through the argument can move from one numbered section to the next and return to the supporting derivations afterward. Symbolic checks are collected in Appendix \ref{app:verification}, and bold red notes mark formulas that require qualification or correction.

Lower-case Latin letters denote real scalars unless stated otherwise, bold letters denote real three-vectors, capital letters denote paravectors or operators according to context, and complexification is carried by the pseudoscalar \(i\). Natural units \(\hbar=c=1\) and metric signature \((+,-,-,-)\) are used unless stated otherwise.

\section{Algebraic Foundations}\label{sec:algebra}\label{sec:foundations}\label{sec:functions}
The formula compendium in Appendix \ref{app:summary} is deliberately complex-first. The exposition now resets to the simplest ingredients: basis rules, the sigma-conjugate and reverse operators, vector products, paravectors, and then the elementary functional calculus built from them.

\subsection*{Basis and pseudoscalar}
The algebra starts with three generators \(\sigma_n\) for \(n\in\{1,2,3\}\). These are basis algebra elements rather than scalars, and they behave like an orthonormal spatial basis whose multiplication carries more information than an ordinary dot product. From these generators one obtains vectors, bivectors, and the pseudoscalar \(i\), which plays the role of the imaginary unit while remaining internal to the algebra.

The basis relations are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_n^2&=1,\qquad \sigma_n\sigma_m=-\sigma_m\sigma_n\quad (n\neq m),\\
i&:=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3,\qquad i^2=-1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:basis}
\end{equation}
Using $\delta_{nm}$ and $\epsilon_{nmk}$ for indices \(n,m,k\in\{1,2,3\}\),
\begin{equation}
\sigma_n\sigma_m=\delta_{nm}+\epsilon_{nmk}\,i\sigma_k.
\label{eq:sigma-product}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{The Sigma-Conjugate, Reverse, and Combined Operators}
The two basic operators are introduced here on their own because they are used repeatedly in every later section. Each is an involution, and the combined operator built from them is fixed at the same point by the direct rules
\begin{align}
(\cdot)^* &:= \eqtext{sigma conjugate; flip each }\sigma_n,
\label{eq:star-definition}\\
(\cdot)^{\mathrm R} &:= \eqtext{reverse the generator order},
\label{eq:reversion-definition}
\\
(\cdot)^{*\mathrm R} &:= \eqtext{sigma conjugate, then reverse.}
\label{eq:crev-definition}
\end{align}
Equation \eqref{eq:star-definition} defines the sigma-conjugate operator, Eq.\ \eqref{eq:reversion-definition} defines the reverse operator, and Eq.\ \eqref{eq:crev-definition} defines the combined sigma-conjugate/reverse operator. The sigma-conjugate operator \((\cdot)^*\) therefore changes the sign of each basis generator while leaving product order unchanged, the reverse operator \((\cdot)^{\mathrm R}\) leaves each \(\sigma_n\) fixed while reversing the order of generator products, and the combined operator \((\cdot)^{*\mathrm R}\) performs both actions in succession.

Because sign-flip and order-reversal are independent, the combined operator may be applied in either order. In operator form,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
{(XY)}^{*}&=X^{*}Y^{*},\qquad
{(XY)}^{\mathrm R}=Y^{\mathrm R}X^{\mathrm R},\\
X^{*\mathrm R}&:=(X^{\mathrm R})^{*}=(X^{*})^{\mathrm R}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:involution-operators}
\end{equation}
Applied to the generators and the pseudoscalar, these rules reduce to
\begin{align}
\conj{\sigma_n}=-\sigma_n,\qquad \rev{\sigma_n}=\sigma_n,\qquad \crev{\sigma_n}=-\sigma_n,
\label{eq:sigma-involutions}\\
\conj{i}=-i,\qquad \rev{i}=-i,\qquad \crev{i}=i.
\label{eq:i-involutions}
\end{align}
These agree with the standard geometric-algebra involution structure described in the STA literature \cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}; throughout this manuscript they are referred to as the sigma-conjugate operator, the reverse operator, and the combined sigma-conjugate/reverse operator. These operators will be used constantly, so it is worth emphasizing their distinct roles. The sigma-conjugate operator flips vector sign, the reverse operator reverses multiplication order, and the combined operation is the one that later produces the Minkowski-type quadratic form. Introducing them at the algebraic stage prevents the interval and operator formulas from seeming arbitrary when they appear later.
The homogeneous sigma grades and the signs acquired under these three involutions are summarized here before the manuscript moves on to products and paravectors.

\subsection*{Vector product identity and complex paravectors}
Collect the three generators into the sigma-vector
\begin{equation}
\sigv := \{ \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3 \},
\label{eq:sigma-vector}
\end{equation}
which is a vector of algebraic objects rather than a vector of scalar components. With that notation fixed, ordinary three-vectors act on the algebra by contraction against \(\sigv\).

For real vectors $\mathbf A,\mathbf B\in\R^3$,
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B+i(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:dot-cross}
\end{equation}
This real-vector product is the first structural identity because it makes the scalar/vector split transparent before any complexification is introduced. The fully complex product then follows as the master formula from which the simpler cases are recovered by restriction.

For real scalars \(a,b\in\R\) and real vectors \(\mathbf A,\mathbf B\in\R^3\), the corresponding complex scalar \(S=a+ib\in\C\), complex vector \(\mathbf V=\mathbf A+i\mathbf B\in\C^3\), real paravectors \(X,Y\in\R\oplus\R^3\), and complex paravector \(Z\in\C\oplus\C^3\) are related by
\begin{equation}
Z=(a+ib)+(\mathbf A+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv = S+\mathbf V\cdot\sigv = X+iY,
\label{eq:paravector}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
S&=a+ib\in\C,\qquad \mathbf V=\mathbf A+i\mathbf B\in\C^3,\\
X&=a+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv\in\R\oplus\R^3,\qquad
Y=b+\mathbf B\cdot\sigv\in\R\oplus\R^3.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:paravector-parts}
\end{equation}
For \(Z=S+\mathbf V\cdot\sigv\) and \(Z'=S'+\mathbf V'\cdot\sigv\) with \(S,S'\in\C\) and \(\mathbf V,\mathbf V'\in\C^3\),
\begin{equation}
ZZ'=(SS'+\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf V')+\big(S\mathbf V'+S'\mathbf V+i(\mathbf V\times\mathbf V')\big)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:paravector-product}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:paravector} and \eqref{eq:paravector-product} fix the generic complex paravector and its master multiplication rule: pure scalar, real vector, real paravector, and mixed products all follow by setting the unused parts to zero.

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
This table is archetypal rather than global. No letter is permanently tied to one type throughout the manuscript; instead, each symbol is assigned locally when it first appears in a new context. The basic ambient spaces are \(\R\), \(\C\), \(\R^3\), \(\C^3\), and the direct-sum paravector spaces \(\R\oplus\R^3\) and \(\C\oplus\C^3\).

The few objects that do not belong to one of those six spaces are signaled explicitly where they arise: the basis elements \(\sigma_n\) are algebra generators, the differential symbols \(\partial\), \(E\), \(\mathbf P\), and \(\Omega\) are operators, and the Dirac map \(W(Z)\) together with \(\I\) lives in a \(2\times2\) block-matrix representation built from paravector entries. No additional scalar fields beyond \(\R\) and \(\C\) are used.

Two central quadratic forms are
\begin{align}
\|Z\|^2 &:= \eqtext{scalar}(Z\rev{Z}) = |S|^2+\|\mathbf V\|^2,
\label{eq:euclidean-form}\\
\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2 &:= Z\crev{Z}=S^2-\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf V.
\label{eq:minkowski-form}
\end{align}
Equation \eqref{eq:minkowski-form} is the paravector form later used for space-time intervals \cite{baylis1999,doran2003}.
The distinction between the Euclidean norm \eqref{eq:euclidean-form} and the Minkowski-type form \eqref{eq:minkowski-form} is one of the main structural themes of the manuscript. The first measures the size of the full complex paravector in a positive way, while the second has the mixed sign structure needed for relativistic geometry. Much of the later physics is the disciplined choice of which quadratic form is appropriate in each setting.

\subsection*{Exponentials, projectors, and functional calculus}
Exponentials, roots, and functions of simple STA objects follow directly once Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:sigma-product} and \eqref{eq:dot-cross} are fixed.

If \(\mathbf A\in\R^3\) is written as \(\mathbf A=\theta\mathbf u\) with \(\theta=\|\mathbf A\|\), \(\mathbf u\in\R^3\), and \(\mathbf u^2=1\), define the idempotents
\begin{equation}
\Pi(\pm):=\frac{1}{2}(1\pm \mathbf u\cdot\sigv)
\label{eq:projectors}
\end{equation}
satisfying $\Pi(\pm)^2=\Pi(\pm)$, $\Pi(+)\Pi(-)=0$, $\Pi(+)+\Pi(-)=1$, and
\((\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)\Pi(\pm)=\pm\Pi(\pm)\). In projector form, the real and imaginary exponentials are
\begin{align}
\exp(\pm\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)
&=e^{\pm\theta}\Pi(+)+e^{\mp\theta}\Pi(-)\notag \\
&=\cosh \theta\pm\sinh\theta\,\mathbf u\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:exp-vector}\\
\exp(\pm i\,\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)
&=e^{\pm i\theta}\Pi(+)+e^{\mp i\theta}\Pi(-)\notag \\
&=\cos \theta\pm i\sin\theta\,\mathbf u\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:exp-bivector}
\end{align}
Equations \eqref{eq:exp-vector} and \eqref{eq:exp-bivector} underlie the later discussions of functions of $\mathbf u\cdot\sigv$, spin decomposition, and the matrix/quaternion bridge.
Geometrically, \(\Pi(+)\) and \(\Pi(-)\) isolate the parts aligned and anti-aligned with the direction \(\mathbf u\). Later these same idempotents become spin projectors, but their algebraic role is already visible here: they split an object into two sectors adapted to a chosen axis.

For a scalar-valued function \(f:\{\pm 1\}\to\C\),
\begin{equation}
f(\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)=f(+)\Pi(+)+f(-)\Pi(-).
\label{eq:functional-calculus}
\end{equation}
This gives a compact spectral calculus for logarithms, exponentials, and projector-based decompositions.
The significance of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:functional-calculus} is broader than the specific examples used later. Once the minimal polynomial of an STA element is known, functions of that element can be defined without leaving the algebraic framework already established.

\section{Space-Time Kinematics}\label{sec:spacetime}
With the algebraic foundations in place, the same paravector structure can now be read as space-time. The transition from algebra to geometry is immediate: the quadratic form becomes the interval, and normalized time-like paravectors become proper velocities.

\subsection*{Differential space-time and proper velocity}
Specializing the real paravector \(X=a+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv\in\R\oplus\R^3\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:paravector-parts} to differentials gives
\begin{equation}
\dd X=\dd t+\dd\mathbf r\cdot\sigv
\label{eq:dX}
\end{equation}
and the interval is the differential version of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:minkowski-form}:
\begin{equation}
\dd s^2=\langle\!\langle \dd X\rangle\!\rangle^2=\dd X\,\conj{\dd X}
=\dd t^2-\dd\mathbf r^2.
\label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
Here \(t,s,\gamma\in\R\), \(\mathbf r,\mathbf v,\mathbf P\in\R^3\), and \(X,U\in\R\oplus\R^3\). The scalar part \(\dd t\) is the temporal separation and the vector part \(\dd\mathbf r\cdot\sigv\) is the spatial separation. Equation \eqref{eq:interval} is therefore not an added metric axiom but the direct spacetime interpretation of the quadratic form already introduced in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:minkowski-form}.

If \(\mathbf v=\dd\mathbf r/\dd t\in\R^3\) and \(\gamma=(1-\mathbf v^2)^{-1/2}\in\R\), then
\begin{equation}
U:=\frac{\dd X}{\dd s}=\gamma(1+\mathbf v\cdot\sigv),
\qquad
\langle\!\langle U\rangle\!\rangle^2=1.
\label{eq:proper-velocity}
\end{equation}
Multiplying Eq.\ \eqref{eq:proper-velocity} by the real rest mass \(m\in\R\) gives the proper momentum formula
\begin{equation}
mU=E+\mathbf P\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
E=m\gamma,\qquad \mathbf P=m\gamma\mathbf v,
\label{eq:proper-momentum}
\end{equation}
which immediately yields $E^2-\mathbf P^2=m^2$.
The normalization \(\langle\!\langle U\rangle\!\rangle^2=1\) is the essential point: proper velocity is the unit time-like paravector tangent to the worldline. Multiplying by \(m\) then produces the energy--momentum paravector in a way that makes the relativistic mass shell relation appear immediately.

\subsection*{Proper acceleration and free motion}
The proper acceleration is the real paravector
\begin{equation}
A:=\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}=\frac{\dd^2 X}{\dd s^2},
\qquad
\eqtext{scalar}(\conj{U}A)=0.
\label{eq:proper-acceleration}
\end{equation}
The orthogonality condition in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:proper-acceleration} is the paravector analogue of the standard four-acceleration orthogonality relation. It is included here because the Lorentz-force law later in the paper should be read as a specific dynamical realization of this general kinematic structure.

A free-particle Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{free}}=-\frac{m}{\gamma},
\label{eq:free-lagrangian}
\end{equation}
whose Euler--Lagrange equations reproduce inertial motion and the usual nonrelativistic expansion
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{free}}=-m+\frac{1}{2}m\mathbf v^2+O(\mathbf v^4).
\label{eq:free-lagrangian-low}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:free-lagrangian-low} is worth displaying explicitly because it shows how the relativistic action reduces to the classical kinetic expression plus an additive rest-energy constant when \(\|\mathbf v\|\ll 1\).

\section{Rotors, Boosts, and Covariant Transformations}\label{sec:transforms}
Exponentials appear not only as algebraic functions but also as generators of transformations. After interval structure is established, rotors and boosts are the cleanest next step because they explain how the same objects move between frames before fields and wave equations are introduced.

\subsection*{General transform law, spatial rotors, and boosts}
A guiding principle of STA is that transformations should be expressed by the same algebra that describes the objects being transformed. Instead of introducing a separate matrix formalism at this stage, one uses exponentials inside the algebra itself to generate rotations and boosts.

A general transform law is
\begin{equation}
X'=T^{-1}XT.
\label{eq:general-transform}
\end{equation}
Here \(T\) denotes a generic invertible transformation element. For a real angle or rapidity \(\theta\in\R\) and a unit real vector \(\mathbf u\in\R^3\), the specific choices used throughout the remainder of the manuscript are \(R\) for spatial rotations and \(L\) for Lorentz boosts. Two important special cases are the spatial rotor
\begin{equation}
R(\theta,\mathbf u)=\exp\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\,\mathbf u\cdot i\sigv\right),
\qquad
X'=\rev{R}XR,
\label{eq:rotor}
\end{equation}
and the Lorentz boost
\begin{equation}
L(\theta,\mathbf u)=\exp\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\,\mathbf u\cdot\sigv\right),
\qquad
X'=\conj{L}XL.
\label{eq:boost}
\end{equation}
The symbols \(R\) and \(L\) are invertible algebra elements rather than vectors or paravectors, while \(T\) is reserved for the generic transform law \eqref{eq:general-transform}. Equation \eqref{eq:rotor} produces Rodrigues' rotation formula, while Eq.\ \eqref{eq:boost} produces the usual split into components parallel and perpendicular to the boost axis \cite{einstein1905,minkowski1908,hestenes1966}.
The formal similarity between Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:rotor} and \eqref{eq:boost} is deliberate. Both are exponential transformations, but bivector-like generators produce trigonometric rotation structure while vector-like generators produce hyperbolic boost structure. The contrast shows how Euclidean and Lorentzian motions coexist inside one algebraic language.

\section{Electromagnetic Sector}\label{sec:em}
With space-time and boosts in place, the next step is the first major physics payoff: the electromagnetic field, gauge structure, and Lorentz force written in compact STA form.

\subsection*{Fields, potentials, and Maxwell equations}
The derivative operator comes first because every later field equation is built from it. Writing time and space derivatives together as a single paravector operator mirrors the earlier unification of temporal and spatial coordinates in \(\dd X\), and it is this parallelism that makes the electromagnetic formulas compact rather than merely abbreviated.

With the paravector differential operator
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\partial&:=\partial_t+\sigv\cdot\gradv,\qquad
\conj{\partial}=\partial_t-\sigv\cdot\gradv,\\
\partial\conj{\partial}&=\conj{\partial}\partial=\partial_t^2-\gradv^2=: \Box,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dalembert}
\end{equation}
and with \(V,\rho,\lambda,S,q\in\R\), \(\mathbf E,\mathbf B,\mathbf A,\mathbf J\in\R^3\), the electromagnetic field and potential are written as
\begin{equation}
F=(\mathbf E+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
\Phi=V+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:F-Phi}
\end{equation}
Here \(\Phi\in\R\oplus\R^3\), while \(F\) is a purely vector complex paravector in \(\{0\}\oplus\C^3\subset\C\oplus\C^3\). Equation \eqref{eq:F-Phi} packages electric and magnetic fields into one object \(F\), and scalar and vector potentials into one object \(\Phi\). The purpose is not only notational compression: once these quantities are assembled into STA objects, differentiation and transformation act on their full content in a single step.

\subsection*{Boost transformation of the electromagnetic field}
With \(F\) now defined, the same boost element \(L\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:boost} acts on the field by
\begin{equation}
F'=\conj{L}FL.
\label{eq:field-transform}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:field-transform} is the direct field analogue of the event transform \(\conj{L}XL\). It shows that \(F\) is not an extra object appended to the mechanics, but part of the same algebraic structure: boosts act on \(F\) by the same two-sided rule that acts on space-time paravectors, and the usual parallel/perpendicular split of \(\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf B\) follows from the same boost algebra.

The identity
\begin{equation}
\partial\Phi = S+\conj{F},
\qquad
S:=\partial_t V+\gradv\cdot\mathbf A
\label{eq:potential-gradient}
\end{equation}
encodes
\begin{equation}
\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf A-\gradv V,
\qquad
\mathbf B=\gradv\times\mathbf A.
\label{eq:E-B-from-potential}
\end{equation}
The compact Maxwell equation is
\begin{equation}
\partial F=\rho-\mathbf J\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:maxwell}
\end{equation}
Splitting real/imaginary scalar/vector parts of \eqref{eq:maxwell} reproduces
\begin{align}
\gradv\cdot\mathbf E&=\rho,
&
\gradv\cdot\mathbf B&=0,
\label{eq:maxwell-scalars}\\
\gradv\times\mathbf B&=\mathbf J+\partial_t\mathbf E,
&
\gradv\times\mathbf E&=-\partial_t\mathbf B.
\label{eq:maxwell-vectors}
\end{align}
Equations \eqref{eq:maxwell-scalars} and \eqref{eq:maxwell-vectors} are standard STA/GA electrodynamics up to sign conventions fixed by \eqref{eq:F-Phi} and \eqref{eq:dalembert} \cite{hestenes1966,baylis1999,jackson1998}.
The advantage of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:maxwell} is that divergence and curl do not need to be introduced as separate primitive operators at this stage. They emerge automatically when the scalar and vector parts of the single STA equation are separated.

\subsection*{Wave equations, gauge, and Lorentz force}
Applying $\conj{\partial}$ to \eqref{eq:maxwell} gives
\begin{equation}
\Box F=\conj{\partial}(\rho-\mathbf J\cdot\sigv),
\label{eq:wave-F}
\end{equation}
whose scalar part yields charge conservation,
\begin{equation}
\partial_t\rho+\gradv\cdot\mathbf J=0.
\label{eq:charge-conservation}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:charge-conservation} is the continuity equation written in the present notation.
For a real scalar gauge function \(\lambda\in\R\),
\begin{equation}
\Phi'=\Phi+\conj{\partial}\lambda,
\qquad
F'=F.
\label{eq:gauge}
\end{equation}
This gauge transformation shows why the potential \(\Phi\) is retained even when the field \(F\) is physically primary: \(\Phi\) carries the redundancy, while \(F\) carries the gauge-invariant derivative content.

For a particle of real mass \(m\in\R\), real charge \(q\in\R\), and proper velocity \(U\in\R\oplus\R^3\), the proper-force formula is
\begin{equation}
m\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}=q\,\mathrm{real}(FU)
=q\gamma\Big(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf E+(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv\Big),
\label{eq:lorentz-force}
\end{equation}
which packages both relativistic power and the Lorentz three-force.
The real part in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:lorentz-force} extracts the physically relevant sector of the product \(FU\). The scalar term gives power transfer and the vector term gives the ordinary Lorentz force, so the standard split between energy change and momentum change is preserved inside one STA formula.

\subsection*{Lagrangian and energy-momentum remarks}
The electromagnetic particle Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=-\frac{m}{\gamma}-q(V-\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf A),
\label{eq:em-lagrangian}
\end{equation}
and its Euler--Lagrange equations return $\dd\mathbf P/\dd t=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$.
This is the standard relativistic coupling prescription: the free action from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:free-lagrangian} is supplemented by the potential term, and the field-strength form of the force law reappears after variation.

A natural paravector candidate for field energy density and flux is
\begin{equation}
T:=\frac{1}{2}F\rev{F}
=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf E^2+\mathbf B^2)+(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:energy-momentum-paravector}
\end{equation}
\flag{Editorial note: Eq.\ \eqref{eq:energy-momentum-paravector} is a useful frame-dependent energy-density/Poynting paravector, but by itself it is not the full electromagnetic stress-energy tensor. In STA language the full stress-energy object is more naturally a linear map such as $T(a)=\frac{1}{2}F a \rev{F}$ \cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}.}

\section{Relativistic Wave Equations and Spinor Dynamics}\label{sec:qm}
The relativistic wave-equation sector is a second application of the same algebra: first the scalar wave equation, then minimal coupling and factorization, and only then the Dirac block map and Pauli limit.

\subsection*{Klein--Gordon and frequency splitting}
For a complex scalar field \(\Psi\in\C\),
\begin{equation}
(\partial\conj{\partial}+m^2)\Psi=0
\label{eq:kg-free}
\end{equation}
is the free Klein--Gordon equation \cite{klein1926,gordon1926}. Define also
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Omega&:=\langle\!\langle m+\sigv\cdot\gradv\rangle\!\rangle=\sqrt{m^2-\gradv^2},\\
(\partial_t\pm i\Omega)\Psi(\pm)&=0,\qquad
\Psi=\Psi(+)+\Psi(-),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:frequency-split}
\end{equation}
which is the positive/negative-frequency factorization of \eqref{eq:kg-free}.
This factorization is useful both conceptually and technically. It separates opposite-frequency sectors of the scalar theory, and it also prepares the reader for the later factorizations in which second-order relativistic equations are rewritten as first-order systems.

\subsection*{Minimal coupling and operator factorization}
Minimal coupling is introduced here directly at the operator level. Rather than modifying each component equation separately, one defines energy and momentum operators that already incorporate the potential \(\Phi\) established in Section \ref{sec:em}.

Here \(\Psi(\pm)\in\C\), while \(\Omega\) is a scalar differential operator built from \(m\in\R\) and \(\gradv\). Define also the scalar differential operator \(E\) and vector differential operator \(\mathbf P\) by
\begin{equation}
E:=i\partial_t-qV,
\qquad
\mathbf P:=-i\gradv-q\mathbf A.
\label{eq:E-P-operators}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2)\Psi=0
\label{eq:kg-coupled}
\end{equation}
expands to the minimal-coupling Klein--Gordon operator
\begin{equation}
\Big(\Box+iqS-q^2\langle\!\langle\Phi\rangle\!\rangle^2+m^2+2iq(V\partial_t+\mathbf A\cdot\gradv)\Big)\Psi=0.
\label{eq:kg-expanded}
\end{equation}
Using the commutators in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:commutators},
\begin{equation}
[E,P_m]=iqE_m,
\qquad
[P_m,P_n]=iq(\partial_m A_n-\partial_n A_m),
\label{eq:commutators}
\end{equation}
one gets the useful factorization
\begin{equation}
(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)(E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)=E^2-\mathbf P^2-iqF.
\label{eq:EP-factorization}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:EP-factorization} is the bridge between the Klein--Gordon and Dirac-type formulas below.
The word ``bridge'' is literal here. The square of a first-order operator built from \(E\) and \(\mathbf P\) produces the second-order relativistic operator together with the field term, which is exactly the structural fact needed before introducing a Dirac-type equation.

\subsection*{Dirac block form, gauge covariance, and Pauli limit}
A useful \(2\times2\) block-operator map is
\begin{equation}
W(Z):=\{0, Z; \crev{Z}, 0\},
\qquad
W(Z)^2=\mathrm{diag}(Z\crev{Z},\crev{Z}Z).
\label{eq:W-map}
\end{equation}
Here \(Z\in\C\oplus\C^3\), and \(W(Z)\) lives in the auxiliary block-matrix representation built from paravector entries. The free and coupled Dirac operators act on a two-component block field \(\Psi=\{\Psi_1;\Psi_2\}\) in that representation:
\begin{align}
D_0&:=W(i\partial)-m\I,
\qquad D_0\Psi=0,
\label{eq:dirac-free}\\
D&:=W(i\partial-q\conj{\Phi})-m\I,
\qquad D\Psi=0.
\label{eq:dirac-coupled}
\end{align}
Multiplying \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled} by $D':=W(i\partial-q\conj{\Phi})+m\I$ yields
\begin{equation}
D'D\Psi=0
\;\Rightarrow\;
\begin{cases}
(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2-iqF^*)\Psi_1=0,\\[2pt]
(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2+iqF)\Psi_2=0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:dirac-square}
\end{equation}
The calculation in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-square} explains why the block construction is more than formal packaging. Squaring the first-order system returns Klein--Gordon-type equations with the expected field couplings, so the Dirac form is seen as a controlled factorization of the relativistic wave operator.
With the present ordering of \(Z=i\partial-q\conj{\Phi}=E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P\) inside \(W(Z)\), the upper component carries \(-iqF^*\) and the lower component carries \(+iqF\). Appendix \ref{app:verification} records a symbolic check of this sign structure.

Gauge covariance combines the electromagnetic gauge law \eqref{eq:gauge} with the block-field phase transformation
\begin{equation}
\Psi=e^{iq\lambda}\Psi',
\label{eq:dirac-gauge}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:dirac-gauge} leaves the form of \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled} unchanged.

For the low-energy reduction, using the operators from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:E-P-operators}, one obtains the leading-order Pauli equation for the same block field \(\Psi\),
\begin{equation}
2mE\Psi=(\mathbf P^2-q\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)\Psi.
\label{eq:pauli-operator}
\end{equation}
Solving Eq.\ \eqref{eq:pauli-operator} for \(i\partial_t\Psi\) gives the more familiar Schr\"odinger-type form
\begin{equation}
i\partial_t\Psi
\approx
\left(\frac{(-i\gradv-q\mathbf A)^2}{2m}+qV\right)\Psi
-\frac{q}{2m}(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)\Psi.
\label{eq:pauli}
\end{equation}
This matches the usual Zeeman term \cite{pauli1927,sakurai2017}, provided one remembers that \eqref{eq:pauli} is a leading-order $1/m$ approximation and that later specialization to uniform static $\mathbf B$ is used when decoupling spin-up and spin-down sectors.
The nonrelativistic theory therefore appears as an approximation internal to the relativistic one, not as an unrelated starting point. The Pauli equation is the leading low-energy shadow of the Dirac block system once the rest-energy scale has been separated and higher-order corrections are neglected.

If \(\mathbf B=\|\mathbf B\|\mathbf u\) with \(\mathbf B,\mathbf u\in\R^3\), \(\|\mathbf u\|=1\), and \(\mathbf B\) uniform and constant, decompose
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Psi&=\Psi(+)+\Psi(-),\qquad
\Psi(\pm)=\Pi(\pm)\Psi,\\
\Pi(\pm)&=\frac{1}{2}(1\pm \mathbf u\cdot\sigv).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pauli-spin-decomposition}
\end{equation}
Then Eq.\ \eqref{eq:pauli} separates into
\begin{equation}
i\partial_t\Psi(\pm)=\left(\frac{\mathbf P^2\mp q\|\mathbf B\|}{2m}+qV\right)\Psi(\pm).
\label{eq:pauli-spin-split}
\end{equation}
In this special case the projector language acquires an immediate physical meaning: the field direction selects a preferred axis, and the two projector sectors evolve with opposite Zeeman shifts.

\section{Representation Links and Additional Spin Structure}\label{sec:spinrep}
The main development ends with the wave-equation sector. The following appendix gathers spin bookkeeping, matrix realization, and quaternion correspondence material that clarifies those results but is not needed to follow the central progression from algebra to physics.

\subsection*{Spin projectors, ladder operators, and four sectors}
The spin projectors along a unit direction \(\mathbf u\in\R^3\) are again \eqref{eq:projectors}. About the \(z\) axis, the ladder operators are algebra elements
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
a(\pm)&:=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_1\pm i\sigma_2),\qquad
[\sigma_3,a(\pm)]=\pm 2a(\pm),\\
a(\pm)^2&=0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ladder}
\end{equation}
Using the projector family \eqref{eq:projectors}, the proper-velocity paravector \(U\in\R\oplus\R^3\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:proper-velocity}, and the block map \eqref{eq:W-map}, define for \(\epsilon,s\in\{+,-\}\)
\begin{equation}
\Lambda(\pm):=\frac{1}{2}(\I\pm W(U)),
\qquad
\Psi(\epsilon,s):=\Lambda(\epsilon)\Pi(s)\Psi.
\label{eq:energy-spin-projectors}
\end{equation}
This packages positive/negative energy and spin up/down into one algebraic splitting.
Writing the four-sector decomposition explicitly helps organize the later interpretation of states. Once energy sign and spin projection are both represented by projectors, the algebra can distinguish the sectors cleanly without introducing a separate layer of notation for each one.

With the same \(U\) and \(W\), one also has
\begin{equation}
(W(U)-\I)\Psi=0.
\label{eq:normalized-dirac}
\end{equation}
\flag{Editorial note: Eq.\ \eqref{eq:normalized-dirac} is not the general Dirac equation. It is better read as an on-shell projector relation for a free state satisfying $W(U)^2=\I$, after momentum has already been normalized by $m$.}

\subsection*{Pauli matrices and matrix realization}
Consistent with Eq.\ \eqref{eq:basis}, the matrix realization is the usual Pauli basis,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_1=\{0, 1; 1, 0\},
\quad
\sigma_2=\{0, -i; i, 0\},
\quad
\sigma_3=\{1, 0; 0, -1\},
\label{eq:pauli-matrices}
\end{equation}
Specializing the real paravector \(X=a+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:paravector-parts} to \(a=t\) and \(\mathbf A=\mathbf r\) gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
X&=t+\mathbf r\cdot\sigv
=\{ t+r_3, r_1-ir_2; r_1+ir_2, t-r_3 \},\\
\det X&=t^2-\mathbf r^2.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:matrix-X}
\end{equation}
This matrix realization is included to anchor the paravector formalism to a familiar representation. The determinant reproduces the same Minkowski quadratic form already seen in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:interval}, so the matrix picture confirms rather than replaces the intrinsic STA interpretation.

\subsection*{Quaternion correspondence}
The quaternion identification is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
q_1&=-\sigma_2\sigma_3,\qquad
q_2=-\sigma_3\sigma_1,\\
q_3&=-\sigma_1\sigma_2,\qquad
\mathbf q=-i\sigv.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:quaternion-basis}
\end{equation}
For \(a\in\R\) and \(\mathbf b\in\R^3\), let \(Q=a+\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf q = a-i\mathbf b\cdot\sigv\). Then
\begin{equation}
QQ^{\mathsf R}=a^2+\mathbf b^2,
\qquad
Q^2=a^2-\mathbf b^2-2ia\,\mathbf b\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:quaternion-square}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:quaternion-square} makes the purpose of this correspondence concrete. Quaternion algebra appears here as a restricted sector of the same STA language after multiplication by \(-i\), which makes it easier to compare quaternion rotations with the rotor formalism introduced earlier.
\flag{A typographical error sometimes appears in the intermediate expansion \(Q^2=a^2-2i(\mathbf b\cdot\sigv)+(-i)^2(\mathbf b\cdot\sigv)^2\). The linear term must carry the factor \(a\).}

\section{Historical Commentary}\label{sec:history}
The physical theories brought together here are historically layered rather than contemporaneous, so the following dates matter for interpretation. The chronology is not decorative: it clarifies that STA is a later unifying language applied to results that originally emerged in different physical settings and historical moments.
\begin{enumerate}
\item 1904: Lorentz published moving-frame transformation formulas for electromagnetism \cite{lorentz1904}.
\item 1905: Einstein recast kinematics in terms of the relativity principle and invariant light speed \cite{einstein1905}.
\item 1908: Minkowski delivered the ``Raum und Zeit'' lecture; the standard publication appeared in 1909 \cite{minkowski1908}.
\item 1926: Klein and Gordon independently established the relativistic scalar wave equation \cite{klein1926,gordon1926}.
\item 1927: Pauli introduced the nonrelativistic spin-magnetic coupling equation \cite{pauli1927}.
\item 1928: Dirac produced the relativistic spin-$\tfrac12$ equation, leading to the standard matrix formalism later reexpressed in STA language \cite{dirac1928}.
\item 1966 and after: Hestenes, Baylis, and Doran--Lasenby gave modern geometric-algebra presentations closely aligned with the spirit of this manuscript \cite{hestenes1966,baylis1999,doran2003}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Cautions and Corrections}\label{sec:flags}
This section gathers the principal formulas that require correction, reinterpretation, or stronger hypotheses than the surrounding exposition might otherwise suggest. It is intended as part of the mathematical record of the paper rather than as commentary external to it.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \flag{The displayed ``Robertson inequality'' is not the standard Robertson or Robertson--Schr\"odinger uncertainty relation.}
\item \flag{The metric diagonalization line $\dd s^2=\dd\mathbf x'\cdot\dd\mathbf x'$ drops the indefinite signature and should not be presented as a Lorentzian metric identity.}
\item \flag{The quaternion square expansion has a missing factor of $a$ in the linear term.}
\item \flag{The line $T=\frac12 F\rev{F}$ should be read as a frame-conditioned energy-density/Poynting paravector, not the full electromagnetic stress-energy tensor.}
\item \flag{The label ``normalized Dirac'' for $(W(U)-\I)\Psi=0$ is too strong; it is an on-shell projector statement, not the general Dirac equation.}
\item \flag{An earlier sign variant of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-square} with $+iqF^*$ in the upper component is inconsistent with the operator ordering in $W(i\partial-q\conj{\Phi})$; the corrected sign is $-iqF^*$.}
\item \flag{Several operator equalities stated in differential form implicitly assume smooth test functions and benign boundary behavior; those hypotheses should be stated whenever commutators of differential operators are used rigorously.}
\end{enumerate}
