\section*{Introduction}\label{sec:orientation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}
This manuscript develops a compact paravector language for several standard parts of flat-space physics. The algebra generated by three elements \(\sigma_n\) with \(\sigma_n^2=1\) and \(\sigma_n\sigma_m=-\sigma_m\sigma_n\) is the associative real Clifford algebra \(\mathrm{Cl}_{3,0}\). It is isomorphic to the Pauli matrix algebra \(M_2(\C)\) and to the even subalgebra of the space-time algebra \(\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}\). It is therefore an eight-real-dimensional realization of even space-time algebra, not the full sixteen-real-dimensional space-time algebra.

The central pseudoscalar \(i=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3\), with \(i^2=-1\), lets every algebra element be written uniquely as
\[
Z=S+\mathbf V\cdot\sigv,
\qquad S\in\C,\quad \mathbf V\in\C^3.
\]
The notation \(\C\oplus\C^3\) used below records this vector-space decomposition; multiplication is always the Pauli product, not componentwise multiplication in a direct-sum algebra. Keeping scalar/vector and real/imaginary parts visible makes the calculations short without suppressing their physical content.

Three involutions organize the presentation: the grade involution \((\cdot)^*\), reversion \((\cdot)^{\mathsf R}\), and their composition \((\cdot)^{*\mathsf R}\). They distinguish the positive coefficient norm from the complex determinant form \(Z\crev Z=S^2-\mathbf V^2\). On real event paravectors the latter restricts to the Minkowski interval. The same product calculus then gives rotations and boosts, relativistic kinematics, Maxwell theory, and Klein--Gordon, Dirac, and Pauli equations.

The physical results derived here are established results in a deliberately economical notation. The aim is not to introduce a new interaction or change the underlying physics, but to show precisely how familiar constructions share one algebraic carrier. This perspective follows the STA and paravector literature of Hestenes, Baylis, and Doran--Lasenby \cite{hestenes1966,baylis1999,doran2003}, with historical roots in Hamilton's quaternions and Clifford's geometric algebra \cite{hamilton1844,clifford1878}.

Bold symbols denote ordinary three-vectors. Natural units \(\hbar=c=1\), rationalized Heaviside--Lorentz electromagnetic units, and metric signature \((+,-,-,-)\) are used throughout. The product formula for scalar coefficients extends directly to complex-valued functions; when differential operators occur, their displayed order and the Leibniz rule are part of the definition.

\section{Algebraic Foundations}\label{sec:algebra}\label{sec:foundations}\label{sec:functions}
This section fixes the algebra before assigning physical meanings to particular elements. The product is associative and unital. All coefficients in the master product formula are central complex scalars unless an ordered differential-operator calculation is stated explicitly.

\subsection{Basis and pseudoscalar}
For \(n\in\{1,2,3\}\), let the algebra be generated over \(\R\) by three basis elements \(\sigma_n\). Their products generate scalars, vectors, bivectors, and pseudoscalars, giving the eight-real-dimensional algebra \(\mathrm{Cl}_{3,0}\cong M_2(\C)\cong\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}^{+}\).
The basis relations are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_n^2&=1,\qquad \sigma_n\sigma_m=-\sigma_m\sigma_n\quad (n\neq m),\\
i&:=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3,\qquad i^2=-1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:basis}
\end{equation}
The element \(i\) is a pseudoscalar because \(i\sigma_n=\sigma_n i\), and squaring \(i\) returns the scalar \(-1\):
\begin{align*}
(\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3)^2
&= \sigma_1\sigma_2(\sigma_3\sigma_1)\sigma_2\sigma_3,\notag\\
&= -\sigma_1(\sigma_2\sigma_1)(\sigma_3\sigma_2)\sigma_3,\notag\\
&= -\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2\sigma_3^2,\notag\\
&= -1.
\end{align*}
With the Kronecker delta and Levi-Civita symbol,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_n\sigma_m=\delta_{nm}+\sum_{k=1}^3\epsilon_{nmk}\,i\sigma_k,
\label{eq:sigma-product}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align*}
\delta_{nm} &:= \begin{cases}
1,& n=m,\\
0,& n\neq m,
\end{cases}\\[0.25em]
\epsilon_{nmk} &:= \begin{cases}
+1,& (n,m,k)\text{ even permutation of }(1,2,3),\\
-1,& (n,m,k)\text{ odd permutation of }(1,2,3),\\
0,& \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{align*}

\subsection{The Conjugate and Reverse Operators}
The two basic involutions are
\begin{align}
(\cdot)^* &= \eqtext{grade involution: flip the signs of } \sigma_n,
\label{eq:star-definition}\\
(\cdot)^{\mathrm R} &= \eqtext{reverse order of multiplied } \sigma_n.
\label{eq:reversion-definition}
\end{align}
The star is a real-linear automorphism and reversion is a real-linear anti-automorphism. Because each sends the central pseudoscalar \(i\) to \(-i\), both are antilinear with respect to the internal complex structure. Their composition is a complex-linear anti-automorphism. In operator form,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
{(XY)}^{*}&=X^{*}Y^{*},\qquad
{(XY)}^{\mathrm R}=Y^{\mathrm R}X^{\mathrm R},\\
X^{*\mathrm R}&=(X^{\mathrm R})^{*}=(X^{*})^{\mathrm R},\qquad
(X^*)^*=(X^{\mathrm R})^{\mathrm R}=X.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:operator-rules}
\end{equation}
Applied to the generators and the pseudoscalar, these rules reduce to
\begin{alignat}{3}
\conj{\sigma_n} &{}= -\sigma_n, \qquad&
\rev{\sigma_n} &{}= \sigma_n, \qquad&
\crev{\sigma_n} &{}= -\sigma_n,
\label{eq:sigma-operator-signs}\\
\conj{i} &{}= -i, \qquad&
\rev{i} &{}= -i, \qquad&
\crev{i} &{}= i.
\label{eq:i-operator-signs}
\end{alignat}
For a two-generator bivector pair with \(n\neq m\),
\begin{align}
(\sigma_n\sigma_m)^*
&= \sigma_n^*\sigma_m^*
= (-\sigma_n)(-\sigma_m)
= \sigma_n\sigma_m,\notag\\
(\sigma_n\sigma_m)^{\mathrm R}
&= \sigma_m^{\mathrm R}\sigma_n^{\mathrm R}
= \sigma_m\sigma_n
= -\sigma_n\sigma_m,\notag\\
(\sigma_n\sigma_m)^{*\mathrm R}
&= (\sigma_m\sigma_n)^*
= \sigma_m^*\sigma_n^* \notag\\
&= (-\sigma_m)(-\sigma_n)
= \sigma_m\sigma_n
= -\sigma_n\sigma_m.
\label{eq:bivector-operator-signs}
\end{align}
Equation \eqref{eq:bivector-operator-signs} records the same sign pattern on the sigma-number-two elements. These are the standard intrinsic involutions of \(\mathrm{Cl}_{3,0}\). Under the usual identification \(\sigma_k=\gamma_k\gamma_0\) with \(\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}^{+}\), the combined operation \((\cdot)^{*\mathrm R}\), rather than \((\cdot)^{\mathrm R}\) alone, corresponds to space-time reversion restricted to the even subalgebra \cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}.

The sigma number of a reduced basis product is the number of sigma generators that remain after simplification. Thus \(1\), \(\sigma_n\), \(\sigma_n\sigma_m\) with \(n\neq m\), and \(i\) have sigma numbers \(0\), \(1\), \(2\), and \(3\). Any unreduced product with sigma number larger than \(3\) simplifies to one of these four cases. Tables \ref{tab:sigma-number-components} and \ref{tab:sigma-number-signs} summarize the basis elements and operator signs by sigma number.

\subsection{Generic Complex Paravector}
Collect the three generators into the sigma-vector
\begin{equation}
\sigv := \{ \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3 \},
\label{eq:sigma-vector}
\end{equation}
which is a vector of algebraic objects rather than a vector of scalar components. Multiplication by the pseudoscalar gives the associated bivector triple
\begin{equation}
i\sigv := \{ i\sigma_1,\ i\sigma_2,\ i\sigma_3 \}
= \{ \sigma_2\sigma_3,\ \sigma_3\sigma_1,\ \sigma_1\sigma_2 \},
\label{eq:isigma-vector}
\end{equation}
because \(i=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3\) and each product \(i\sigma_n\) reduces to the complementary ordered two-generator product. Equation \eqref{eq:isigma-vector} therefore records the canonical sigma-number-two basis built from the generators.
With this basis fixed, for real scalars \(a,b\in\R\) and real vectors \(\mathbf A,\mathbf B\in\R^3\), with \(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv=A_1\sigma_1+A_2\sigma_2+A_3\sigma_3\), define the real paravectors \(X,Y\in\R\oplus\R^3\) by
\begin{equation}
X=a+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:real-paravector-x}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
Y=b+\mathbf B\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:real-paravector-y}
\end{equation}
Every algebra element can be written as a complex paravector \(Z\in\C\oplus\C^3\), either as a real paravector plus an imaginary paravector,
\begin{equation}
Z=X+iY,
\label{eq:paravector-xy}
\end{equation}
or as a complex scalar plus a complex vector component,
\begin{align}
Z&=(a+ib)+(\mathbf A+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv
\label{eq:paravector-cartesian}\\
 &=S+\mathbf V\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:paravector}
\end{align}
where the complex scalar and vector components are
\begin{equation}
S=a+ib\in\C,
\label{eq:paravector-scalar}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf V=\mathbf A+i\mathbf B\in\C^3.
\label{eq:paravector-vector}
\end{equation}
On coefficient objects, the induced actions of either basic involution are
\(
S^*=S^{\mathrm R}=a-ib
\)
and
\(
\mathbf V^*=\mathbf V^{\mathrm R}=\mathbf A-i\mathbf B
\).
When an involution acts on the full algebra term
\(\mathbf V\cdot\sigv\), its action on the sigma basis is included as well,
according to Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:operator-rules} and
\eqref{eq:sigma-operator-signs}.
Define the real, imaginary, scalar, and vector functions by
\begin{align}
\eqtext{real}(Z)&:=X=\frac{Z+\rev{Z}}{2},
\label{eq:real-function}\\
\eqtext{img}(Z)&:=Y=\frac{Z-\rev{Z}}{2i},
\label{eq:img-function}\\
\eqtext{scalar}(Z)&:=S=\frac{Z+\crev{Z}}{2},
\label{eq:scalar-function}\\
\eqtext{vector}(Z)&:=\mathbf V,
\qquad
\mathbf V\cdot\sigv=\frac{Z-\crev{Z}}{2}.
\label{eq:vector-function}
\end{align}
For the scalar part and vector part of the paravector \(Z\), we also write \(Z_{S}:=\eqtext{scalar}(Z)\) and \(Z_{\mathbf V}:=\eqtext{vector}(Z)\).

The conjugate and reverse operators act on the generic complex paravector by
\begin{align}
\conj{Z} &= (a-ib)+(-\mathbf A+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:paravector-conjugate}\\
\rev{Z} &= X-iY=(a-ib)+(\mathbf A-i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:paravector-reverse}\\
\crev{Z} &= (a+ib)-(\mathbf A+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:paravector-reverse-conjugate}
\end{align}
For the real paravector \(X\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x}, these reduce to
\begin{align}
\conj{X} &= a-\mathbf A\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:real-paravector-conjugate}\\
\rev{X} &= X=a+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv,
\label{eq:real-paravector-reverse}\\
\crev{X} &= a-\mathbf A\cdot\sigv=\conj{X}.
\label{eq:real-paravector-combined}
\end{align}

Notice in particular that coefficient conjugation is already encoded by reversion; no overbar notation is needed. Equations \eqref{eq:real-paravector-conjugate}--\eqref{eq:real-paravector-combined} give the real specialization.

\subsection{Dot products and cross products}
For real vectors \(\mathbf A,\mathbf B\in\R^3\), the dot product is defined by
\begin{align}
\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B
&:= \sum_{n=1}^3\sum_{m=1}^3\delta_{nm}A_nB_m \notag\\
&= A_1B_1+A_2B_2+A_3B_3.
\label{eq:vector-dot-norm}
\end{align}
Define the square of a vector by
\begin{equation}
\mathbf A^2:=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf A.
\label{eq:vector-square}
\end{equation}
\noindent For the cross product, the Levi-Civita form gives
\begin{equation*}
(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)_k
\,:= \sum_{n=1}^3\sum_{m=1}^3\epsilon_{nmk}A_nB_m,
\end{equation*}
which gives the vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf A\times\mathbf B=
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&A_2B_3-A_3B_2,\\
&A_3B_1-A_1B_3,\\
&A_1B_2-A_2B_1
\end{aligned}
\right\}.
\label{eq:cross-product}
\end{equation}
Dot products are commutative, whereas cross products are antisymmetric:
\begin{align*}
\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B &= +\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf A,\\
\mathbf A\times\mathbf B &= -\mathbf B\times\mathbf A.
\end{align*}
For a fixed unit vector \(\mathbf u\), define the parallel and perpendicular
component functions once for later use:
\begin{align}
\eqtext{par}(\mathbf A)&:=(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf u)\mathbf u,\notag\\
\eqtext{prp}(\mathbf A)&:=\mathbf A-\eqtext{par}(\mathbf A),
\qquad \mathbf u^2=1.
\label{eq:axis-functions}
\end{align}
With Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:basis} and \eqref{eq:sigma-product},
\begin{align*}
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)
&= \sum_{n=1}^3\sum_{m=1}^3 A_n B_m \sigma_n \sigma_m \notag\\
&= \sum_{n=1}^3 A_n B_n \notag\\
&\quad + \sum_{n<m}(A_n B_m-A_m B_n)\sigma_n \sigma_m \notag\\
&= A_1B_1+A_2B_2+A_3B_3 \notag\\
&\quad + (A_2B_3-A_3B_2)i\sigma_1 \notag\\
&\quad - (A_1B_3-A_3B_1)i\sigma_2 \notag\\
&\quad + (A_1B_2-A_2B_1)i\sigma_3.
\end{align*}
This can be efficiently expressed in terms of the dot product and cross product as
\begin{equation}
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)=\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B+i(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:dot-cross}
\end{equation}
Table \ref{tab:components-vector-product} records the same product in component form. The dot and cross products extend complex-bilinearly to \(\C^3\). Formula \eqref{eq:dot-cross} may also be used for commuting scalar-valued functions. Differential-operator coefficients need a separate ordered calculation because derivatives act on everything to their right and obey the Leibniz rule.

The reversed order flips only the antisymmetric term:
\begin{align*}
(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)
&= \mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B-i(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv,\\
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)+(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)
&= 2\,\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B,\\
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)-(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)
&= 2i(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
The same product immediately gives
\begin{align*}
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)^2 &= \mathbf A^2,\\
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)^{2n} &= (\mathbf A^2)^n,\\
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)^{2n+1} &= (\mathbf A^2)^n(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv),\\
(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)\sigv &= \mathbf A+i\,\mathbf A\times\sigv.
\end{align*}
These are the basic sigma-product identities from which the later paravector formulas are built.

\subsection{Products}
With Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dot-cross} fixing the scalar/vector split for real vectors, the fully complex product follows as the master formula from which the simpler cases are recovered by restriction.

For \(Z\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:paravector} and \(Z'=S'+\mathbf V'\cdot\sigv\in\C\oplus\C^3\) defined analogously to Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:paravector} and \eqref{eq:paravector-vector},
\begin{equation}
ZZ'=(SS'+\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf V')+\big(S\mathbf V'+S'\mathbf V+i(\mathbf V\times\mathbf V')\big)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:paravector-product}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:paravector} and \eqref{eq:paravector-product} fix the generic complex paravector and its master multiplication rule: pure scalar, real vector, real paravector, and mixed products all follow by setting the unused parts to zero.

\subsection{Norm and Determinant Form}
Two quadratic constructions will be used, and they should not be conflated: a positive coefficient norm and a generally complex determinant form.
For a complex scalar \(S=a+ib\in\C\), define the scalar norm by
\begin{equation}
|S|:=\sqrt{SS^*}.
\label{eq:scalar-norm}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
|S|^2&=SS^*\\
&=a^2+b^2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
If \(S\in\R\), then Eq.\ \eqref{eq:scalar-norm} reduces to the ordinary absolute value \(|S|=\sqrt{S^2}\).
For a complex vector \(\mathbf V=\mathbf A+i\mathbf B\in\C^3\), define the vector norm by
\begin{equation}
\|\mathbf V\|:=\sqrt{\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf V^*}.
\label{eq:vector-norm}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\|\mathbf V\|^2&=\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf V^*\\
&=\mathbf A^2+\mathbf B^2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The quadratic product \(\mathbf V^2=\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf V\) is not the same object as the positive norm square \(\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf V^*\).
For the complex paravector \(Z\) from Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:paravector}--\eqref{eq:paravector-cartesian}, define the paravector norm by
\begin{align}
\|Z\|
&:= \sqrt{|S|^2+\|\mathbf V\|^2},\label{eq:euclidean-form}\\
\|Z\|^2
&= |S|^2+\|\mathbf V\|^2\notag\\
&= a^2+b^2+\mathbf A^2+\mathbf B^2\notag\\
&= \eqtext{scalar}(Z\rev{Z})
= \eqtext{scalar}(\rev{Z}Z).\notag
\end{align}
This is a nonnegative real norm. In the Pauli matrix realization it is \(\|Z\|^2=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(ZZ^\dagger)\). The second construction is the complex determinant quadratic form
\begin{align}
\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle
&:= \sqrt{S^2-\mathbf V^2},\label{eq:minkowski-form}\\
\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2
&= S^2-\mathbf V^2\notag\\
&= (a^2-b^2-\mathbf A^2+\mathbf B^2)+2i(ab-\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B)\notag\\
&= Z\crev{Z}
= \crev{Z}Z.\notag
\end{align}
The product order does not matter because \(\crev{Z}Z=Z\crev{Z}\) is central. Under the Pauli realization this quantity is \(\det(Z)\). Its principal square root in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:minkowski-form} is branch-dependent and is not itself a globally multiplicative norm; the squared determinant form is the primary algebraic object.

For the real paravector \(X\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x}, \(X^{*\mathrm R}=X^*\), and the determinant form restricts to the Minkowski quadratic form
\begin{align}
\langle\!\langle X\rangle\!\rangle^2
&= a^2-\mathbf A^2\notag\\
&= XX^*=X^*X.\label{eq:real-paravector-metric}
\end{align}
For \(Z,Z'\in\C\oplus\C^3\), the squared determinant form is multiplicative:
\begin{align}
\langle\!\langle ZZ'\rangle\!\rangle^2
&= ZZ'(ZZ')^{*\mathrm{R}}
= ZZ'Z'^{*\mathrm{R}}Z^{*\mathrm{R}}
= Z\bigl(Z'Z'^{*\mathrm{R}}\bigr)Z^{*\mathrm{R}}\notag\\
&= Z\,\langle\!\langle Z'\rangle\!\rangle^2\,Z^{*\mathrm{R}}
= \langle\!\langle Z'\rangle\!\rangle^2\,ZZ^{*\mathrm{R}}
= \langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2\langle\!\langle Z'\rangle\!\rangle^2\notag\\
&= \langle\!\langle Z'Z\rangle\!\rangle^2.
\label{eq:metric-product}
\end{align}
Here \((ZZ')^{*\mathrm{R}}=Z'^{*\mathrm{R}}Z^{*\mathrm{R}}\), and both determinant factors are central scalars. Under pure phase multiplication, the norm and determinant form behave as
\begin{align*}
\|\exp(i\theta) Z\|^2 &= \|Z\|^2,\\
\langle\!\langle \exp(i\theta) Z\rangle\!\rangle^2
&= \exp(2i\theta)\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2.
\end{align*}
More generally, under complex scalar multiplication by \(r\exp(i\theta)\) with \(r\in\R\), the two quadratic constructions scale differently:
\begin{align}
\|r\exp(i\theta) Z\|^2 &= r^2\|Z\|^2,
\label{eq:norm-scaling}\\
\langle\!\langle r\exp(i\theta) Z\rangle\!\rangle^2
&= r^2\exp(2i\theta)\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2.
\label{eq:metric-scaling}
\end{align}
The positive norm is phase-insensitive, while the determinant form retains the phase square. The term ``Minkowski metric'' below is reserved for the real-paravector restriction \eqref{eq:real-paravector-metric} and its polarization.

\subsection{Inverse formulas}
The inverse follows from the determinant identities \eqref{eq:minkowski-form} and \eqref{eq:metric-product}. For \(Z=S+\mathbf V\cdot\sigv\), when \(S^2-\mathbf V^2\neq0\),
\begin{align}
Z^{-1}
&:= \frac{\crev{Z}}{\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2}
= \frac{\crev{Z}}{Z\crev{Z}}
= \frac{\crev{Z}}{\crev{Z}Z}\notag\\
&= \frac{S-\mathbf V\cdot\sigv}{S^2-\mathbf V^2}.
\label{eq:paravector-inverse}
\end{align}
If the determinant form vanishes, the inverse does not exist.

Differentiating the identity \(Z^{-1}Z=1\) gives
\begin{align}
\dd(Z^{-1}) &= -Z^{-1}(\dd Z)Z^{-1}.
\label{eq:paravector-inverse-diff}
\end{align}
For \(Z'=S'+\mathbf V'\cdot\sigv\in\C\oplus\C^3\), if \(\mathbf V\times\mathbf V'=\mathbf 0\), then
\begin{equation}
Z^{-1}Z'=Z'Z^{-1}.
\label{eq:inverse-commuting}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Projector Algebra}\label{sec:projectors}
Projectors are introduced next because they organize the later function formulas and ladder constructions. Once Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:sigma-product} and \eqref{eq:dot-cross} are fixed, the projector calculus gives the cleanest route from simple unit-direction elements to more general paravector decompositions.

If \(\mathbf A\in\R^3\) is written as \(\mathbf A=\theta\mathbf u\) with \(\theta=\|\mathbf A\|\), \(\mathbf u\in\R^3\), and \(\mathbf u^2=1\), define the idempotents
\begin{equation}
\Pi(\pm):=\frac{1}{2}(1\pm \mathbf u\cdot\sigv),
\label{eq:projectors}
\end{equation}
satisfying $\Pi(\pm)^2=\Pi(\pm)$, $\Pi(+)\Pi(-)=0$, $\Pi(+)+\Pi(-)=1$, and
\((\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)\Pi(\pm)=\pm\Pi(\pm)\).
Algebraically, \(\Pi(+)\) and \(\Pi(-)\) isolate the parts aligned and anti-aligned with the direction \(\mathbf u\). Their role here is purely structural: they split an object into two parts adapted to a chosen axis.

For a scalar-valued function \(f:\{\pm 1\}\to\C\),
\begin{equation}
f(\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)=f(+)\Pi(+)+f(-)\Pi(-).
\label{eq:functional-calculus}
\end{equation}
This gives a compact projector-based formula for roots, inverses, exponentials, logarithms, and related functions. Once the minimal polynomial of an element of this Pauli algebra is known, polynomial and suitably analytic functions can be reduced within the algebraic framework, with derivative data required at a defective root. The detailed formulas begin with projector and eigenvalue identities and continue to ladder operations associated with a chosen axis.

About the \(z\) axis, define the ladder elements
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\ell(\pm)&:=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_1\pm i\sigma_2),\qquad
\com{\sigma_3}{\ell(\pm)}=\pm 2\ell(\pm),\\
\ell(\pm)^2&=0,\qquad
\ell(+)\ell(-)=\Pi(+),\qquad \ell(-)\ell(+)=\Pi(-).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ladder}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:ladder} records the nilpotent combinations of \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\) that exchange the two projector parts selected by Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projectors}. The projector eigenvalue formulas, mixed projector products, and ladder identities built from Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:projectors}, \eqref{eq:functional-calculus}, and \eqref{eq:ladder} are collected next.

\section{Space-Time Kinematics}\label{sec:spacetime}
On real event paravectors, the determinant form becomes the Minkowski quadratic form and normalized timelike paravectors become four-velocities. This is standard special-relativistic kinematics in paravector form \cite{lorentz1904,einstein1905,minkowski1908,hestenes1966,baylis1999}.

\subsection{Differential space-time and four-velocity}
Specializing the real paravector \(X\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x} to differentials gives
\begin{equation}
\dd X=\dd t+\dd\mathbf r\cdot\sigv
\label{eq:dX}
\end{equation}
and the interval is the differential version of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:minkowski-form}:
\begin{equation}
\dd s^2=\langle\!\langle \dd X\rangle\!\rangle^2=\langle\!\langle \dd t+\sigv\cdot\dd\mathbf r\rangle\!\rangle^2=\dd X\,\conj{\dd X}
=\dd t^2-\dd\mathbf r^2.
\label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
Here \(t,s,\gamma\in\R\), \(\mathbf r,\mathbf v,\mathbf P\in\R^3\), and \(X,U\in\R\oplus\R^3\). Equation \eqref{eq:interval} is the real-event restriction of the determinant form in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:minkowski-form}. For a future-directed timelike worldline, \(\dd t>0\) and \(\dd s=\sqrt{\dd t^2-\dd\mathbf r^2}>0\).

If \(\mathbf v=\dd\mathbf r/\dd t\in\R^3\) and \(\gamma=(1-\mathbf v^2)^{-1/2}\in\R\), then
\begin{equation}
U:=\frac{\dd X}{\dd s}=\gamma(1+\mathbf v\cdot\sigv),
\qquad
\langle\!\langle U\rangle\!\rangle^2=1.
\label{eq:proper-velocity}
\end{equation}
Multiplying Eq.\ \eqref{eq:proper-velocity} by the real rest mass \(m>0\) gives the four-momentum
\begin{equation}
mU=E+\mathbf P\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
E=m\gamma,\qquad \mathbf P=m\gamma\mathbf v,
\label{eq:proper-momentum}
\end{equation}
which immediately yields $E^2-\mathbf P^2=m^2$.
The normalized tangent \(U\) is conventionally called the four-velocity; ``proper velocity'' often denotes only its spatial part \(\gamma\mathbf v\). Multiplication by \(m>0\) gives the four-momentum of a massive particle. For a massless particle \(\dd s=0\), so \(U\) is undefined and a null momentum paravector must instead be introduced directly.

\subsection{Four-acceleration and free motion}
The four-acceleration is the real paravector
\begin{equation}
A:=\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}=\frac{\dd^2 X}{\dd s^2},
\qquad
\eqtext{scalar}(\conj{U}A)=0.
\label{eq:proper-acceleration}
\end{equation}
The orthogonality condition in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:proper-acceleration} is the standard four-acceleration orthogonality relation. The invariant proper acceleration is the magnitude \(\sqrt{-A\conj A}\), not the paravector \(A\) itself.

A free-particle Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{free}}=-\frac{m}{\gamma},
\label{eq:free-lagrangian}
\end{equation}
whose Euler--Lagrange equations reproduce inertial motion and the usual nonrelativistic expansion
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{free}}=-m+\frac{1}{2}m\mathbf v^2+O(\mathbf v^4).
\label{eq:free-lagrangian-low}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:free-lagrangian-low} is worth displaying explicitly because it shows how the relativistic action reduces to the classical kinetic expression plus an additive rest-energy constant when \(\|\mathbf v\|\ll 1\).

\section{Differential Operators}\label{sec:differential}
The electromagnetic and wave-equation sections use one fixed set of differential conventions. They are collected here once so the later sections can refer back to the same numbered equations instead of redefining the operators in multiple places.

For spatial derivatives with respect to \(\mathbf r=(r_1,r_2,r_3)\), define
\begin{equation}
\delr:=\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial r_1},\frac{\partial}{\partial r_2},\frac{\partial}{\partial r_3}\right\}.
\label{eq:delr}
\end{equation}
The gradient notation is then
\begin{equation}
\gradv:=\delr
=\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial r_1},\frac{\partial}{\partial r_2},\frac{\partial}{\partial r_3}\right\}
=\{\partial_1,\partial_2,\partial_3\}.
\label{eq:gradient-notation}
\end{equation}
For shorthand, the coordinate symbol \(\mathbf r\) is dropped in the last form, so \(\partial_i:=\partial/\partial r_i\) for \(i\in\{1,2,3\}\). The corresponding spatial Laplacian is
\begin{equation}
\gradv^2:=\partial_1^2+\partial_2^2+\partial_3^2.
\label{eq:laplacian}
\end{equation}
The STA differential paravector and its conjugate are
\begin{equation}
\partial:=\partial_t+\sigv\cdot\gradv
=\partial_t+\sigma_1\partial_1+\sigma_2\partial_2+\sigma_3\partial_3.
\label{eq:partial-operator}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\conj{\partial}:=\partial_t-\sigv\cdot\gradv
=\partial_t-\sigma_1\partial_1-\sigma_2\partial_2-\sigma_3\partial_3.
\label{eq:partial-conjugate}
\end{equation}
Because the coordinate derivatives commute on smooth fields, their ordered products give the scalar d'Alembertian
\begin{align}
\Box&:=\partial\conj{\partial}
\label{eq:dalembertian-norm}\\
&=\conj{\partial}\partial\notag\\
&=\partial_t^2-\gradv^2.
\label{eq:dalembertian}
\end{align}

\section{Electromagnetic Fields}\label{sec:em}
Electromagnetic fields, gauge structure, and the Lorentz force admit compact paravector forms. In physical content this is Maxwell theory in rationalized Heaviside--Lorentz units \cite{maxwell1865}; the packaging follows later STA presentations \cite{hestenes1966,baylis1999,doran2003}.

\subsection{Fields, potentials, and Maxwell equations}
The differential conventions are fixed once in Section \ref{sec:differential}; in particular, Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:delr}--\eqref{eq:dalembertian} define the spatial derivative vector, the shorthand \(\partial_i\), the paravector derivative, its conjugate, and the d'Alembertian. With those operators fixed, and with \(V,\rho,\lambda,S,q\in\R\), \(\mathbf E,\mathbf B,\mathbf A,\mathbf J\in\R^3\), the electromagnetic field vector and potential are written as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf F:=\mathbf E+i\mathbf B,
\qquad
F:=\mathbf F\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
\Phi=V+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:F-Phi}
\end{equation}
Here \(\Phi\in\R\oplus\R^3\), \(\mathbf F\in\C^3\), and \(F\in\{0\}\oplus\C^3\) is a pure-vector complex paravector. Equation \eqref{eq:F-Phi} packages electric and magnetic fields into \(F\), and scalar and vector potentials into \(\Phi\).

The identity
\begin{equation}
\partial\Phi = S+\conj{F},
\qquad
S:=\partial_t V+\gradv\cdot\mathbf A
\label{eq:potential-gradient}
\end{equation}
encodes
\begin{equation}
\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf A-\gradv V,
\qquad
\mathbf B=\gradv\times\mathbf A.
\label{eq:E-B-from-potential}
\end{equation}
The compact Maxwell equation is
\begin{equation}
\partial F=\rho-\mathbf J\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:maxwell}
\end{equation}
Splitting real/imaginary scalar/vector parts of \eqref{eq:maxwell} reproduces
\begin{align}
\gradv\cdot\mathbf E&=\rho,
&
\gradv\cdot\mathbf B&=0,
\label{eq:maxwell-scalars}\\
\gradv\times\mathbf B&=\mathbf J+\partial_t\mathbf E,
&
\gradv\times\mathbf E&=-\partial_t\mathbf B.
\label{eq:maxwell-vectors}
\end{align}
Equations \eqref{eq:maxwell-scalars} and \eqref{eq:maxwell-vectors} are standard STA/GA electrodynamics up to sign conventions fixed by Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:partial-operator}, \eqref{eq:partial-conjugate}, \eqref{eq:dalembertian}, and \eqref{eq:F-Phi}; component by component they recover Maxwell's field equations in the present signature and field convention \cite{maxwell1865,hestenes1966,baylis1999,jackson1998}.
Equation \eqref{eq:maxwell} avoids taking divergence and curl as separate primitive operators at this stage. They emerge automatically when the scalar and vector parts of the single STA equation are separated.

\subsection{Boost transformation of electromagnetic fields}
The same boost element \(L\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:boost} acts on the field paravector by
\begin{equation}
F'=\conj{L}FL.
\label{eq:field-transform}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:field-transform} is deliberately different from the event congruence \(X'=\conj L X\conj L\). Events carry the Minkowski-vector (real-paravector) representation, whereas \(F\) carries the STA-bivector/biparavector representation; the resulting similarity law gives the usual parallel/perpendicular mixing of \(\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf B\).

\subsection{Wave equations, gauge, and Lorentz force}
Applying $\conj{\partial}$ to \eqref{eq:maxwell} gives
\begin{equation}
\Box F=\conj{\partial}(\rho-\mathbf J\cdot\sigv),
\label{eq:wave-F}
\end{equation}
whose scalar part yields charge conservation,
\begin{equation}
\partial_t\rho+\gradv\cdot\mathbf J=0.
\label{eq:charge-conservation}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:charge-conservation} is the continuity equation written in the present notation.
For a real scalar gauge function \(\lambda\in\R\), write the transformed quantities with a subscript to avoid confusing this gauge change with a Lorentz-frame prime:
\begin{equation}
\Phi_\lambda=\Phi+\conj{\partial}\lambda,
\qquad
F_\lambda=F.
\label{eq:gauge}
\end{equation}
This gauge transformation shows why the potential \(\Phi\) is retained even when the field is physically primary: \(\Phi\) carries the redundancy, while \(F\) carries the gauge-invariant derivative content.

For a particle of real mass \(m>0\), real charge \(q\), and four-velocity \(U\), the four-force formula is
\begin{equation}
m\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}=q\,\eqtext{real}\bigl(FU\bigr)
=q\gamma\Big(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf E+(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv\Big),
\label{eq:lorentz-force}
\end{equation}
which packages both relativistic power and the Lorentz three-force. Since \(\dd/\dd s=\gamma\,\dd/\dd t\), division by \(\gamma\) gives \(\dd E/\dd t=q\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf E\) and \(\dd\mathbf P/\dd t=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\).

\subsection{Lagrangian and energy-momentum remarks}
The electromagnetic particle Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=-\frac{m}{\gamma}-q(V-\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf A),
\label{eq:em-lagrangian}
\end{equation}
and its Euler--Lagrange equations return $\dd\mathbf P/\dd t=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$.
Here the free action from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:free-lagrangian} is supplemented by the potential term, and the field-strength form of the force law reappears after variation.

The frame-dependent field energy-density and flux paravector is
\begin{equation}
T:=\frac{1}{2}F\rev{F}
=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf E^2+\mathbf B^2)+(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:energy-momentum-paravector}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:energy-momentum-paravector} packages the energy density and Poynting vector into one frame-dependent paravector. The supporting subsection below records the precise caveat and the corresponding covariant stress-energy map.

\section{Relativistic Wave Equations and Spin Dynamics}\label{sec:qm}
The same ordered product calculus gives the standard relativistic wave equations. This section separates three logically different carriers: a complex scalar for Klein--Gordon theory, one fixed minimal ideal for a Dirac field, and the surviving two-component field in the Pauli limit. Keeping those distinctions explicit prevents algebraic degrees of freedom from being mistaken for physical spinor components \cite{klein1926,gordon1926,dirac1928,pauli1927}.

\subsection{Klein--Gordon field and frequency splitting}
For a complex scalar field \(\psi\in\C\), the free Klein--Gordon equation is
\begin{equation}
(\partial\conj{\partial}+m^2)\psi=(\Box+m^2)\psi=0.
\label{eq:kg-free}
\end{equation}
On a suitable spatial domain, let
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Omega&:=\sqrt{m^2-\gradv^2},\\
(\partial_t+i\Omega)\psi(+)&=0,\qquad
(\partial_t-i\Omega)\psi(-)=0,\\
\psi&=\psi(+)+\psi(-).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:frequency-split}
\end{equation}
Here \(\Omega\) is the positive self-adjoint Fourier multiplier with symbol
\(\sqrt{m^2+\mathbf k^2}\). Thus
\[
\psi(\pm,t)=\exp\!\bigl(\mp i\Omega(t-t_0)\bigr)\psi(\pm,t_0).
\]
When \(\Omega^{-1}\) exists on the chosen data, the two branches are
\[
\psi(\pm)=\frac12\bigl(\psi\pm i\Omega^{-1}\partial_t\psi\bigr).
\]
A massless zero mode is treated separately. The scalar \(|\psi|^2\) is not the relativistic Klein--Gordon probability density; the conserved current contains derivatives and is not positive on the full positive/negative-frequency solution space.
The sign in \(\psi(\pm)\) labels frequency in this subsection; it is unrelated to the spin-axis projection \(\Pi(\pm)\psi\) used later.

\subsection{Minimal coupling and ordered factorization}
With the real potential \(\Phi=V+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv\), define
\begin{equation}
E:=i\partial_t-qV,
\qquad
\mathbf P:=-i\gradv-q\mathbf A.
\label{eq:E-P-operators}
\end{equation}
The minimally coupled scalar equation is
\begin{equation}
(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2)\psi=0,
\label{eq:kg-coupled}
\end{equation}
or, after applying the Leibniz rule,
\begin{equation}
\Bigl(\Box+iqS-q^2\langle\!\langle\Phi\rangle\!\rangle^2+m^2
 +2iq(V\partial_t+\mathbf A\cdot\gradv)\Bigr)\psi=0.
\label{eq:kg-expanded}
\end{equation}
For smooth fields,
\begin{align}
\com{E}{P_m}&=iqE_m,\notag\\
\com{P_m}{P_n}&=iq(\partial_mA_n-\partial_nA_m).
\label{eq:commutators}
\end{align}
Consequently the written order matters:
\begin{align}
(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)(E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)
 &=E^2-\mathbf P^2-iqF,
\label{eq:EP-factorization}\\
(E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)
 &=E^2-\mathbf P^2-iq\conj F.
\label{eq:EP-factorization-conjugate}
\end{align}
These are ordered reverse-conjugate products of differential operators, not applications of the scalar-coefficient determinant formula.

\subsection{One Dirac field}
In the Pauli realization a paravector is an arbitrary \(2\times2\) complex matrix. Left multiplication acts independently on its two columns. Therefore two unrestricted paravector blocks describe two degenerate Dirac fields, not one. A single Dirac field is obtained by selecting one matrix column. Intrinsically, fix
\begin{equation*}
\Pi_0:=\frac12(1+\sigma_3),
\end{equation*}
and require
\begin{equation}
\bPsi_n=\bPsi_n\Pi_0,\qquad n=1,2.
\label{eq:minimal-ideal}
\end{equation}
The carrier \(\mathrm{Cl}_{3,0}\Pi_0\) is a minimal left ideal. Every operator below acts from the left, so the restriction is preserved. The fixed right factor \(\Pi_0\) selects a column; it is not a measurement of spin along the third axis. Equivalently, in the Pauli matrix realization each \(\bPsi_n\) is an ordinary two-component Weyl column and \(\bPsi=\{\bPsi_1;\bPsi_2\}\) has the usual four complex components.

Using the block map \(\Wmat\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:W-map}, define
\begin{align}
\Dmat_0&:=\Wmat(i\partial)-m\I,
&\Dmat_0\bPsi&=0,
\label{eq:dirac-free}\\
\Dmat&:=\Wmat(i\partial-q\conj\Phi)-m\I,
&\Dmat\bPsi&=0.
\label{eq:dirac-coupled}
\end{align}
The coupled system is equivalently
\begin{equation}
(E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)\bPsi_2=m\bPsi_1,\qquad
(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)\bPsi_1=m\bPsi_2.
\label{eq:dirac-weyl}
\end{equation}
Multiplication by \(\Dmat'=\Wmat(i\partial-q\conj\Phi)+m\I\) and use of the two ordered factorizations gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2+iq\conj F)\bPsi_1=0,\\[2pt]
(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2+iqF)\bPsi_2=0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:dirac-square}
\end{equation}
Thus the first-order system squares to the expected Klein--Gordon operator plus the electromagnetic spin coupling.

Under the gauge law \eqref{eq:gauge},
\begin{equation}
\bPsi_\lambda=\exp(-iq\lambda)\bPsi,
\label{eq:dirac-gauge}
\end{equation}
and the form of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled} is unchanged.

The reverse product supplies a positive time-slice norm, but its vector part is not a probability current. With the fixed-ideal convention above, the Dirac density and current may be written without component indices as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\rho_D&=2\,\eqtext{scalar}\!\left(
 \rev{\bPsi_1}\bPsi_1+\rev{\bPsi_2}\bPsi_2\right),\\
\mathbf j_D&=2\,\eqtext{vector}\!\left(
 \bPsi_2\rev{\bPsi_2}-\bPsi_1\rev{\bPsi_1}\right),
\end{aligned}
\qquad
\partial_t\rho_D+\gradv\cdot\mathbf j_D=0.
\label{eq:dirac-current}
\end{equation}
The normalization condition is the spatial integral
\(\int\rho_D\,\dd^3\mathbf r=1\), not a pointwise equation. In the Pauli column realization these are the familiar Weyl-basis Dirac bilinears.

\subsection{Pauli limit}
A chiral block by itself is not the nonrelativistic large component. First remove the rest phase,
\(\bPsi_n=\exp(-imt)\widetilde\bPsi_n\), and define the energy-basis combinations
\begin{equation}
\phi:=\frac{\widetilde\bPsi_1+\widetilde\bPsi_2}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
\chi:=\frac{\widetilde\bPsi_2-\widetilde\bPsi_1}{\sqrt2}.
\label{eq:large-small}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:dirac-weyl} then becomes exactly
\begin{equation}
E\phi=(\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)\chi,\qquad
(E+2m)\chi=(\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)\phi.
\label{eq:large-small-coupled}
\end{equation}
At energies small compared with \(m\),
\[
\chi=\frac{\mathbf P\cdot\sigv}{2m}\phi+O(m^{-2}).
\]
Using
\[
(\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)^2
=\mathbf P^2-q\,\mathbf B\cdot\sigv,
\]
one obtains
\begin{equation}
E\phi=\frac{\mathbf P^2-q\,\mathbf B\cdot\sigv}{2m}\,\phi+O(m^{-2}).
\label{eq:pauli-operator}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
i\partial_t\phi
=
\left[\frac{(-i\gradv-q\mathbf A)^2}{2m}+qV
-\frac{q}{2m}\mathbf B\cdot\sigv\right]\phi
+O(m^{-2}).
\label{eq:pauli}
\end{equation}
Electric spin--orbit and Darwin terms enter at the next order; no electric term has been discarded while retaining another term of the same order.

If \(\mathbf B\ne0\) is uniform and constant, set
\(\mathbf u=\mathbf B/\|\mathbf B\|\) and
\begin{equation}
\phi=\phi(+)+\phi(-),\qquad
\phi(\pm)=\Pi(\pm)\phi.
\label{eq:pauli-spin-decomposition}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
i\partial_t\phi(\pm)
=\left(\frac{\mathbf P^2\mp q\|\mathbf B\|}{2m}+qV\right)\phi(\pm).
\label{eq:pauli-spin-split}
\end{equation}
For a varying axis the derivatives of \(\Pi(\pm)\) and its commutator with the Hamiltonian couple the two projected fields, so this decoupling no longer holds.

\subsection{Free energy and helicity projectors}
For a free momentum mode, Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-weyl} is the Hamiltonian system
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
i\partial_t\bPsi&=H_D\bPsi,\\
H_D&:=
\begin{pmatrix}
-\sigv\cdot\mathbf p&m\\
m&\sigv\cdot\mathbf p
\end{pmatrix},\\
H_D^2&=E_{\mathbf p}^2\I,
\qquad E_{\mathbf p}:=\sqrt{m^2+\mathbf p^2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dirac-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
Its energy projectors are
\[
\Lmat(\epsilon):=\frac12\left(\I+\epsilon\frac{H_D}{E_{\mathbf p}}\right),
\qquad \epsilon\in\{+1,-1\}.
\]
For \(\mathbf p\ne0\), choose the helicity axis
\(\mathbf u=\mathbf p/\|\mathbf p\|\) and let
\[
\Pi_h(s):=
\begin{pmatrix}\Pi(s)&0\\0&\Pi(s)\end{pmatrix},
\qquad s\in\{+1,-1\}.
\]
The two projector families commute, so
\begin{equation}
\bPsi(\epsilon,s):=\Lmat(\epsilon)\Pi_h(s)\bPsi,
\qquad
\bPsi=\sum_{\epsilon=\pm1}\sum_{s=\pm1}\bPsi(\epsilon,s).
\label{eq:energy-spin-projectors}
\end{equation}
At rest any spin axis may be chosen. Away from rest, an arbitrary fixed axis need not commute with the energy projector; helicity is the notation-light simultaneous choice. Negative-frequency classical solutions are not identified with antiparticles until the field is quantized.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}\label{sec:synthesis}
The algebra used here is the real Pauli algebra \(\mathrm{Cl}_{3,0}\), written as one complex scalar plus one complex three-vector. Its economy comes from a structural fact rather than a new physical postulate: the central pseudoscalar provides the complex unit, the Pauli product combines dot and cross products, and the reverse-conjugate product is the \(2\times2\) determinant. On real event paravectors that determinant is the Minkowski interval.

Within this framework the manuscript derives, with one notation, spatial rotations, Lorentz boosts, four-velocity and four-momentum, Maxwell's equations, the Lorentz force, scalar relativistic waves, the Dirac factorization, and the leading Pauli Hamiltonian. The distinctions between representations are essential. Events transform by Lorentz congruence, the electromagnetic STA-bivector/biparavector carried by \(F\) transforms by similarity, and one Dirac field occupies a fixed minimal left ideal (one Pauli-matrix column). Once these distinctions are enforced, the compact notation agrees with the standard component theories.

The reformulation also makes several complementary structures easy to see. The real and imaginary parts of \(\partial F\) yield the four Maxwell equations; the two ordered momentum products yield the conjugate spin couplings \(F\) and \(F^*\); and the same axis projectors organize elementary functions, Pauli spin sectors, and free helicity sectors. These are connections among established equations, not evidence for an additional interaction.

\subsection{Scope and outlook}
The results in this paper assume flat Minkowski space, smooth fields, and domains on which the displayed differential operators and square roots are defined. The electromagnetic discussion is classical, and the wave-equation discussion is first-quantized. A negative-frequency solution becomes an antiparticle statement only after second quantization. Likewise, \(F\rev F/2\) is the energy-density/Poynting paravector seen in one frame; the full electromagnetic stress-energy object is the linear map \(a\mapsto \tfrac12Fa\rev F\) on real paravectors \(a\).

A nonlinear coordinate map or algebraic change of basis in flat space changes the displayed components but does not by itself create curvature. A genuine extension to general relativity would require additional geometric structure---a position-dependent frame, a compatible connection, curvature, and covariant field equations. Complex space-time, dark-sector, and cosmological claims are outside what has been derived here.

Natural next steps are narrower and testable: a systematic treatment of operator domains and boundary conditions; a local-frame extension with independent curvature; constitutive maps for media; and a second-quantized construction in which the minimal-ideal carrier, charge conjugation, and multiparticle state space are specified explicitly.
