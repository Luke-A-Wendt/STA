\subsubsection{Products of real scalars, vectors, and paravectors}
Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be the real paravectors from Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x} and \eqref{eq:real-paravector-y}. Using Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dot-cross},
\begin{align*}
XY
&= (a+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)(b+\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)\notag\\
&= (ab+\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B)
+ \bigl(a\mathbf B+b\mathbf A+i(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\bigr)\cdot\sigv,\\
YX
&= (ab+\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B)
+ \bigl(a\mathbf B+b\mathbf A-i(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\bigr)\cdot\sigv \notag\\
&= (XY)^{\mathrm R}.
\end{align*}
Table \ref{tab:components-real-paravector-product} gives the same product in component form. The scalar part is symmetric, while the imaginary-vector part changes sign with the order.

Two immediate consequences are
\begin{align*}
\com{X}{Y} &= 2i(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv,\\
XY\in\R\oplus\R^3 &\iff \mathbf A\times\mathbf B=0.
\end{align*}
So two real paravectors commute exactly when their vector parts are parallel.

Setting \(Y=X\) removes the antisymmetric term automatically:
\begin{align*}
X^2
&= a^2+\mathbf A^2+2a\,\mathbf A\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
If \(X^2=b+\mathbf B\cdot\sigv\), then matching scalar and vector parts gives
\begin{align*}
b &= a^2+\mathbf A^2,\\
\mathbf B &= 2a\,\mathbf A,
\end{align*}
hence
\begin{align*}
a^2 &= \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(b\pm\sqrt{b^2-\mathbf B^2}\Bigr),\\
\mathbf A^2 &= b-a^2.
\end{align*}
These equations are necessary, but the direction equation
\(\mathbf B=2a\mathbf A\) must also be retained. When
\(\mathbf B\neq\mathbf 0\), real paravector roots exist precisely when
\(b\geq\lVert\mathbf B\rVert\). For either choice of the inner sign, choose
one of the two signs of the resulting nonzero \(a\) and set
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf A=\frac{\mathbf B}{2a}.
\end{equation*}
When \(\mathbf B=\mathbf0\), a real root requires \(b\geq0\); the scalar
roots are \(X=\pm\sqrt b\), and the degenerate pure-vector roots satisfy
\(a=0\) and \(\mathbf A^2=b\). Thus the displayed quadratic relation is a
branch condition, rather than by itself a complete square-root formula.

\subsubsection{Products of complex paravectors}
The master product law \eqref{eq:paravector-product} is the complex extension of the real formulas above. Writing \(Z\) as in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:paravector}--\eqref{eq:paravector-cartesian} and \(Z'=X'+iY'=S'+\mathbf V'\cdot\sigv\), one obtains every later specialization by setting the unused scalar or vector parts to zero. Here \(S,S'\) and the components of \(\mathbf V,\mathbf V'\) are central complex scalars. This hypothesis permits scalar coefficients to pass through one another and through the sigma generators. Table \ref{tab:components-paravector-product} gives the full component decomposition of \(ZZ'\), Table \ref{tab:components-paravector-square} gives \(Z^2\), and Tables \ref{tab:components-zzstar}--\ref{tab:components-zcrevz} record the order-sensitive products built from the conjugate and reverse operators.

Within this finite-dimensional scalar-coefficient algebra, the scalar part is cyclic:
\begin{equation*}
\eqtext{scalar}(ZZ')=\eqtext{scalar}(Z'Z).
\end{equation*}
In particular, if \(Z'=MZ^{*\mathrm R}\), then
\begin{align*}
\eqtext{scalar}(Z M Z^{*\mathrm R})
&=\eqtext{scalar}(M Z^{*\mathrm R} Z)\notag\\
&=\eqtext{scalar}(M)\,(Z^{*\mathrm R} Z),
\end{align*}
because \(Z^{*\mathrm R} Z\) is scalar.

The order-sensitive variants fall into three algebraic types:
\begin{align*}
ZZ^* &\eqtext{ is a real scalar plus a complex vector},\\
ZZ^{\mathrm R} &\eqtext{ is a real scalar plus a real vector},\\
ZZ^{\mathrm{R}*} &\eqtext{ is a complex scalar}.
\end{align*}
Those three cases are precisely the ones needed later for positive norms, determinant forms, inverses, and transformation formulas.

If a component is instead a differential or another noncommuting operator,
the ordered expansion must be used before any simplification: in general
\(\mathbf V S'\neq S'\mathbf V\), Leibniz terms may occur, and cyclicity of
the algebraic scalar projection alone does not justify cycling the operator
coefficients. The operator applications later in the manuscript therefore
require their displayed ordering and are not automatic substitutions into
the central-coefficient formulas of this subsection.

\subsubsection{Order dependence and commutators}
For arbitrary algebra elements \(A\) and \(B\), define the order functions
\begin{equation}
\com{A}{B}:=AB-BA,
\qquad
\acom{A}{B}:=AB+BA.
\label{eq:order-functions}
\end{equation}
The part of a product that depends on order is its commutator function. For generic complex paravectors \(Z,Z'\in\C\oplus\C^3\),
\begin{equation}
\com{Z}{Z'}
= 2i(\mathbf V\times\mathbf V')\cdot\sigv
\label{eq:paravector-commutator}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:paravector-commutator} is the compact commutator law. In real and imaginary vector components it expands to
\begin{align*}
\com{Z}{Z'}
&= -2(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B'+\mathbf B\times\mathbf A')\cdot\sigv \notag\\
&\quad +2i(\mathbf A\times\mathbf A'-\mathbf B\times\mathbf B')\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
The real-paravector commutator
\begin{equation*}
\com{X}{Y}=2i(\mathbf A\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv
\end{equation*}
is the corresponding specialization. In every case the scalar terms cancel, so the commutator isolates the cross-product part of the multiplication law.

The symmetric companion is the anticommutator. For pure vectors,
\begin{align}
\acom{\mathbf A\cdot\sigv}{\mathbf B\cdot\sigv}
&=(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)\notag\\
&\quad+(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)\notag\\
&=2\,\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B.
\label{eq:vector-anticommutator}
\end{align}
So \(\acom{\mathord\cdot}{\mathord\cdot}\) isolates the symmetric
dot-product part, just as \(\com{\mathord\cdot}{\mathord\cdot}\) isolates
the antisymmetric cross-product part.

More generally, for generic complex paravectors \(Z,Z'\in\C\oplus\C^3\), define
\begin{equation}
\acom{Z}{Z'}=ZZ'+Z'Z.
\label{eq:paravector-anticommutator-definition}
\end{equation}
Using Eq.\ \eqref{eq:paravector-product}, one finds
\begin{equation}
\acom{Z}{Z'}
=2\bigl(S S'+\mathbf V\cdot\mathbf V'\bigr)
+2\bigl(S\mathbf V'+S'\mathbf V\bigr)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:paravector-anticommutator}
\end{equation}
The cross-product contribution to the anticommutator cancels identically.
The result keeps only the symmetric scalar and vector couplings.

The same order dependence appears already in the sigma basis itself. The pseudoscalar commutes with the generators,
\begin{align*}
\com{i}{\sigma_n} &= 0,\\
i\sigv &= \sigv i = \{\sigma_2\sigma_3,\ \sigma_3\sigma_1,\ \sigma_1\sigma_2\},
\end{align*}
while distinct sigma generators satisfy
\begin{equation*}
\com{\sigma_n}{\sigma_m}=2\sigma_n\sigma_m=2\sum_{k=1}^3\epsilon_{nmk}i\sigma_k
\qquad (n\neq m).
\end{equation*}
Thus the oriented sigma-number-two basis is exactly the commutator closure of the sigma-number-one generators.

Tables \ref{tab:components-zzrev}--\ref{tab:components-zcrevz} display the same product types in full component form. Their positive-norm and determinant-form consequences are already fixed by Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:euclidean-form}, \eqref{eq:real-paravector-metric}, and \eqref{eq:minkowski-form}, so no new quadratic definitions are introduced here.
