\subsubsection{Order functions}
For arbitrary paravectors \(\pv{A},\pv{B}\in\Pspace\), the commutator
function is defined by
\begin{equation}
\com{\pv{A}}{\pv{B}}:=\pv{A}\pv{B}-\pv{B}\pv{A}.
\label{eq:com-function}
\end{equation}
The anticommutator function is defined independently by
\begin{equation}
\acom{\pv{A}}{\pv{B}}:=\pv{A}\pv{B}+\pv{B}\pv{A}.
\label{eq:acom-function}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Products of real scalars, vectors, and paravectors}
For the real paravectors \(\pv{X}\) and \(\pv{Y}\) from
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-x} and \eqref{eq:real-paravector-y},
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dot-cross} gives
\begin{align*}
\pv{X}\pv{Y}
&= (a+\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)(b+\vct{B}\cdot\sigv)\notag\\
&= (ab+\vct{A}\cdot\vct{B})
+ \bigl(a\vct{B}+b\vct{A}+i(\vct{A}\times\vct{B})\bigr)\cdot\sigv,\\
\pv{Y}\pv{X}
&= (ab+\vct{A}\cdot\vct{B})
+ \bigl(a\vct{B}+b\vct{A}-i(\vct{A}\times\vct{B})\bigr)\cdot\sigv \notag\\
&= (\pv{X}\pv{Y})^{\Hop}.
\end{align*}
The same product is given in component form in
Table \ref{tab:components-real-paravector-product}. The scalar part is
symmetric, while the sign of the imaginary-vector part is changed by the
order.

Two immediate consequences are
\begin{align*}
\com{\pv{X}}{\pv{Y}} &= 2i(\vct{A}\times\vct{B})\cdot\sigv,\\
\pv{X}\pv{Y}\in\RPspace
&\quad\eqtext{if and only if}\quad \vct{A}\times\vct{B}=0.
\end{align*}
Thus exact commutation of two real paravectors is obtained precisely when
\(\vct{A}\times\vct{B}=0\), including either zero-vector case. For two
nonzero vector parts, this means that they are parallel or antiparallel.

The antisymmetric term is removed automatically by setting
\(\pv{Y}=\pv{X}\):
\begin{align*}
\pv{X}^2
&= a^2+\vct{A}^2+2a\,\vct{A}\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
If \(\pv{X}^2=b+\vct{B}\cdot\sigv\), the following relations are obtained by
matching scalar and vector parts:
\begin{align*}
b &= a^2+\vct{A}^2,\\
\vct{B} &= 2a\,\vct{A},
\end{align*}
hence
\begin{align*}
a^2 &= \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(b\pm\sqrt{b^2-\vct{B}^2}\Bigr),\\
\vct{A}^2 &= b-a^2.
\end{align*}
These equations are necessary, but the direction equation
\(\vct{B}=2a\vct{A}\) must also be retained. When
\(\vct{B}\neq\vct{0}\), real paravector roots exist precisely when
\(b\geq\lVert\vct{B}\rVert\). For either choice of the inner sign, either
sign of the resulting nonzero \(a\) may be chosen, with
\begin{equation*}
\vct{A}=\frac{\vct{B}}{2a}.
\end{equation*}
When \(\vct{B}=\vct{0}\), a real root requires \(b\geq0\); the scalar
roots are \(\pv{X}=\pm\sqrt{b}\), and the exceptional pure-vector roots satisfy
\(a=0\) and \(\vct{A}^2=b\). Thus the displayed quadratic relation selects
among possible square-root values but is not, by itself, a complete
square-root formula. Such a selection among the possible values of a
multivalued function is called a branch choice.

\subsubsection{Products of complex paravectors}
The master product law \eqref{eq:paravector-product} is the complex
extension of the real formulas. When \(\pv{Z}\) is written as in Eqs.\
\eqref{eq:paravector}--\eqref{eq:paravector-cartesian} and
\(\pv{Z}'=\pv{X}'+i\pv{Y}'=S'+\vct{V}'\cdot\sigv\), every special case is
obtained by setting the unused scalar or vector parts to zero. The
quantities \(S,S'\) and the components of \(\vct{V},\vct{V}'\) may be
passed through one another and through every sigma generator. Complex
scalars with this property are called central. The full components of
\(\pv{Z}\pv{Z}'\) are given in
Table \ref{tab:components-paravector-product}, those of \(\pv{Z}^2\) are
given in Table \ref{tab:components-paravector-square}, and the
order-sensitive products built from star and \(\Hop\) are recorded in
Tables \ref{tab:components-zzstar}--\ref{tab:components-zcrevz}.

For products with ordinary scalar coefficients, exchanging the order of two
factors does not change the extracted scalar:
\begin{equation*}
\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{Z}\pv{Z}')=\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{Z}'\pv{Z}).
\end{equation*}
This order-independence of the scalar part is conventionally called
cyclicity.
In particular, if
\(\pv{Z}'=\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{Z}^{*\Hop}\), then
\begin{align*}
\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{Z}\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{Z}^{*\Hop})
&=\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{Z}^{*\Hop}\pv{Z})\notag\\
&=\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{\mathcal{O}})\,
  (\pv{Z}^{*\Hop}\pv{Z}),
\end{align*}
because \(\pv{Z}^{*\Hop}\pv{Z}\) is scalar.

The order-sensitive variants are divided into three algebraic types:
\begin{align*}
\pv{Z}\pv{Z}^* &\eqtext{ is a real scalar plus a complex vector in }\CPspace,\\
\pv{Z}\pv{Z}^{\Hop} &\eqtext{ is a real scalar plus a real vector in }\RPspace,\\
\pv{Z}\pv{Z}^{*\Hop} &\eqtext{ is a complex scalar in }\CPspace.
\end{align*}
The positive norms, determinant forms, inverses, and transformation formulas
are supplied by those three cases.

If a component is instead a derivative operation or another quantity whose
order cannot be exchanged,
the ordered expansion must be used before any simplification: in general
\(\vct{V} S'\neq S'\vct{V}\), terms from the ordinary derivative product
rule may occur, and cyclicity of
the algebraic scalar projection alone does not justify cycling the operator
coefficients. The displayed ordering is therefore required when the factors
act on functions. Such results cannot be obtained by automatic substitution
into the central-coefficient formulas.

\subsubsection{Order dependence and commutators}
The order-dependent part of a product is represented by the commutator
function defined in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:com-function}. For generic complex paravectors
\(\pv{Z},\pv{Z}'\in\CPspace\),
\begin{equation}
\com{\pv{Z}}{\pv{Z}'}
= 2i(\vct{V}\times\vct{V}')\cdot\sigv
\label{eq:paravector-commutator}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:paravector-commutator} is the compact commutator law. In
real and imaginary vector components, the following expansion is obtained:
\begin{align*}
\com{\pv{Z}}{\pv{Z}'}
&= -2(\vct{A}\times\vct{B}'+\vct{B}\times\vct{A}')\cdot\sigv \notag\\
&\quad +2i(\vct{A}\times\vct{A}'-\vct{B}\times\vct{B}')\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
The real-paravector commutator
\begin{equation*}
\com{\pv{X}}{\pv{Y}}=2i(\vct{A}\times\vct{B})\cdot\sigv
\end{equation*}
is the corresponding special case. In every case the scalar terms cancel,
so the cross-product part of the multiplication law is isolated by the
commutator.
The following identity is obtained by multiplying the \(n\)th basis
commutator by \(B_n\) and summing:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{n=1}^3 B_n\com{\vct{A}\cdot\sigv}{\pv{\sigma}_n}
=\com{\vct{A}\cdot\sigv}{\vct{B}\cdot\sigv}
=2i(\vct{A}\times\vct{B})\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:sigma-commutator-contraction}
\end{equation}
This is the component bridge between the vector cross-product law and the
commutator correction when the ordinary derivative product rule is applied
to a paravector product.

The symmetric companion is the anticommutator from
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:acom-function}. For pure vectors,
\begin{align}
\acom{\vct{A}\cdot\sigv}{\vct{B}\cdot\sigv}
&=(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)(\vct{B}\cdot\sigv)\notag\\
&\quad+(\vct{B}\cdot\sigv)(\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)\notag\\
&=2\,\vct{A}\cdot\vct{B}.
\label{eq:vector-anticommutator}
\end{align}
Thus the symmetric dot-product part is isolated by
\(\acom{\mathord\cdot}{\mathord\cdot}\), just as the antisymmetric
cross-product part is isolated by \(\com{\mathord\cdot}{\mathord\cdot}\).

More generally, for generic complex paravectors
\(\pv{Z},\pv{Z}'\in\CPspace\), Eq.\ \eqref{eq:acom-function} gives
\begin{equation}
\acom{\pv{Z}}{\pv{Z}'}=\pv{Z}\pv{Z}'+\pv{Z}'\pv{Z}.
\label{eq:paravector-anticommutator-expansion}
\end{equation}
The anticommutator is therefore
\begin{equation}
\acom{\pv{Z}}{\pv{Z}'}
=2\bigl(S S'+\vct{V}\cdot\vct{V}'\bigr)
+2\bigl(S\vct{V}'+S'\vct{V}\bigr)\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:paravector-anticommutator}
\end{equation}
The cross-product contribution to the anticommutator cancels identically.
Only the symmetric scalar and vector couplings are retained.

The same order dependence is already present in the sigma basis itself.
Commutation of the pseudoscalar with the generators is expressed by
\begin{align*}
\com{i}{\pv{\sigma}_n} &= 0,\\
i\sigv &= \sigv i
=\vthree{\pv{\sigma}_2\pv{\sigma}_3}
{\pv{\sigma}_3\pv{\sigma}_1}
{\pv{\sigma}_1\pv{\sigma}_2},
\end{align*}
while the following relation is satisfied by distinct sigma generators:
\begin{equation*}
\com{\pv{\sigma}_n}{\pv{\sigma}_m}=2\pv{\sigma}_n\pv{\sigma}_m=2\sum_{k=1}^3\epsilon_{nmk}i\pv{\sigma}_k
\qquad (n\neq m).
\end{equation*}
Thus commutators of the sigma-number-one generators reproduce the entire
oriented sigma-number-two basis.

and determinant-form consequences are fixed by Eqs.\
\eqref{eq:euclidean-form}, \eqref{eq:real-paravector-metric}, and
\eqref{eq:minkowski-form}; no additional quadratic definitions are required.
