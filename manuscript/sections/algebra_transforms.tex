\subsubsection{Intertwining and the General Similarity Transform}
The starting relation \eqref{eq:transform-intertwining} expresses
compatibility between two descriptions of the same algebraic action. Let
\(T\) carry a primed carrier into the unprimed description. If an algebra
element acts as \(Z'\) before that identification and as \(Z\) afterward,
then applying the action and changing description must commute:
\begin{equation*}
T(Z'\psi')=Z(T\psi')
\qquad\text{for every carrier }\psi'.
\end{equation*}
Removing the arbitrary carrier gives \(TZ'=ZT\). Because \(T\) is
invertible, its unique solution is
\begin{equation*}
Z'=T^{-1}ZT.
\end{equation*}
Thus Eq.\ \eqref{eq:general-transform} is not an additional dynamical law:
it is the change-of-description law forced by the intertwining requirement.
Defining \(T\) to carry descriptions in the opposite direction would replace
it everywhere by \(T^{-1}\) and would give the equivalent convention
\(Z'=TZT^{-1}\). The inverse-first convention is retained here because the
positive exponent in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:rotor} then gives the displayed positive
sign in Rodrigues's formula.

Now take the fully general complex paravectors
\begin{align*}
Z&=S+\mathbf V\cdot\sigv,
&S&\in\C,
&\mathbf V&\in\C^3,\\
T&=f+\mathbf g\cdot\sigv,
&f&\in\C,
&\mathbf g&\in\C^3,
\end{align*}
where the dot and cross products are extended complex-bilinearly. Define
the central determinant of \(T\) by
\begin{equation*}
\Delta_T:=T\crev T=f^2-\mathbf g^2.
\end{equation*}
When \(\Delta_T\neq0\), the general inverse formula
\eqref{eq:paravector-inverse} gives
\begin{equation*}
T^{-1}=\frac{\crev T}{\Delta_T}
=\frac{f-\mathbf g\cdot\sigv}{f^2-\mathbf g^2}.
\end{equation*}
Because the scalar part \(S\) is central,
\begin{align*}
Z'
&=S+\frac{(f-\mathbf g\cdot\sigv)
 (\mathbf V\cdot\sigv)(f+\mathbf g\cdot\sigv)}{\Delta_T}.
\end{align*}
The first multiplication is
\begin{equation*}
(f-\mathbf g\cdot\sigv)(\mathbf V\cdot\sigv)
=-\mathbf g\cdot\mathbf V
+\bigl(f\mathbf V-i\,\mathbf g\times\mathbf V\bigr)\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
After multiplication by the final factor, the scalar terms cancel and the
remaining vector terms give
\begin{align}
Z'&=S+\mathbf V'\cdot\sigv,\notag\\
\mathbf V'
&=\frac{(f^2+\mathbf g^2)\mathbf V
-2(\mathbf g\cdot\mathbf V)\mathbf g
-2if(\mathbf g\times\mathbf V)}{f^2-\mathbf g^2}.
\label{eq:similarity-components}
\end{align}
This is the similarity law for every complex paravector \(Z\) and every
invertible complex paravector \(T\). It shows directly that the complex
scalar part is fixed. Multiplying \(T\) by any nonzero central complex
scalar changes neither Eq.\ \eqref{eq:general-transform} nor
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:similarity-components}.

The same intertwining controls products, inverses, and functions. For a
second algebra element \(W\),
\begin{align}
(ZW)'&=T^{-1}ZWT
=T^{-1}ZT\,T^{-1}WT=Z'W',\notag\\
(Z')^{-1}&=T^{-1}Z^{-1}T
\qquad\text{when }Z^{-1}\text{ exists},\notag\\
(Z')^n&=T^{-1}Z^nT,\notag\\
h(Z')&=T^{-1}h(Z)T.
\label{eq:similarity-covariance}
\end{align}
The last line holds for every convergent power-series function \(h\), and
therefore in particular
\begin{equation*}
\exp(Z')=T^{-1}\exp(Z)T.
\end{equation*}
Logarithms, roots, and rational functions obey the same relation wherever
the same branch and domain are defined.

The reverse conjugation also commutes with this similarity. Since
\(\crev T=\Delta_TT^{-1}\) and
\(\crev{(T^{-1})}=T/\Delta_T\),
\begin{align*}
\crev{Z'}
&=\crev T\,\crev Z\,\crev{(T^{-1})}
=T^{-1}\crev ZT,\\
Z'\crev{Z'}
&=T^{-1}(Z\crev Z)T=Z\crev Z.
\end{align*}
Thus similarity preserves the determinant form
\(S^2-\mathbf V^2\), the spectrum, and the inverse framework for every
invertible \(T\). The positive coefficient norm is a different construction
and is not generally invariant under a nonunitary similarity.

The similarity law transports algebra elements that act by multiplication;
it is not automatically the Lorentz law for every physical representation.
A real event paravector satisfies \(X^{\mathsf R}=X\). Such paravectors are
spanned by Hermitian carrier bilinears \(\psi\psi^{\mathsf R}\): concretely,
in the Pauli realization every Hermitian \(2\times2\) matrix is a real linear
combination of bilinears of this form. The same carrier identification used
above is \(\psi'=T^{-1}\psi\), and therefore
\begin{equation*}
\psi'\psi'^{\mathsf R}
=T^{-1}(\psi\psi^{\mathsf R})(T^{-1})^{\mathsf R}.
\end{equation*}
By real linearity, the event law is consequently forced to be the
inverse-carried Hermitian congruence. For a normalized Lorentz element
\(T\crev T=1\), it is
\begin{align}
X'
&=T^{-1}X(T^{-1})^{\mathsf R}\notag\\
&=T^{*\mathsf R}XT^*.
\label{eq:general-event-congruence}
\end{align}
Here the involutions have distinct roles. In the Pauli realization,
\({}^{\mathsf R}\) is Hermitian adjunction, whereas
\({}^{*\mathsf R}\) is the adjugate. The latter equals \(T^{-1}\) only
after the determinant-one normalization \(T\crev T=1\) has been imposed.
It preserves the real subspace because
\((X')^{\mathsf R}=X'\), and it preserves the event determinant because
\(T^{-1}\) has unit determinant. When \(X\) is invertible, the transformed
inverse follows without a new definition:
\begin{equation}
(X')^{-1}=T^{\mathsf R}X^{-1}T,
\label{eq:event-inverse-transform}
\end{equation}
because direct multiplication with
\(X'=T^{-1}X(T^{-1})^{\mathsf R}\) gives the identity. This complementary
law is also the algebraic pattern used later by the dual derivative
paravector. Multiplying
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:general-event-congruence} on the left by \(T\) gives the
event intertwiner
\begin{equation}
TX'=XT^*.
\label{eq:event-intertwining}
\end{equation}
This becomes \(TX'=XT\) for a circular rotor because \(R^*=R\), but not
for a boost. Deriving Rodrigues's formula first can therefore make the
similarity intertwiner look universal even though the boost event belongs
to a different representation.

In contrast, a bivector such as the electromagnetic field acts by left
multiplication on a carrier. Applying the original intertwining argument
to that operator forces the similarity
\begin{equation}
F'=T^{-1}FT.
\label{eq:general-field-similarity}
\end{equation}
For a circular rotor, \(R^*=R\) and
\(R^{-1}=R^{\mathsf R}\), so Eq.\ \eqref{eq:general-event-congruence}
reduces to \(X'=R^{\mathsf R}XR\). For a hyperbolic element,
\(L^{\mathsf R}=L\) and \(L^{-1}=L^*=L^{*\mathsf R}\), so the same equation
reduces to \(X'=L^*XL^*\), whereas
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:general-field-similarity} reduces to \(F'=L^*FL\).
The unequal right factors therefore follow from the representations being
transported, not from an inconsistent choice of inverse.
With the present definition of \(L(\theta,\mathbf u)\), the sandwich
\(L^{\mathsf R}XL=LXL\) is the inverse-direction event boost, obtained by
replacing \(\theta\) with \(-\theta\). It is physically legitimate, but it
is not the passive \(+\theta\) convention used in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:boost}.

The hyperbolic reduction of the general complex formula can also be read
off directly. With
\[
f=\cosh(\theta/2),
\qquad
\mathbf g=\sinh(\theta/2)\mathbf u,
\qquad
\mathbf u^2=1,
\]
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:similarity-components} becomes
\begin{equation}
\mathbf V'
=(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf V)\mathbf u
+\cosh(\theta)\bigl(\mathbf V-(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf V)\mathbf u\bigr)
-i\sinh(\theta)\,\mathbf u\times\mathbf V.
\label{eq:hyperbolic-similarity}
\end{equation}
For \(\mathbf V=\mathbf E+i\mathbf B\), its real and imaginary parts are
exactly the electric--magnetic boost formulas derived later. For a real
\(\mathbf V\), however, the cross term is generally imaginary; this again
shows why hyperbolic similarity is a bivector or basis transformation and
not an event boost.

The infinitesimal forms make the distinction especially transparent. If
\(T=1+K+O(K^2)\), then
\begin{align*}
Z'&=Z+\com{Z}{K}+O(K^2),\\
X'&=X-KX-XK^{\mathsf R}+O(K^2).
\end{align*}
For a circular generator \(K^{\mathsf R}=-K\), the event law reduces to the
same commutator that generates the similarity rotation. For a hyperbolic
generator \(K^{\mathsf R}=K\), it instead contains an anticommutator:
\(X'=X-\acom{K}{X}+O(K^2)\). The former rotates spatial vectors; the latter
mixes the scalar time component with the vector component parallel to the
boost. This is why the rotation can first be derived as a similarity while
the event boost cannot.

For a one-parameter family \(\lambda\mapsto T(\lambda)\), introduce
\begin{align*}
\Omega&:=T^{-1}\dot T,\qquad
\dot T^{-1}=-T^{-1}\dot T\,T^{-1},\\
\dot Z' &= T^{-1}\dot Z T + \com{Z'}{\Omega},\notag \\
\ddot Z' &= T^{-1}\ddot Z T,\notag \\
&\quad +2\com{\dot Z'}{\Omega},\notag \\
&\quad +\com{Z'}{\dot\Omega},\notag \\
&\quad +\com{\Omega}{\com{Z'}{\Omega}}.
\end{align*}
Dots here mean ordinary derivatives with respect to the real parameter
\(\lambda\). This avoids confusing the transport calculation with exterior
differentiation, for which \(d^2=0\).

\subsubsection{Transformed Basis}
Let the parameter-dependent element be
\begin{align*}
T(\lambda)&=f(\lambda)+\mathbf g(\lambda)\cdot\sigv,
\qquad f\in\C,\quad \mathbf g\in\C^3,\\
T^{-1}&=\frac{f-\mathbf g\cdot\sigv}{f^2-\mathbf g^2},
\qquad f^2-\mathbf g^2\neq0,\\
\sigma_n'&:=T^{-1}\sigma_nT,\qquad
\sigma_n=T\sigma_n'T^{-1}.
\end{align*}
Allowing complex \(\mathbf g\) includes both real hyperbolic elements and
imaginary-vector rotors. Similarity immediately gives
\begin{align*}
(\sigma_n')^2&=1,\\
\sigma_n'\sigma_m'
&=\delta_{nm}+\sum_{k=1}^3\epsilon_{nmk}\,i\sigma_k',\\
\sigma_1'\sigma_2'\sigma_3'&=i.
\end{align*}
Thus the transformed generators obey the same Clifford algebra for every
invertible \(T\).

For every invertible \(T\), reverse conjugation commutes with the
similarity. Using
\(\crev T=(f^2-\mathbf g^2)T^{-1}\) and
\(\crev{(T^{-1})}=T/(f^2-\mathbf g^2)\),
\begin{align*}
(\sigma_n')^{*\mathsf R}
&=(f^2-\mathbf g^2)T^{-1}(-\sigma_n)
  \frac{T}{f^2-\mathbf g^2}\\
&=-T^{-1}\sigma_nT=-\sigma_n'.
\end{align*}
Consequently a paravector written in the transformed basis,
\[
Z=Z_s+Z_{\mathbf V}\cdot\sigv',
\]
has the same determinant quadratic form,
\[
ZZ^{*\mathsf R}=Z_s^2-Z_{\mathbf V}^2.
\]

For differentiation with respect to \(\lambda\), let
\[
\Omega:=T^{-1}\dot T.
\]
Then
\begin{align*}
\dot\sigma_n'&=\com{\sigma_n'}{\Omega},&
\dot\sigv'&=\com{\sigv'}{\Omega}.
\end{align*}
The coefficient form follows directly from
\begin{align*}
\Omega
&=\frac{f\dot f-\mathbf g\cdot\dot{\mathbf g}}
        {f^2-\mathbf g^2}\\
&\quad+
\frac{f\dot{\mathbf g}-\dot f\,\mathbf g
      -i\,\mathbf g\times\dot{\mathbf g}}
     {f^2-\mathbf g^2}\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
Relative to the primed basis, write its vector coefficient uniquely as
\[
\Omega=\Omega_s+\tfrac12(\mathbf k+i\boldsymbol\omega)\cdot\sigv',
\qquad \mathbf k,\boldsymbol\omega\in\R^3.
\]
The scalar part commutes with the basis, while
\begin{align*}
\dot\sigv'
&=(-\boldsymbol\omega+i\mathbf k)\times\sigv',\\
\dot Z
&=\dot Z_s+
\bigl(\dot Z_{\mathbf V}
      -Z_{\mathbf V}\times\boldsymbol\omega
      +i\,Z_{\mathbf V}\times\mathbf k\bigr)\cdot\sigv'.
\end{align*}
These are algebraic moving-basis identities. No space-time, energy, or
proper-time interpretation follows from similarity alone; such an
interpretation requires a specified Lorentz congruence and frame geometry.

\subsubsection{Rotation}
The identities in this subsection unpack Eq.\ \eqref{eq:rotor} as the rotation case of the general transform law, with the transform element specialized to \(T=R\).
Here the generator has sigma number two, so the exponential is circular rather than hyperbolic:
\begin{align*}
R(\theta ,\mathbf u) &:= \exp( \tfrac{1}{2} \theta (\mathbf u \cdot i \sigv ) ),\notag \\
&= \cos( \tfrac{1}{2} \theta ) + \sin( \tfrac{1}{2} \theta ) (\mathbf u \cdot i \sigv )\qquad \text{rotor}.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
R^{-1} = \exp( -\tfrac{1}{2} \theta (\mathbf u \cdot i \sigv ) ).
\end{equation*}
Here \(\theta\in\R\), \(\mathbf u\in\R^3\) is a unit real vector, and \(R\) is generated by the sigma-number-two element \(\mathbf u\cdot i\sigv\). Its inverse satisfies \(R^{-1}=R^{\mathrm{R}}=R^{*\mathrm{R}}\).
To derive the sign rather than choose it afterward, substitute
\begin{equation*}
f=\cos(\theta/2),
\qquad
\mathbf g=i\sin(\theta/2)\mathbf u
\end{equation*}
into Eq.\ \eqref{eq:similarity-components}. Then
\begin{align*}
f^2-\mathbf g^2&=1,\\
f^2+\mathbf g^2&=\cos(\theta),\\
-2(\mathbf g\cdot\mathbf r)\mathbf g
&=(1-\cos(\theta))(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf r)\mathbf u,\\
-2if(\mathbf g\times\mathbf r)
&=\sin(\theta)(\mathbf u\times\mathbf r).
\end{align*}
Therefore the transformed paravector is
\begin{align*}
X' &= R^{\mathrm{R}} X R,\notag \\
&= t' + \mathbf r' \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
t' &= t,\notag \\
\mathbf r' &= \mathbf r \cos(\theta ) + (\mathbf u \times \mathbf r) \sin(\theta ),\notag \\
&\quad + (\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf r) (1 - \cos(\theta )) \mathbf u,\notag \\
\qquad \text{Rodrigues's formula}.
\end{align*}
For a parameter-dependent rotor, define the right- and left-carried
generators
\begin{equation*}
\Omega:=\dot R R^{\mathsf R},
\qquad
\Omega':=R^{\mathsf R}\dot R=R^{\mathsf R}\Omega R.
\end{equation*}
Differentiating \(R^{\mathsf R}R=1\) gives
\[
\Omega^{\mathsf R}=-\Omega,\qquad
(\Omega')^{\mathsf R}=-\Omega'.
\]
The rotation transport law is the specialization of the general
one-parameter similarity formula:
\begin{align*}
\dot X'
&=R^{\mathsf R}\dot X R+\com{X'}{\Omega'}\\
&=R^{\mathsf R}\bigl(\dot X+\com{X}{\Omega}\bigr)R.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Spin Rotors}
The pseudoscalar identifies each oriented complementary bivector without an
index-order ambiguity:
\[
i\sigma_1=\sigma_2\sigma_3,\qquad
i\sigma_2=\sigma_3\sigma_1,\qquad
i\sigma_3=\sigma_1\sigma_2.
\]
For unit \(\mathbf n,\mathbf u\in\R^3\), let
\begin{align*}
R(\phi,\mathbf n)
&=\exp\!\left(\frac{\phi}{2}\,\mathbf n\cdot i\sigv\right)\\
&=\cos(\phi/2)
 +\sin(\phi/2)\,\mathbf n\cdot i\sigv .
\end{align*}
Then
\[
R^{-1}=R^{\mathsf R}=R^{*\mathsf R},\qquad
R^{\mathsf R}R=1,
\]
and
\[
R^{\mathsf R}(\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)R
=\mathbf u'\cdot\sigv
\]
with \(\mathbf u'\) given by Rodrigues's formula in the preceding
subsection. The rotor itself is single-valued and \(4\pi\)-periodic:
\[
R(\phi+2\pi,\mathbf n)=-R(\phi,\mathbf n),\qquad
R(\phi+4\pi,\mathbf n)=R(\phi,\mathbf n).
\]
The induced spatial rotation is two-to-one because \(R\) and \(-R\) give
the same conjugation.

In the Pauli matrix realization, reversion is Hermitian adjunction. A
general \(\psi\in\C\oplus\C^3\) is therefore a full \(2\times2\) matrix, not
one irreducible two-component spinor: left multiplication rotates its two
columns independently. It may instead be used as a density amplitude, or
restricted to a minimal left ideal, implemented by a fixed projector on the
right, to represent one Pauli spinor.
With the active convention consistent with the vector action above,
\[
\psi'=R^{\mathsf R}\psi,\qquad
\psi'\psi'^{\mathsf R}
=R^{\mathsf R}(\psi\psi^{\mathsf R})R.
\]
For a nonzero state, a normalized expectation numerator is converted into
an expectation value by
\[
\langle M\rangle_\psi
:=\frac{\eqtext{scalar}(\psi^{\mathsf R}M\psi)}
        {\eqtext{scalar}(\psi^{\mathsf R}\psi)}.
\]
These qualifications separate the algebra action from the choice of an
irreducible spin state.
