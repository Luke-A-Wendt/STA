\subsubsection{Quaternion Basis and Involutions}
The same sigma-number-two basis used in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:exp-bivector} also reproduces Hamilton's quaternion units, so the rotation algebra can be written in quaternion form without leaving the STA multiplication table \cite{hamilton1844,clifford1878}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
q_1&=-\sigma_2\sigma_3,\qquad
q_2=-\sigma_3\sigma_1,\\
q_3&=-\sigma_1\sigma_2,\qquad
\mathbf q:=(q_1,q_2,q_3)=-i\sigv.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:quaternion-basis}
\end{equation}
These basis elements satisfy the standard quaternion identities
\begin{equation*}
q_1^2=q_2^2=q_3^2=q_1q_2q_3=-1.
\end{equation*}
So the quaternion units are not new generators; they are the sigma-number-two basis already present in the STA algebra, only relabeled.

If \(\mathbf B=(B_1,B_2,B_3)\in\R^3\), then \(\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf q=-i\,\mathbf B\cdot\sigv\). Since \(q_k=-i\sigma_k\), the abstract operator rules immediately give
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf q^*=\mathbf q,
\qquad
\mathbf q^{\mathrm R}=-\mathbf q.
\end{equation*}
Thus the conjugate operator fixes the quaternion basis, while the reverse operator changes its sign.

\subsubsection{Quaternion Products}
For \(a\in\R\) and \(\mathbf B\in\R^3\), define the quaternion
\begin{equation}
Q:=a+\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf q
=a-i\,\mathbf B\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:quaternion-paravector}
\end{equation}
Because \(\mathbf q^*=\mathbf q\), one has
\begin{equation*}
Q^*=Q.
\end{equation*}
Therefore
\begin{align}
QQ^*&=Q^2\notag\\
&=(a-i\,\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)^2\notag\\
&=a^2-2ia\,\mathbf B\cdot\sigv+(-i)^2(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)^2\notag\\
&=a^2-\mathbf B^2-2ia\,\mathbf B\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:quaternion-square}
\end{align}
The linear term must carry the factor \(a\). This is just the sigma-number-two multiplication law written in quaternion language.

Since \(\mathbf q^{\mathrm R}=-\mathbf q\), the reverse of \(Q\) is
\begin{equation*}
Q^{\mathrm R}=a-\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf q
=a+i\,\mathbf B\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
Multiplying \(Q\) by its reverse gives
\begin{equation}
QQ^{\mathrm R}=a^2+\mathbf B^2.
\label{eq:quaternion-norm}
\end{equation}
So the reverse product recovers the standard positive quaternion norm square, while Eq.\ \eqref{eq:quaternion-square} records the ordinary algebra square.

\subsubsection{Quaternion Form of the Rotor Law}
Now that Eq.\ \eqref{eq:rotor} and its transform consequences have been developed, the quaternion rotation law can be read off directly. For a unit quaternion \(Q\) satisfying \(QQ^{\mathrm R}=1\), and for a real vector \(\mathbf r\in\R^3\), one has
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r'\cdot\mathbf q
=Q(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf q)Q^{\mathrm R}.
\label{eq:quaternion-rotation}
\end{equation}
This is the quaternion version of the rotor action \(\mathbf r'\cdot\sigv=\rev{R}(\mathbf r\cdot\sigv)R\). In particular, choosing
\begin{equation*}
Q=\cos\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)-\sin\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf q
\end{equation*}
is equivalent to the rotor \(R(\theta,\mathbf u)=\exp(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\mathbf u\cdot i\sigv)\), because \(\mathbf q=-i\sigv\). Thus quaternion rotations are not a parallel construction: they are exactly the same circular half-angle algebra already developed in the STA basis.
