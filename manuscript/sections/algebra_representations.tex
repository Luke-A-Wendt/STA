\subsubsection{Standard Two-by-Two Matrix Realization}
\label{sec:matrix-realization}
The real associative algebra generated by the three sigma elements is
commonly called the real Pauli algebra or the three-dimensional real
Clifford algebra. The information carried by the even products in the usual
flat space-time algebra is retained, and a faithful representation is
provided by two-by-two complex matrices. Its eight real coefficients are
grouped by the internal pseudoscalar into four complex coefficients: one
scalar and one three-vector. Context is provided by these established
descriptions, while only
the previously defined operations are used in the construction
\cite{pauli1927}.

For compactness, the notation \(\{a,b;c,d\}\) is used for the two-by-two
matrix with first row \(a,b\) and second row \(c,d\), where every entry is a
complex scalar.
Each matrix operation used below is defined separately:
\begin{equation}
\I:=\{1,0;0,1\}.
\label{eq:matrix-identity}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\eqtext{tr}(\{a,b;c,d\}):=a+d.
\label{eq:matrix-trace}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\eqtext{diag}(A,B):=\{A,0;0,B\}.
\label{eq:matrix-diag}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\{a,b;c,d\}^{T}:=\{a,c;b,d\}.
\label{eq:matrix-transpose}
\end{equation}
For a scalar entry \(z=x+iy\), the matrix-only conjugate is
\begin{equation}
\mconj{z}:=x-iy.
\label{eq:matrix-entry-conjugate}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mconj{\{a,b;c,d\}}:=\{\mconj{a},\mconj{b};\mconj{c},\mconj{d}\}.
\label{eq:matrix-conjugate}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mherm{\{a,b;c,d\}}:=\{\mconj{a},\mconj{c};\mconj{b},\mconj{d}\}.
\label{eq:matrix-hermitian}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\adjf{\{a,b;c,d\}}:=\{d,-b;-c,a\}.
\label{eq:matrix-adjugate}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\detf{\{a,b;c,d\}}:=ad-bc.
\label{eq:matrix-determinant}
\end{equation}
The sign of \(i\) in each scalar entry is changed by the matrix-only
superscript \(c\), and nothing else is changed. Entrywise conjugation
followed by transpose is denoted by the superscript \(cT\); the two
operations commute. The
superscript \(c\) is reserved for matrices and their scalar entries. It is
not an additional intrinsic operation on the sigma algebra.

The standard realization is
\begin{equation}
\mat{\sigma}_1=\{0,1;1,0\},
\quad
\mat{\sigma}_2=\{0,-i;i,0\},
\quad
\mat{\sigma}_3=\{1,0;0,-1\}.
\label{eq:pauli-matrices}
\end{equation}
The entry \(i\) is the same central unit defined in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:basis}; as a scalar matrix entry it obeys \(i^2=-1\).
No second imaginary unit is introduced. The distinction in the matrix
model lies in the operation being applied. Every displayed \(i\), including
the one built into \(\mat{\sigma}_2\), is conjugated by entrywise \(c\).
Every abstract sigma generator is additionally flipped by intrinsic star.

The abstract pseudoscalar is represented by the matrix product
\(\mat{\sigma}_1\mat{\sigma}_2\mat{\sigma}_3=i\I\). In this realization,
\begin{equation*}
\mat{\sigma}_n^2=\I,
\qquad
\mat{\sigma}_m\mat{\sigma}_n=-\mat{\sigma}_n\mat{\sigma}_m\quad(m\neq n),
\qquad
\mat{\sigma}_1\mat{\sigma}_2\mat{\sigma}_3=i\I.
\end{equation*}
Thus Eq.\ \eqref{eq:pauli-matrices} is a concrete model of the sigma
generators and their pseudoscalar.

For the generic complex paravector
\(\pv{Z}=S+\vct{V}\cdot\sigv\), its represented matrix is denoted by
\(\mat{M}\):
\begin{equation}
\mat{M}:=
\{S+V_3,V_1-iV_2;V_1+iV_2,S-V_3\}.
\label{eq:matrix-paravector-map}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\pv{Z}\) is represented by \(\mat{M}\).
Conversely, if \(\mat{M}=\{a,b;c,d\}\), then
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
S&=\frac{a+d}{2},&
V_1&=\frac{b+c}{2},\\
V_2&=\frac{c-b}{2i},&
V_3&=\frac{a-d}{2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:matrix-paravector-inverse-map}
\end{equation}
The realization in both directions is therefore given by
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:matrix-paravector-map} and
\eqref{eq:matrix-paravector-inverse-map}.

For a real paravector
\(\pv{X}=t+\vct{r}\cdot\sigv\in\R+\R^3\), with
\(\vct{r}=(r_1,r_2,r_3)\in\R^3\), the matrix form is
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\pv{X}=t+\vct{r}\cdot\sigv\\
\eqtext{is represented by}\\
\{t+r_3,r_1-ir_2;r_1+ir_2,t-r_3\},\\
\detf{\pv{X}}=t^2-\vct{r}^2.
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:matrix-X}
\end{equation}
The real Minkowski quadratic form in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-metric} is reproduced by
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:matrix-X}, together with
\begin{align*}
\eqtext{tr}(\mat{M})&=2t,\\
\lambda^2-\eqtext{tr}(\mat{M})\lambda+\detf{\mat{M}}&=0,\\
\lambda(\pv{X},\pm)&=t\pm\lVert\vct{r}\rVert.
\end{align*}
These eigenvalues are the matrix form of the split into scalar part \(t\)
and vector magnitude \(\lVert\vct{r}\rVert\).

The sigma-basis action not captured by entrywise \(c\) is accounted for by
the fixed matrix
\begin{equation}
\Tmat:=\{0,1;-1,0\}.
\label{eq:matrix-T}
\end{equation}
It satisfies \(\Tmat^{-1}=-\Tmat\).
The complete intrinsic-to-matrix dictionary is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\pv{Z}
&\quad\eqtext{is represented by}\quad \mat{M},\\
\conj{\pv{Z}}
&\quad\eqtext{is represented by}\quad
\conj{\mat{M}},\\
\conj{\mat{M}}
&=\Tmat\,\mconj{\mat{M}}\,\Tmat^{-1}
\\
&=\{\mconj{d},-\mconj{c};-\mconj{b},\mconj{a}\},\\
\herm{\pv{Z}}
&\quad\eqtext{is represented by}\quad
\herm{\mat{M}},\\
\herm{\mat{M}}
&=\mherm{\mat{M}}
\\
&=\{\mconj{a},\mconj{c};\mconj{b},\mconj{d}\},\\
\adjf{\pv{Z}}
&=\cherm{\pv{Z}},\\
\adjf{\pv{Z}}
&\quad\eqtext{is represented by}\quad
\adjf{\mat{M}},\\
\adjf{\mat{M}}
&=\Tmat\,\mat{M}^{T}\,\Tmat^{-1}
\\
&=\{d,-b;-c,a\},\\
\detf{\pv{Z}}
&\quad\eqtext{is represented by}\quad
\detf{\mat{M}}=ad-bc,\\
0&\quad\eqtext{is represented by}\quad\{0,0;0,0\},\\
1&\quad\eqtext{is represented by}\quad\I,\\
\pv{Z}^{-1}
&\quad\eqtext{is represented by}\quad
\mat{M}^{-1},\\
\mat{M}^{-1}
&=\frac{\adjf{\mat{M}}}{\detf{\mat{M}}},
\qquad \detf{\mat{M}}\neq0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:matrix-operation-dictionary}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\herm{\mat{M}}=\mherm{\mat{M}}\) is the ordinary Hermitian
adjoint. A matrix is Hermitian precisely when
\(\herm{\mat{M}}=\mat{M}\), while the matrix adjugate is always denoted by
\(\adjf{\mat{M}}\). Crucially, intrinsic star is not matrix \(c\):
\(\conj{\mat{M}}\neq\mconj{\mat{M}}\) in general.

The star on the left is the intrinsic sigma conjugate transported into
the matrix realization; the \(c\) on the right is only entrywise complex
conjugation. They agree on central complex scalars, but not on a generic
paravector. When \(\pv{Z}\) is represented by \(\mat{M}\), the direct bridge
is that \(\herm{\pv{Z}}\) is represented by \(\mherm{\mat{M}}\). Intrinsic star
instead requires the basis correction
\(\conj{\mat{M}}=\Tmat\mconj{\mat{M}}\Tmat^{-1}\).

The extraction functions have the equally direct matrix form
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\eqtext{scalar}(\pv{Z})
&\quad\eqtext{is represented by}\quad\tfrac12\eqtext{tr}(\mat{M}),\\
\eqtext{vector}(\pv{Z})\cdot\sigv
&\quad\eqtext{is represented by}\quad
\mat{M}-\tfrac12\eqtext{tr}(\mat{M})\I,\\
\eqtext{real}(\pv{Z})
&\quad\eqtext{is represented by}\quad
\tfrac12\bigl(\mat{M}+\herm{\mat{M}}\bigr),\\
\eqtext{img}(\pv{Z})
&\quad\eqtext{is represented by}\quad
\frac{\mat{M}-\herm{\mat{M}}}{2i}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:matrix-extraction-dictionary}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the algebraic determinant is exactly the
ordinary two-by-two matrix determinant:
\begin{equation}
\mat{M}\adjf{\mat{M}}
=\adjf{\mat{M}}\mat{M}
=\detf{\mat{M}}\I.
\label{eq:matrix-adjugate-product}
\end{equation}

An unambiguous correspondence is obtained among the operation types. Matrix \(c\) is an
antilinear automorphism and matrix \(T\) is a linear anti-automorphism.
Their composition \(cT\), represented intrinsically by \(H\), is an
antilinear anti-automorphism. The intrinsic star is a different antilinear
automorphism, and the combined intrinsic operation \(*H\) is the linear
anti-automorphism represented by the matrix adjugate. Consequently,
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:hermitian-adjugate-identity} is represented by
\begin{equation*}
\herm{\mat{M}}
=\conj{\adjf{\mat{M}}}
=\adjf{\conj{\mat{M}}},
\end{equation*}
where the star in \(\conj{\mat{M}}\) remains the induced sigma conjugate,
not entrywise \(c\).

The distinction is made concrete by the basis actions:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mconj{\mat{\sigma}_1}&=\mat{\sigma}_1,
&
\mconj{\mat{\sigma}_2}&=-\mat{\sigma}_2,
&
\mconj{\mat{\sigma}_3}&=\mat{\sigma}_3,\\
\mat{\sigma}_1^T&=\mat{\sigma}_1,
&
\mat{\sigma}_2^T&=-\mat{\sigma}_2,
&
\mat{\sigma}_3^T&=\mat{\sigma}_3,\\
\mherm{\mat{\sigma}_n}&=\mat{\sigma}_n,
&
\conj{\mat{\sigma}_n}&=-\mat{\sigma}_n,
&
\cherm{\mat{\sigma}_n}&=-\mat{\sigma}_n.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:matrix-basis-actions}
\end{equation}
On these three individual generator matrices, the same sign table is obtained
from \(c\) and \(T\). Distinct operations are nevertheless represented:
\((\mat{A}\mat{B})^c=\mat{A}^c\mat{B}^c\), whereas
\((\mat{A}\mat{B})^T=\mat{B}^T\mat{A}^T\). Only the represented second
generator is changed by entrywise \(c\), while all three are changed by
intrinsic star. Every \(\mat{\sigma}_n\) is fixed by their composition
\(cT\), exactly matching intrinsic \(H\). Thus \(c\) is a
basis-dependent matrix-coordinate
operation and is distinct from both intrinsic involutions. In particular,
it is not intrinsic star. For \(n\neq m\),
\[
\herm{\mat{\sigma}_n\mat{\sigma}_m}
=\mat{\sigma}_m\mat{\sigma}_n
=-\mat{\sigma}_n\mat{\sigma}_m,
\]
exactly as required by the intrinsic order-reversal rule.

\subsubsection{Auxiliary Block Map}
A scalar-coefficient complex paravector \(\pv{Z}\in\C+\C^3\) is packaged
into the \(2\times2\) block map
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:W-map}
\Wmat(\pv{Z}):=\{0,\pv{Z};\adjf{\pv{Z}},0\}.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\Wmat(\pv{Z})^2
=\eqtext{diag}(\detf{\pv{Z}},\detf{\pv{Z}})
=\detf{\pv{Z}}\I.
\label{eq:W-square}
\end{equation}
The block map \(\Wmat(\pv{Z})\) is a \(2\times2\) matrix over the paravector
algebra. After each block is replaced by its Pauli matrix, it is a
\(4\times4\) complex matrix. It is a useful linear packaging map, not an
algebra homomorphism. Equation \eqref{eq:W-square} is obtained because its
diagonal block products are reduced to the determinant in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:minkowski-form}; the off-diagonal blocks are zero
\cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}.

If \(\detf{\pv{Z}}\neq0\), the annihilating polynomial
\(x^2-\detf{\pv{Z}}\) has distinct roots, so the block matrix is
diagonalizable after a square-root branch is chosen. If
\(\detf{\pv{Z}}=0\) and \(\pv{Z}\neq0\), then
\(\Wmat(\pv{Z})^2=0\) while \(\Wmat(\pv{Z})\neq0\): it is nilpotent and
not diagonalizable. For differential or other noncommuting
operator-valued blocks, the unreduced statement is
\(\Wmat(\pv{Z})^2
=\eqtext{diag}(\pv{Z}\cherm{\pv{Z}},\cherm{\pv{Z}}\pv{Z})\).
A separate operator calculation is required before either ordered product can
be called a determinant or both can be reduced to one scalar multiple of the
identity.
