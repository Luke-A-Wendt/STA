\subsubsection*{Standard \texorpdfstring{$2\times2$}{2x2} Matrix Realization}
Although the algebra has been developed directly in STA form, it is useful to record a standard \(2\times2\) matrix realization. This realization confirms the earlier product rules in a familiar concrete model without replacing the algebraic viewpoint used throughout the paper \cite{pauli1927}.

Consistent with Eq.\ \eqref{eq:basis}, the matrix realization is
\begin{equation}
\sigma_1=\{0, 1; 1, 0\},
\quad
\sigma_2=\{0, -i; i, 0\},
\quad
\sigma_3=\{1, 0; 0, -1\},
\label{eq:pauli-matrices}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:pauli-matrices} gives a concrete \(2\times2\) model for the generators. In that realization, a real paravector \(X=t+\mathbf r\cdot\sigv\in\R\oplus\R^3\) becomes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
X&=t+\mathbf r\cdot\sigv
=\{ t+r_3, r_1-ir_2; r_1+ir_2, t-r_3 \},\\
\det X&=t^2-\mathbf r^2.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:matrix-X}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:matrix-X} reproduces exactly the indefinite quadratic form already encoded by Eq.\ \eqref{eq:minkowski-form}. The same matrix also carries the familiar trace and characteristic data:
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{tr}(X)&=2t,\\
\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}X)\lambda+\det X&=0,\\
\lambda(\pm)&=t\pm \lVert\mathbf r\rVert.
\end{align*}
These eigenvalues are the matrix version of the split into scalar part \(t\) and vector magnitude \(\lVert\mathbf r\rVert\).

Within the same realization, the reverse operator and conjugate operator become concrete matrix maps. Writing
\begin{equation*}
M^\dagger:=M^{cT},
\end{equation*}
with \(M^c\) denoting entrywise complex conjugation, one has
\begin{equation*}
i^c=-i,
\qquad
\sigma_1^c=\sigma_1,
\qquad
\sigma_2^c=-\sigma_2,
\qquad
\sigma_3^c=\sigma_3,
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
(\sigma_n\sigma_m)^{cT}=\sigma_m\sigma_n=-\sigma_n\sigma_m
\qquad (n\neq m),
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
\sigma_n^{cT}=\sigma_n,
\qquad
(i\I)^{cT}=-i\I,
\qquad
(i\sigma_n)^{cT}=-i\sigma_n,
\end{equation*}
so in the matrix model the reverse operator coincides with the Hermitian adjoint on the sigma basis. The remaining conjugate formulas then follow from a fixed similarity matrix. With
\begin{equation*}
\mathsf J:=\{0, 1; -1, 0\},
\end{equation*}
the conjugate operator and the combined action of the conjugate and reverse operators may be written as
\begin{equation*}
M^{*}=\mathsf J\,M^{c}\,\mathsf J^{-1},
\qquad
M^{*\mathrm R}=\mathsf J\,M^{T}\,\mathsf J^{-1}.
\end{equation*}
These identities recover the same operator rules defined abstractly in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:star-definition}--\eqref{eq:paravector-involutions}.

\subsubsection*{Auxiliary \texorpdfstring{$2\times2$}{2x2} Realization}
It is also useful to package a complex paravector \(Z\in\C\oplus\C^3\) into the auxiliary \(2\times2\) map
\begin{equation}
W(Z):=\{0, Z; \crev{Z}, 0\},
\qquad
W(Z)^2=\mathrm{diag}(Z\crev{Z},\crev{Z}Z).
\label{eq:W-map}
\end{equation}
Here \(W(Z)\) is a realization built from the same complex paravector and the combined action of the conjugate and reverse operators. Its square is diagonal because the off-diagonal products collapse to the quadratic forms already introduced in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:euclidean-form} and \eqref{eq:minkowski-form} \cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}.
