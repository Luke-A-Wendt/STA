\subsubsection{Standard \texorpdfstring{$2\times2$}{2x2} Matrix Realization}
Although the algebra has been developed directly in STA form, it is useful to close the algebraic portion with a single self-contained \(2\times2\) matrix model. This realization confirms the earlier product rules in a familiar concrete form without replacing the algebraic viewpoint used throughout the paper \cite{pauli1927}.

The standard realization is
\begin{equation}
\sigma_1=\{0, 1; 1, 0\},
\quad
\sigma_2=\{0, -i; i, 0\},
\quad
\sigma_3=\{1, 0; 0, -1\},
\label{eq:pauli-matrices}
\end{equation}
Here the \(i\) appearing in the entries of \(\sigma_2\) is strictly the ordinary complex unit \(i:=\sqrt{-1}\). The matrix entries are not defined recursively from the STA pseudoscalar; rather, the abstract pseudoscalar is represented afterward by the matrix product \(\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3=i\I\).
In this realization the basis identities become
\begin{equation*}
\sigma_n^2=\I,
\qquad
\sigma_m\sigma_n=-\sigma_n\sigma_m\quad (m\neq n),
\qquad
\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3=i\I.
\end{equation*}
So Eq.\ \eqref{eq:pauli-matrices} gives a concrete \(2\times2\) model for the generators and their pseudoscalar.

For a real paravector \(X=t+\mathbf r\cdot\sigv\in\R\oplus\R^3\), with \(\mathbf r=(r_1,r_2,r_3)\in\R^3\), the matrix form is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
X&=t+\mathbf r\cdot\sigv
=\{ t+r_3, r_1-ir_2; r_1+ir_2, t-r_3 \},\\
\det X&=t^2-\mathbf r^2.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:matrix-X}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:matrix-X} reproduces exactly the real scalar paravector metric already encoded by Eq.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-metric}. The same matrix also carries the familiar trace and characteristic data:
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{tr}(X)&=2t,\\
\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}X)\lambda+\det X&=0,\\
\lambda(\pm)&=t\pm \lVert\mathbf r\rVert.
\end{align*}
These eigenvalues are the matrix version of the split into scalar part \(t\) and vector magnitude \(\lVert\mathbf r\rVert\).

Within the same realization, the matrix involutions can also be written explicitly. Let \((\cdot)^c\) denote entrywise complex conjugation and let \((\cdot)^{cT}\) denote complex conjugate transpose. Then
\begin{equation*}
i^c=-i,
\qquad
\sigma_1^c=\sigma_1,
\qquad
\sigma_2^c=-\sigma_2,
\qquad
\sigma_3^c=\sigma_3,
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
(\sigma_n)^{cT}&=\sigma_n,\\
(\sigma_n\sigma_m)^{cT}&=\sigma_m\sigma_n=-\sigma_n\sigma_m
\qquad (n\neq m),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
(i\I)^{cT}&=-i\I,\\
(i\sigma_n)^{cT}
&=\bigl((\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3)\sigma_n\bigr)^{cT}\\
&=\sigma_n^{cT}(\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3)^{cT}\\
&=-i\sigma_n,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
and therefore
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\I^{\mathrm R}&=\I,
&
\sigma_n^{\mathrm R}&=\sigma_n,\\
(i\I)^{\mathrm R}&=-i\I,
&
(i\sigma_n)^{\mathrm R}&=-i\sigma_n,\\
(\cdot)^{\mathrm R}&=(\cdot)^{cT}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
So in the matrix model the reverse operator coincides with the Hermitian adjoint. The sigma conjugation formulas are generated by the fixed matrix
\begin{equation*}
\Mmat:=\{0, 1; -1, 0\},
\qquad
\Mmat^{-1}=-\Mmat.
\end{equation*}
With this matrix one finds
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\I^{*}&=\I,
&
\sigma_n^{*}&=-\sigma_n,\\
(i\I)^{*}&=-i\I,
&
(i\sigma_n)^{*}&=i\sigma_n,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
(\cdot)^{*}&=\Mmat\,(\cdot)^{c}\,\Mmat^{-1},\\
(\cdot)^{*\mathrm R}=(\cdot)^{\mathrm R*}
&=\Mmat\,(\cdot)^{T}\,\Mmat^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Thus the matrix section now contains, in one place, the basis matrices, the spacetime paravector matrix, the determinant/trace/eigenvalue formulas, complex conjugation, complex-conjugate transpose, reverse, sigma conjugation, and reverse sigma conjugation. These concrete formulas match the abstract operator rules defined earlier in the algebraic development.

\subsubsection{Auxiliary \texorpdfstring{$2\times2$}{2x2} Realization}
It is also useful to package a complex paravector \(Z\in\C\oplus\C^3\) into the auxiliary \(2\times2\) map
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:W-map}
\Wmat(Z):=\{0, Z; \crev{Z}, 0\}.
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\Wmat(Z)^2=\mathrm{diag}(Z\crev{Z},\crev{Z}Z)
=\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2\I.
\label{eq:W-square}
\end{equation}
Here \(\Wmat(Z)\) is the block realization determined by \(Z\) and its reverse-conjugate. Equation \eqref{eq:W-square} shows that \(\Wmat\) converts the scalar paravector metric into a quadratic matrix identity: the square of \(\Wmat(Z)\) is the scalar \(\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2\) times the identity. The result is diagonal because the off-diagonal products reduce to the scalar paravector metric formulas already established in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:euclidean-form} and \eqref{eq:minkowski-form} \cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}.
