\section{Future Work}\label{sec:future-work}
The developed results constitute a flat-space reformulation of established
physics. Extensions not yet constructed are separated from points requiring
greater mathematical precision. A proposed extension is marked
\textbf{TODO}. A gap requiring resolution before physical claims can be
supported is marked \textbf{FIXME}. No premise for the established results is
taken from this section.

\subsection{TODO: Local Frames and Gravitation}
One fixed sigma basis is used throughout space and time in the present calculations.
A gravitational extension would instead need a position-dependent local
basis and a rule for comparing algebra elements defined at neighboring
positions.  That comparison rule would have to distinguish an ordinary
change of description from a physical gravitational field.  In particular,
a basis written everywhere as a single transform of a fixed basis is locally
only a change of description; curvature is not produced by it alone.

A complete construction would therefore require an independently specified
connection, a definition of curvature as the failure of successive local
transports to commute, and field equations by which that curvature is
determined from physical sources. The interval, freely falling motion, Maxwell
equation, Dirac system, and energy-momentum source map would then all have to
be rebuilt with the same local transport rule.  Agreement with the flat
formulas in the limit of a constant basis would be a required check, not an
additional assumption.

A moving-basis extension could be formed by combining translational motion
with rotation-rate and boost-rate cross-position terms, interpreting the
result as a velocity, multiplying it by mass to obtain energy and momentum,
and constructing a transformed interval. Those physical identifications are
not justified by the moving-basis generator derived in
Sec.\ \ref{sec:transform-algebra} alone. In particular, a
similarity-transported basis is not the event congruence used for a Lorentz
transformation, and a complex velocity-like expression is generally produced
by the proposed boost-rate term. For such a construction to be valid, a real
event law, an observer and its measured time and distance, and a covariant
derivative based on the local connection must first be defined. Real measured
velocity, energy, momentum, and interval must then be derived from those
definitions, and the inertial formulas must be recovered when the generator
is constant or vanishes.

Space-time curvature is also not yet supplied by a plane-curve analogy.
A numerator obtained by differentiating a coordinate quotient is only one
piece of a parameter-dependent curve formula.  Before it can be used here,
the tangent normalization, parameter choice, transport rule, and
multidirectional curvature test must all be specified.  Curvature cannot be
inferred from that numerator alone.

\subsection{TODO: Internal Gauge Symmetries}
One phase freedom and one potential paravector are used in the electromagnetic
coupling. Other interactions involve several coupled internal
field components whose transformations need not commute.  It remains to be
determined whether those transformations can be added while keeping the
space-time product and the internal action conceptually separate.

The allowed internal field values, their transformation rule, the
corresponding potentials, and the conserved source quantities would need to
be defined before any field equation is written. The products applied to
space-time paravectors and those applied only to the internal field components
would also have to be shown explicitly. Merely replacing the electromagnetic
potential by a larger array would not establish a consistent theory.

\subsection{TODO: Many-Particle and Quantized Fields}
Classical fields or first-quantized single-particle wave mechanics are
described by the wave equations in this manuscript. In particular, a
negative-frequency solution is not by itself an antiparticle. Field
operators, particle creation and removal, charge conjugation, and a
vacuum state must be introduced in a fuller theory, and the particle and
antiparticle interpretation must be derived from that structure.

The combination of several one-particle carrier spaces, imposition of
identical-particle exchange, representation of entangled and reduced states,
and extraction of local observables must be specified in the same
development.
The fixed projected column space used for one Dirac field is a useful
starting point, but these many-particle constructions are not supplied by it
alone.

The indefinite Klein--Gordon pairing and the absence of a positive local
one-particle position density are also not repaired by a change of algebraic
packaging. In a quantized-field treatment, the origin of its positive
state-space norm, charge operator, particles, and antiparticles must be shown,
and the one-particle formulas must be recovered in their proper regime.

\subsection{TODO: Media and Constitutive Maps}
Vacuum fields and prescribed sources are treated in the electromagnetic section.
A constitutive map is required for applications to material media: a locally
defined rule by which the electromagnetic field is converted into the
polarization, magnetization, current, or other response of the material. Anisotropy,
dispersion, conductivity, and dissipation generally make that rule depend
on direction, frequency, field history, or more than one input.

The notation-light approach should be tested first on simple linear media
and then on direction-dependent and time-dependent responses. The distinction
between a field paravector and an operator acting on that field must be
preserved by each proposed map, and its energy balance must be derived rather than
assumed. Material laws that fit naturally into one paravector product will
thereby be distinguished from those requiring a separately defined linear
map.

\subsection{FIXME: Operator Foundations}
Differential, inverse, and square-root operators are used in several
factorizations in the wave-mechanics sections. Their algebraic
forms are correct on the smooth test fields stated in the derivations, but
the allowed fields, boundary conditions, and behavior at infinity must be
specified in a complete analytic treatment. The conditions under which
integration by parts adds no boundary term, an inverse exists, and an energy
operator has real measured values are determined by those choices.

A consistent modewise definition of the square-root frequency operator,
including its branch and treatment of zero modes, is also required.
Sufficient regularity for both orders of operation to exist is required by
commutators containing derivatives. These conditions should eventually be stated
once in a common operator-domain section and then cited wherever they are used
in a factorization, commutator, or low-energy expansion.

Likewise, a formal expression such as the determinant of a block
differential operator minus a spectral parameter is not defined merely
because the paravector determinant is defined.  A characteristic determinant
may be used only after a Fourier-mode, finite-volume, or other finite-matrix
reduction has been specified.  At the operator level, the ordered
factorization and minimal-polynomial identities used in the manuscript are
the justified statements; additional analytic construction is required for a
spectral determinant.

For scalar relativistic waves, the positive-energy solution space for each
permitted stationary background must be constructed by a rigorous treatment,
and the measurement operators under which it is preserved must be determined.
General time-dependent
backgrounds can mix the frequency sectors, and relativistic localization
cannot be settled by declaring the local Klein--Gordon density to be a
probability. For Dirac and Pauli carriers, the same treatment must specify
the common domains on which the displayed \(H\)-adjoints, block operators,
spectral projectors, and expectation values are genuinely Hermitian.

\subsection{FIXME: Uncertainty Relations}
A spin uncertainty bound cannot be inferred directly from the axis
commutator and a determinant inequality. Such a calculation is not a
state-dependent variance inequality and is therefore not established as a
result. The normalized projected-wave bracket in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:adjoint-paravector-braket} must be used as the starting point,
centered Hermitian operators and their variances must be defined, and a common
domain on which both operator orders and their adjoints exist must be
required. The usual commutator bound,
and the stronger form that also retains the centered anticommutator, should
then be proved in the manuscript's \(\eqtext{com}()\) and
\(\eqtext{acom}()\) notation.  Axis observables should be checked as a worked
special case rather than treated as the proof.

\subsection{TODO: Higher-Order Pauli Reduction}
The leading kinetic and magnetic spin terms are proved by the present Dirac
reduction. In a controlled next-order expansion, the electric spin-orbit
term, the contact term from spatially varying electric fields, and the
corrections required for time-dependent potentials should be derived with the
same sign and charge conventions. The transformed density and current must be
expanded to the same order as the Hamiltonian; retaining higher-order energy
terms while leaving the measurement map at leading order would be
inconsistent.  These terms are established consequences of the Dirac
equation, but their derivation in this notation remains future work.

\subsection{TODO: Classify Companion Components}
Paired real and imaginary components and paired scalar and vector components
are repeatedly produced by the algebra. Some of these are familiar physical
laws: the magnetic-divergence and induction equations, for example, are
encoded by the imaginary parts of the Maxwell equation. Other components, such as the
imaginary part of the force product, are valid algebraic companions without
a separate physical interpretation established here.

Two cases are already identified with established physics: a projected
block's opposite-order product is the familiar rank-one two-state density
matrix, and the \(\pv{F}\) and \(\conj{\pv{F}}\) residuals in the ordered
products are the familiar two Dirac spin couplings. For the remaining
nonscalar channels of a generic
\(\herm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{\psi}\) sandwich, a separate current,
polarization, or measurement derivation is still required before physical
meaning is assigned.

The following exact identity may also be studied:
\begin{equation*}
\Box(\pv{F}^{\,2})
=\conj{\pv{\partial}}\pv{\partial}(\pv{F}^{\,2}).
\end{equation*}
Because \(\pv{F}^{\,2}\) is a complex scalar, this equality is an exact use
of the defined wave operator, but it is not an independent field equation.
Any useful source expansion must be derived from Maxwell's equation with the
ordered product rule of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:paravector-leibniz}.  Future work may
classify the resulting scalar identity as an invariant balance or a
redundant differential consequence; it must not be presented as new
dynamics without such a derivation.

Each companion formula should be placed by a systematic classification into
one of three categories: an alternate form of an established law, redundant
information implied by another displayed equation, or an algebraic channel
whose physical meaning is presently unassigned. The involutions, order
functions, projectors, and field bilinears should all be included in that
classification.  No unassigned component should be advertised as a new
interaction merely because it is made available by the algebra.
Automorphisms and projector, factorization, exponential, and logarithmic
constructions that are intrinsic to the algebra should be distinguished from
those dependent on the chosen matrix realization.

\subsection{TODO: Complex Space-Time and Imaginary Coordinates}
A complex paravector is already permitted by the generic algebra:
\(\pv{Z}=\pv{X}+i\pv{Y}\), with \(\pv{X}\) and \(\pv{Y}\) real
paravectors. Treating such an element as a physical event, rather than as an
algebraic field value, would be a new hypothesis. The following self-contained
consistency identity is obtained from the existing determinant and adjugate
definitions:
\begin{equation*}
\detf{\pv{Z}}
=\detf{\pv{X}}-\detf{\pv{Y}}
+i\Bigl(
  \pv{X}\adjf{\pv{Y}}
 +\pv{Y}\adjf{\pv{X}}
\Bigr).
\end{equation*}
The quantity in parentheses is a scalar, but the determinant of
\(\pv{Z}\) is generally complex. No interval interpretation is fixed by this
identity. In a proposed complex-event theory, it would have to be stated which,
if any, real quantity is measured as an interval and why that choice is
preserved by the allowed transformations.

The scalar part of \(\pv{Y}\) could be described as an imaginary-time
coordinate, and its vector part could be described as imaginary space.
Those descriptions alone carry no physical content. A complex space-time
metric would require a specified real measurement map, a nondegenerate
interval, and a transformation law that preserves that interval. Taking the
real part, magnitude, or positive norm of a complex determinant would produce
different geometries and therefore cannot be selected by terminology alone.
The ordinary real interval would have to be recovered when the imaginary
part vanishes.

Only after that choice is justified could proper time, causal order,
velocity, momentum, or force be defined for a complex event. It would also
have to be explained why real events and the usual light cone are recovered
by ordinary experiments to their observed accuracy. Complex mass, hidden
trajectories, or new force channels should not be introduced before these
more basic questions are resolved.

Faster-than-light propagation is not implied by complex coordinates. A
propagation speed can be assigned only after field equations, the surfaces
along which disturbances propagate, observers, and measured time and distance
have been defined. If
the measured projection remains the real paravector \(\pv{X}\), the ordinary
light cone may remain unchanged. If \(\pv{Y}\) changes the propagation
characteristics, the resulting causal cone and the possibility of
controllable signaling would have to be derived explicitly.

Mixed terms in \(\detf{\pv{Z}}\) show an algebraic relationship between the
real and imaginary parts, but they do not establish a weak physical
interaction. Such an interaction would require a dynamical equation and a
source law. Only then could empirical parameters be identified. Possible
parameters could include a real--imaginary mixing strength, a mass or length
scale suppressing the imaginary sector, and a relative propagation or decay
scale. None of these constants is predicted by the algebra alone; each would
have to be connected to a definite observable and constrained
experimentally.

The algebraic complexification cannot by itself be falsified because it is a
mathematical extension. A proposed physical interpretation could be rejected
if measured intervals or energies became unavoidably complex, probabilities
failed to remain real and nonnegative, time evolution failed to preserve
normalization, source-free solutions grew without bound, or predicted departures
from the observed light cone and ordinary Lorentz behavior exceeded
experimental limits. It remains to be determined whether a nontrivial
complex-event dynamics can pass these basic tests or whether every
consistent observable sector reduces to the real-paravector theory already
developed.

\subsection{FIXME: Dark-Sector and Cosmological Interpretations}
No derivation of vacuum energy, accelerated cosmic expansion, missing
gravitational mass, or any other dark-sector phenomenon is established in
this manuscript. A gravitational, clustering, or observable-energy role is
not implied by the existence of imaginary algebra components. Such
interpretations remain contingent on tests that would have to be passed by a
future model.

A consistent local gravitational equation and a conserved physical source
must first be supplied by any such model. Ordinary local gravity must then be
recovered while quantitative predictions are made for cosmic expansion,
gravitational lensing, structure growth, and the motion of matter. A nearly
constant large-scale contribution and a clustering matter-like contribution
are physically different requirements and should not be inferred from the
same algebraic term without separate derivations.

\subsection{TODO: Limits of the Notation-Light Form}
The compact notation is most effective when the relevant scalar and vector
data are carried by one algebra element. In some extensions, maps with several
independent inputs, information that is valid only on overlapping local
regions, or global conditions that cannot be recovered from one local formula
are instead required. That mathematical structure is not removed by avoidance of indices;
only its recorded form is changed.

The conditions under which a paravector is sufficient, a linear map on
paravectors is required, or several-input or global data must be introduced
should be stated explicitly. Any new notation should be justified by a construction
that cannot be expressed clearly with the existing functions and operators.
The raw-text character of the framework is thereby preserved while
notational economy is prevented from being mistaken for a reduction in
physical content.
