\subsubsection{Electricity and Magnetism}
The compact field definitions in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:F-Phi} and \eqref{eq:maxwell} are expanded into explicit scalar, vector, and bivector parts. Rationalized Heaviside--Lorentz units are used, with \(c=1\). Here \(\rho,S\in\R\), \(\vct{E},\vct{B},\vct{J}\in\R^3\), \(\vct{F}=\vct{E}+i\vct{B}\in\C^3\), and \(\pv{F}=\vct{F}\cdot\sigv\) is a pure-vector complex paravector, so \(\eqtext{scalar}(\pv{F})=0\). The spatial derivative conventions are
\begin{align*}
\gradv &= \{\partial_1,\partial_2,\partial_3\}, \\
\partial_i &= \partial/\partial r_i, \\
\gradv^2 &= \partial_1^2+\partial_2^2+\partial_3^2,
\end{align*}
and the differential paravector is
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial}
&= \partial_t+\sigv\cdot\gradv\notag\\
&= \partial_t+\pv{\sigma}_1\partial_1+\pv{\sigma}_2\partial_2+\pv{\sigma}_3\partial_3,\\
\pv{\partial}^{*}
&= \partial_t-\sigv\cdot\gradv.
\end{align*}
These conventions are restatements of Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:gradient-notation}, \eqref{eq:partial-operator}, and \eqref{eq:partial-conjugate}; no new definitions are introduced. The generic real/imaginary scalar/vector decomposition of \(\pv{\partial}\pv{F}\) and \(i\pv{\partial}\pv{F}\) has already been recorded in Subsection \ref{subsec:differential-complex-fields}. With that operator bookkeeping fixed, the electric and magnetic fields can be combined without introducing any new multiplication rule.

The electric and magnetic fields are collected into the complex three-vector \(\vct{F}\) and the field paravector \(\pv{F}\):
\begin{align*}
\vct{E} &= \{E_{1}, E_{2}, E_{3}\} && \text{real vector electric field},\notag \\
\vct{B} &= \{B_{1}, B_{2}, B_{3}\} && \text{real vector magnetic field},\notag \\
\vct{F} &= \vct{E} + i \vct{B} && \text{electromagnetic field},\notag \\
\pv{F} &= \vct{F} \cdot \sigv = (\vct{E} + i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv, && i = \pv{\sigma}_{1}\pv{\sigma}_{2}\pv{\sigma}_{3}.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
\pv{F}^{*} &= (-\vct{E} + i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&= -\vct{F}^{*} \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
The two simplest quadratic field combinations are given by
\begin{align*}
\tfrac12\pv{F}^2
&=\tfrac12(\vct{E}^2-\vct{B}^2)+i\,\vct{E}\cdot\vct{B},\\
\tfrac12\pv{F}\pv{F}^{\mathsf H}
&=\tfrac12(\vct{E}^2+\vct{B}^2)+(\vct{E}\times\vct{B})\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
Thus the proper-Lorentz invariants are contained in \(\tfrac12\pv{F}^2\), while \(\tfrac12\pv{F}\pv{F}^{\mathsf H}\) is separated into the energy density and the Poynting vector. Under parity, the sign of \(\vct{E}\cdot\vct{B}\) is changed, and this quantity is therefore a pseudoscalar rather than a scalar invariant of the full Lorentz group.

The real source paravector is defined by
\begin{equation}
\pv{D}:=\rho+\vct{J}\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:source-paravector}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\pv{D}\in\R+\R^3\).
Its sigma conjugate is
\begin{equation*}
\pv{D}^{*}=\rho-\vct{J}\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
Here \(\rho\in\R\) is the scalar charge density and \(\vct{J}\in\R^3\) is the current-density vector. Maxwell's equations are then written as
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial}\pv{F} &= \pv{D}^{*}\qquad \text{Maxwell's equations},\notag \\
&= \rho - \vct{J} \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\partial}^{*} \pv{F}^{*} = \pv{D}.
\end{equation*}
In this form, the four classical Maxwell equations are reproduced in one step by the scalar, sigma-number-one, and sigma-number-two parts of \(\pv{\partial}\pv{F}\). The location of each component is shown by expansion of the product.

The following expression is obtained by expansion of \(\pv{\partial}\pv{F}\) into scalar, pseudoscalar, vector, and bivector parts:
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial}\pv{F} &= (\partial _{t} + \sigv \cdot \gradv) ( (\vct{E} + i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&= ( \gradv \cdot \vct{E} ),\notag \\
&\quad + ( \gradv \cdot \vct{B} ) i,\notag \\
&\quad + (\partial _{t} \vct{E} - \gradv \times \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&\quad + (\partial _{t} \vct{B} + \gradv \times \vct{E}) \cdot (i \sigv ).
\end{align*}
The first two lines are the scalar and pseudoscalar parts. The last two lines are the sigma-number-one and sigma-number-two vector parts. By comparison with \(\pv{D}^*=\rho-\vct{J}\cdot\sigv\), the magnetic-divergence term and Faraday term are immediately forced to vanish, while the electric source terms are fixed.

Accordingly, the component equations of \(\pv{\partial}\pv{F}=\rho-\vct{J}\cdot\sigv\) are
\begin{align*}
\gradv \cdot \vct{E} &= \rho, \\
\gradv \cdot \vct{B} &= 0, \\
\partial_t \vct{E} - \gradv \times \vct{B} &= -\vct{J}, \\
\partial_t \vct{B} + \gradv \times \vct{E} &= 0.
\end{align*}

The following relations are then obtained by separation of the vector components:
\begin{align*}
\gradv \times \vct{B} &= \vct{J} + \partial _{t} \vct{E}, \\
\gradv \times \vct{E} &= -\partial _{t} \vct{B}.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Lorentz Transform of Fields}
The boost law is specialized to the electromagnetic field paravector
\begin{equation*}
\pv{F}=(\vct{E}+i\vct{B})\cdot\sigv .
\end{equation*}
For a constant inertial boost
\(\pv{L}=\exp(\tfrac12\theta\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)\) with \(\vct{u}^{2}=1\), \(\beta=\tanh(\theta)\), and \(\gamma=\cosh(\theta)\), the field is transformed by the general similarity \eqref{eq:general-field-similarity}, even though event and current paravectors are transformed by the congruence \eqref{eq:general-event-congruence}. In the inverse-first convention the left factor is \(\pv{L}^{-1}=\conj{\pv{L}}\), so the field is transformed as
\[
\pv{F}'=\pv{L}^{-1}\pv{F}\pv{L}=\conj{\pv{L}}\pv{F}\pv{L}.
\]
The final factor is \(\pv{L}\), rather than \(\conj{\pv{L}}\), because the field is assigned
the similarity \(\pv{T}^{-1}(\,\cdot\,)\pv{T}\). With a double-\(\conj{\pv{L}}\)
sandwich, the event congruence would instead be applied to \(\pv{F}\), and its
pure-vector form would not be preserved. For example, if
\(\pv{F}=a\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv\) for a real scalar amplitude \(a\), then
\[
\conj{\pv{L}}\pv{F}\pv{L}=\pv{F},
\qquad
\conj{\pv{L}}\pv{F}\conj{\pv{L}}
=\gamma a\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv-\gamma\beta a,
\]
and an inadmissible scalar part is contained in the second expression. By
application of the general complex-vector result
\eqref{eq:hyperbolic-similarity} to
\(\vct{V}=\vct{E}+i\vct{B}\), the field and its
\(\eqtext{par}\) and \(\eqtext{prp}\) parts are obtained:
\begin{align*}
\pv{F}' &= \conj{\pv{L}}\pv{F}\pv{L}, \\
\eqtext{par}( \vct{E} ' ) &= \eqtext{par}( \vct{E} ), \\
\eqtext{par}( \vct{B} ' ) &= \eqtext{par}( \vct{B} ), \\
\eqtext{prp}( \vct{E} ' ) &= \gamma ( \eqtext{prp}( \vct{E} ) + \beta \vct{u} \times \vct{B} ), \\
\eqtext{prp}( \vct{B} ') &= \gamma ( \eqtext{prp}( \vct{B} ) - \beta \vct{u} \times \vct{E} ).
\end{align*}
Equivalently, the transformed fields can be written in the compact form
\begin{align*}
\vct{E} ' &= \eqtext{par}(\vct{E}) + \gamma ( \eqtext{prp}(\vct{E}) + \beta \vct{u} \times \vct{B} ), \\
\vct{B} ' &= \eqtext{par}(\vct{B}) + \gamma ( \eqtext{prp}(\vct{B}) - \beta \vct{u} \times \vct{E} ).
\end{align*}
The parallel components are unchanged, while the perpendicular components are mixed by the standard electric-magnetic cross terms.
Because \(\pv{L}\pv{L}^*=1\) and \(\pv{F}^2\) is central, the field invariants are directly preserved:
\begin{equation*}
\pv{F}'^2=\pv{L}^*\pv{F}(\pv{L}\pv{L}^*)\pv{F}\pv{L}
=\pv{L}^*\pv{F}^2\pv{L}=\pv{F}^2.
\end{equation*}
Thus the real part of \(\pv{F}^2\) is a scalar and its imaginary part is a pseudoscalar under parity; both are preserved by proper Lorentz transformations.

For any constant normalized Lorentz element \(\pv{T}\), the potential and source
are event-type paravectors, while the complementary action is carried by the
derivative:
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\Phi}'=\pv{T}^{*\mathsf H}\pv{\Phi}\pv{T}^*,
\qquad
\pv{D}'=\pv{T}^{*\mathsf H}\pv{D}\pv{T}^*,
\qquad
\pv{\partial}'=\pv{T}^{\mathsf H}\pv{\partial}\pv{T}.
\end{equation*}
For the boost \(\pv{T}=\pv{L}\), these expressions are reduced to
\[
\pv{\Phi}'=\pv{L}^*\pv{\Phi}\pv{L}^*,
\qquad
\pv{D}'=\pv{L}^*\pv{D}\pv{L}^*,
\qquad
\pv{\partial}'=\pv{L}\pv{\partial}\pv{L}.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial}'\pv{F}'
&=(\pv{T}^{\mathsf H}\pv{\partial}\pv{T})
(\pv{T}^{*\mathsf H}\pv{F}\pv{T})\notag\\
&=\pv{T}^{\mathsf H}(\pv{\partial}\pv{F})\pv{T}\notag\\
&=\pv{T}^{\mathsf H}\pv{D}^*\pv{T}\notag\\
&=\pv{D}'^*.
\end{align*}
Thus covariance of Maxwell's equation is established. In components, the current transformation is
\begin{align*}
\rho'&=\gamma\bigl(\rho-\beta\,\vct{u}\cdot\vct{J}\bigr),\\
\eqtext{par}(\vct{J}')
&=\gamma\bigl(\eqtext{par}(\vct{J})-\beta\rho\vct{u}\bigr),\\
\eqtext{prp}(\vct{J}')&=\eqtext{prp}(\vct{J}).
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Wave Operator and d'Alembertian}
The operator conventions were fixed in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:partial-operator}--\eqref{eq:dalembertian}. They are used only in the derivation of the sourced wave consequences and in the distinction between the scalar wave operator \(\Box\) and the full square \(\pv{\partial}^2\):
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial} &= \partial _{t} + \sigv \cdot \gradv, \\
\pv{\partial}^{*} &= \partial _{t} - \sigv \cdot \gradv, \\
\Box &= \partial _{t}^{2} - \gradv^{2} \\
&= \pv{\partial}\pv{\partial}^{*} = \pv{\partial}^{*}\pv{\partial}.
\end{align*}
These are the previously defined identities \eqref{eq:partial-operator}--\eqref{eq:dalembertian}, restated for the calculation.
The following relation is also obtained from the sigma algebra:
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\partial}^{2} = \partial _{t}^{2} + \gradv^{2} + 2 \partial _{t} \gradv \cdot \sigv,
\end{equation*}
so \(\pv{\partial}^2\neq\Box\). With the conventions of Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:partial-operator}--\eqref{eq:dalembertian}, the following result is obtained by applying \(\pv{\partial}^*\) to Maxwell's equation:
\begin{align*}
\Box \pv{F}
&=\pv{\partial}^*\pv{\partial}\pv{F}\notag\\
&=\pv{\partial}^*(\rho-\vct{J}\cdot\sigv)\notag\\
&=(\partial_t\rho+\gradv\cdot\vct{J})\notag\\
&\quad+\bigl(-\partial_t\vct{J}-\gradv\rho
+i\gradv\times\vct{J}\bigr)\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
The companion relation is obtained by applying \(\pv{\partial}\) to the
star-conjugate Maxwell equation \(\pv{\partial}^*\pv{F}^*=\pv{D}\):
\begin{equation*}
\Box\pv{F}^*=\pv{\partial}\pv{D}
=\pv{\partial}(\rho+\vct{J}\cdot\sigv),
\end{equation*}
which is Eq.\ \eqref{eq:wave-F-conjugate} in the compact development.
Because \(\pv{F}\) is pure vector, the scalar part of the left-hand side vanishes. The scalar and vector parts are therefore given by
\begin{align*}
\partial_t\rho+\gradv\cdot\vct{J}&=0,\\
\Box\vct{E}&=-\partial_t\vct{J}-\gradv\rho,\\
\Box\vct{B}&=\gradv\times\vct{J}.
\end{align*}
In vacuum, both source terms vanish and the ordinary wave equation is satisfied by each field component.

In vacuum, where \(\rho=0\) and \(\vct{J}=0\), the following reduction is
obtained:
\begin{align*}
\Box \vct{E} &= 0
\quad\eqtext{implies}\quad
\partial _{t}^{2} \vct{E} = \gradv^{2} \vct{E}, \\
\Box \vct{B} &= 0
\quad\eqtext{implies}\quad
\partial _{t}^{2} \vct{B} = \gradv^{2} \vct{B}.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Potential Fields}
Gauge freedom, source reconstruction, and the electric/magnetic field split are collected in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:potential-gradient} and \eqref{eq:E-B-from-potential}. Here \(V,S,\rho,\lambda\in\R\), \(\vct{A},\vct{E},\vct{B},\vct{J}\in\R^3\), \(\pv{\Phi}\in\R+\R^3\), \(\vct{F}\in\C^3\), and \(\pv{F}\) is the pure-vector complex paravector defined previously:
\begin{align*}
\pv{F}^{*} &= (-\vct{E} + i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
\pv{\Phi} &= V + \vct{A} \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} &= \partial _{t} V + \gradv \cdot \vct{A},\notag \\
&\quad + ( \partial _{t} \vct{A} + \gradv V ) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&\quad + i ( \gradv \times \vct{A} ) \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
The potential gradient is separated by this expansion into one real scalar part and one pure-vector field part. The scalar piece is identified with the Lorenz-gauge scalar \(S\), and the remaining vector piece is identified with the electromagnetic field:

\begin{align*}
S &= \eqtext{scalar}( \pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} ),\notag \\
&= \partial _{t} V + \gradv \cdot \vct{A}.
\end{align*}
The definition of \(S\) in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:potential-gradient} is thereby recovered. The component identities extracted from \(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}\) are
\begin{align*}
\vct{E} &= - \partial _{t} \vct{A} - \gradv V, \\
\vct{B} &= \gradv \times \vct{A}.
\end{align*}
The identity is explicit:
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} &= S + \pv{F}^{*}, \\
\pv{F}^{*} &= \pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} - S, \\
\eqtext{scalar}(\pv{F}^{*}) &= \eqtext{scalar}(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} - S) = 0, \\
\eqtext{vector}(\pv{F}^{*}) &= \eqtext{vector}( \pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} ).
\end{align*}
The field is therefore the non-scalar part of \(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}\). Under the displayed gauge change, only the scalar part of \(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}\) is changed; the field paravector remains unchanged.

The differential product \(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}\) is evaluated first; the involutions are then applied to the resulting field. No right-acting differential operator is implied.
Equivalently, without introducing \(S\), the relation can be written as
\begin{equation*}
\pv{F}^{*}
=\bigl(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}
-(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi})^{*\mathsf H}\bigr)/2.
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Density from Potential}
The derivation of the charge density and current density directly from the potential field is collected in one place.
From the previously defined potential \(\pv{\Phi}=V+\vct{A}\cdot\sigv\), scalar part \(S=\partial_t V+\gradv\cdot\vct{A}\), and identity \(\pv{F}^{*}=\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}-S\), the following relation is obtained:
\begin{align*}
\pv{F} &= \pv{\partial}^{*} \pv{\Phi}^{*} - S,\notag \\
\pv{\partial}\pv{F}
&= \pv{\partial}\pv{\partial}^{*}\pv{\Phi}^{*}
- \pv{\partial} S,\notag \\
&= \Box \pv{\Phi}^{*} - \pv{\partial} S,\notag \\
&= ( \Box V - \partial _{t} S) + (- \Box \vct{A} - \gradv S) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&= \rho - \vct{J} \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
\rho &= \Box V - \partial _{t} S, \\
\vct{J} &= \Box \vct{A} + \gradv S.
\end{align*}
These are the component source-reconstruction formulas contained in the compact Maxwell equation. Charge density is determined from the potential data by the scalar equation, and current density is determined from the same data by the vector equation.

In Lorenz gauge, where \(S=0\), these relations reduce to
\begin{align*}
\partial _{t} V &= -\gradv \cdot \vct{A}, \\
\rho &= \Box V, \\
\vct{J} &= \Box \vct{A}.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Gauge Transform of the Potential}
\begin{align*}
\eqtext{scalar}( \pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} ) &= S, \\
S &= \partial _{t} V + \gradv \cdot \vct{A}.
\end{align*}
A real scalar gauge function \(\lambda\) is introduced. A gauge change is
distinguished from the boost primes used above by function inputs:
The potential and its components defined in
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:gauge}, \eqref{eq:gauge-scalar-potential}, and
\eqref{eq:gauge-vector-potential} are given by
\begin{align*}
\pv{\Phi}(\lambda)
&=\pv{\Phi}+\pv{\partial}^*\lambda,\\
V(\lambda)&=V+\partial_t\lambda,\\
\vct{A}(\lambda)&=\vct{A}-\gradv\lambda.
\end{align*}
The transformed potential gradient is defined separately by
\begin{equation}
(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi})(\lambda)
:=\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}(\lambda).
\label{eq:gauge-potential-gradient}
\end{equation}
By direct differentiation, the following relation is obtained:
\begin{equation*}
(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi})(\lambda)
=\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}+\Box\lambda.
\end{equation*}
Since \(\Box\lambda\) is again a scalar field, only the scalar part is changed.
Its transformed value is defined by
\begin{equation}
S(\lambda):=S+\Box\lambda.
\label{eq:gauge-scalar-part}
\end{equation}
The field invariance already defined in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:gauge-field} is
\begin{equation*}
\pv{F}(\lambda)=\pv{F}.
\end{equation*}
The gauge split is thereby made explicit. The residual condition \(\Box\lambda=0\) is satisfied by transformations that preserve Lorenz gauge.

\subsubsection{Four-Force}
The relativistic work law, the Lorentz-force law, the component split of \(\pv{F}\pv{U}/\gamma\), and the four-force equation are collected in one place.
\begin{align*}
m \pv{U} &= m\frac{\dd \pv{X}}{\dd s},\notag \\
&= m \gamma + m \gamma \vct{v} \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd}{\dd s}=\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}.
\end{equation*}
The scalar part of \(\dd(m\pv{U})/\dd t\) is the power equation and the vector part is the three-force equation. The usual split is made visible without abandonment of the relativistic form by writing both pieces in one paravector equation.

The relativistic work law is given by the time component,
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}=q\vct{v}\cdot\vct{E}.
\end{equation*}
Here \(\vct{E}\) denotes the electric field, not the energy operator \(E\), and no work term is contributed by the magnetic field in the scalar component. The relativistic Lorentz force law is given by the spatial component,
\begin{align*}
\vct{P} &= m \gamma \vct{v}, \\
\frac{\dd\vct{P}}{\dd t} &= q \vct{E} + q \vct{v} \times \vct{B}.
\end{align*}
Together these are assembled into
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd(m\pv{U})}{\dd t}
=q\vct{v}\cdot\vct{E}
+q(\vct{E}+\vct{v}\times\vct{B})\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
The paravector content is exposed explicitly by the expansion
\begin{align*}
\pv{F}\pv{U} / \gamma &= ((\vct{E} + i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv ) ( 1 + \vct{v} \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&= \vct{E} \cdot \sigv + i \vct{B} \cdot \sigv + (\vct{E} \cdot \sigv ) (\vct{v} \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&\quad + i (\vct{B} \cdot \sigv )(\vct{v} \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&= \vct{E} \cdot \sigv + i \vct{B} \cdot \sigv + \vct{E} \cdot \vct{v},\notag \\
&\quad + i (\vct{E} \times \vct{v}) \cdot \sigv + i \vct{B} \cdot \vct{v} - (\vct{B} \times \vct{v}) \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
The product \(\pv{F}\pv{U}\) is naturally separated into real and imaginary parts. The physical Lorentz-force content is reproduced by the real part, while electric and magnetic roles are exchanged by the imaginary part, which is therefore not used in the force law itself.
\input{sections/em_tables_force.tex}

\begin{align*}
\eqtext{real}( \pv{F}\pv{U} )
&= \tfrac{1}{2} ( \pv{F}\pv{U} + \pv{U}\pv{F}^{\mathsf H} ),\notag \\
&= \gamma (\vct{v} \cdot \vct{E} + ( \vct{E} + \vct{v} \times \vct{B} ) \cdot \sigv ).
\end{align*}
The physical four-force is therefore
\begin{align*}
m\frac{\dd \pv{U}}{\dd s}
&= q \eqtext{real}( \pv{F}\pv{U} ),\notag \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2} q \big( \pv{F}\pv{U} + \pv{U}\pv{F}^{\mathsf H} \big).
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
m\frac{\dd^2\pv{X}}{\dd s^{2}}
=q\,\eqtext{real}\!\left(\pv{F}\frac{\dd \pv{X}}{\dd s}\right)
\qquad \text{four-force}.
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Lagrangian Derivation of the Lorentz Force}
The minimally coupled action, the Euler--Lagrange derivation of the Lorentz force, the Hamiltonian, and the corresponding power identity are collected in one place.
From the Lagrangian of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:em-lagrangian}, together with the
potential \(\pv{\Phi}=V+\vct{A}\cdot\sigv\), four-velocity
\(\pv{U}=\dd \pv{X}/\dd s\), and \(\gamma=\dd t/\dd s\), the action is
obtained in the form
\begin{align*}
\text{action}
&=-m\int\dd s-q\int\eqtext{scalar}(\pv{\Phi}^*\pv{U})\,\dd s\notag\\
&=\int\left[-\frac{m}{\gamma}
-q\,\eqtext{scalar}\!\left((V-\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)
(1+\vct{v}\cdot\sigv)\right)\right]\dd t\notag\\
&=\int\left(-\frac{m}{\gamma}-qV+q\vct{v}\cdot\vct{A}\right)\dd t.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{L}=-\frac{m}{\gamma}-q(V-\vct{v}\cdot\vct{A}).
\end{equation*}
This is the standard minimal-coupling Lagrangian written in the present paravector notation. The canonical momentum is shifted by the vector potential, exactly as in the ordinary component derivation.

The canonical momentum is
\begin{align*}
\delv{} \mathcal{L} &= m \gamma \vct{v} + q \vct{A},\notag \\
&= \vct{P} + q \vct{A}.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\delv{} \mathcal{L} &= \frac{\dd\vct{P}}{\dd t} + q \partial _{t} \vct{A} + q (\vct{v} \cdot \gradv) \vct{A}, \\
\gradv \mathcal{L} &= - q \gradv V + q \gradv (\vct{v} \cdot \vct{A}).
\end{align*}
The vector-calculus identity
\begin{equation*}
(\vct{v}(t) \cdot \gradv) \vct{A}(t, \vct{r}) = \gradv ( \vct{v} \cdot \vct{A} ) - \vct{v} \times ( \gradv \times \vct{A} ).
\end{equation*}
is used to obtain the following result from the Euler--Lagrange equation
\((\dd/\dd t)\delv{} \mathcal{L} = \gradv \mathcal{L}\):
\begin{align*}
\frac{\dd\vct{P}}{\dd t} &= - q \gradv V - q \partial _{t} \vct{A},\notag \\
&\quad + q ( \gradv (\vct{v} \cdot \vct{A}) - (\vct{v} \cdot \gradv) \vct{A} ),\notag \\
&= q ( -\gradv V - \partial _{t}\vct{A} + \vct{v} \times ( \gradv \times \vct{A} ) ),\notag \\
&= q ( \vct{E} + \vct{v} \times \vct{B} ),\notag \\
&= \frac{m}{\gamma}\eqtext{vector}\!\left(\frac{\dd \pv{U}}{\dd s}\right).
\end{align*}
The Hamiltonian is
\begin{align*}
\mathcal H
&=(\delv{}\mathcal L)\cdot\vct{v}-\mathcal L\notag\\
&=(m\gamma\vct{v}+q\vct{A})\cdot\vct{v}
+\frac{m}{\gamma}+qV-q\vct{v}\cdot\vct{A}\notag\\
&=m\gamma\left(\vct{v}^2+\frac{1}{\gamma^2}\right)+qV\notag\\
&=m\gamma+qV.
\end{align*}
Thus \(\mathcal H\) is given by the mechanical relativistic energy plus the scalar-potential energy. Along a solution,
\begin{align*}
\frac{\dd\mathcal H}{\dd t}
&=-\partial_t\mathcal L
=q\partial_tV-q\vct{v}\cdot\partial_t\vct{A},\\
\frac{\dd\mathcal H}{\dd t}
&=\frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}
+q\partial_tV+q\vct{v}\cdot\gradv V.
\end{align*}
The following relation is obtained by equating these expressions:
\begin{align*}
\frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}
&=q\vct{v}\cdot(-\gradv V-\partial_t\vct{A})\notag\\
&=q\vct{v}\cdot\vct{E},
\end{align*}
in agreement with the scalar component of the Lorentz-force equation.

\subsubsection{Energy-Density and Poynting Paravector}
The electromagnetic energy density and Poynting vector are packaged into one paravector by Eq.\ \eqref{eq:energy-momentum-paravector}. Its components and the scalar energy balance implied by Maxwell's equation \eqref{eq:maxwell} are recorded by the displayed algebra:

\begin{align*}
\pv{F} &= (\vct{E} + i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
\pv{F}^{\mathsf H} &= (\vct{E} - i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
\pv{T} &= \tfrac{1}{2} \pv{F}\pv{F}^{\mathsf H},\notag \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2} (\vct{E}^{2} + \vct{B}^{2}) + (\vct{E} \times \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&= T_{s} + \vct{T}_{\vct{v}} \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\partial} = \partial _{t} + \sigv \cdot \gradv.
\end{equation*}
The paravector \(\pv{T}=\tfrac12\pv{F}\herm{\pv{F}}\) is the energy density and flux measured relative to the chosen time direction. It is not, by itself, the full electromagnetic stress-energy tensor. The covariant content is represented by the following function of a real paravector \(\pv{a}\in\R+\R^3\):
\begin{equation}
\pv{T}(\pv{a})
:=\frac12\pv{F}\,\pv{a}\,\herm{\pv{F}}.
\label{eq:stress-energy-map}
\end{equation}
The argument-free notation is reserved for its value at the identity:
\[
\pv{T}=\pv{T}(1)=\frac12\pv{F}\herm{\pv{F}}.
\]
Thus the displayed energy-density and flux paravector is the
\(\pv{a}=1\) value of the full stress-energy function
\cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}.
Indeed, for \(\pv{a}'=\pv{L}^*\pv{a}\pv{L}^*\),
\(\pv{F}'=\pv{L}^*\pv{F}\pv{L}\), and
\((\pv{F}')^{\mathsf H}=\pv{L}\pv{F}^{\mathsf H}\pv{L}^*\),
\begin{equation}
\pv{T}'(\pv{a}')
:=\frac12\pv{F}'\pv{a}'(\pv{F}')^{\mathsf H}.
\label{eq:transformed-stress-energy-map}
\end{equation}
By substitution of the transformed field and argument, the covariance law is
obtained:
\begin{equation}
\pv{T}'(\pv{a}')
=\pv{L}^*\left(\frac12\pv{F}\pv{a}\pv{F}^{\mathsf H}\right)\pv{L}^*
=\pv{L}^*\pv{T}(\pv{a})\pv{L}^*.
\label{eq:stress-energy-map-covariance}
\end{equation}
so the required event-paravector covariance is satisfied by the function.

The scalar energy balance is obtained immediately from Maxwell's equations:
\begin{align*}
\partial_tT_s+\gradv\cdot \vct{T}_{\vct{v}}
&=\vct{E}\cdot\partial_t\vct{E}
+\vct{B}\cdot\partial_t\vct{B}
+\gradv\cdot(\vct{E}\times\vct{B})\notag\\
&=-\vct{J}\cdot\vct{E}.
\end{align*}
This is Poynting's theorem in the present units: the field-energy loss equals the work done on charge.
