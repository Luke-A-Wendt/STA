\subsubsection{Electricity and Magnetism}
The compact field definitions in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:F-Phi} and
\eqref{eq:maxwell} are expanded into the algebra parts already defined.
Rationalized Heaviside--Lorentz units are used, with \(c=1\)
\cite{jackson1998}. Here \(\rho\in\R\),
\(\vct{E},\vct{B},\vct{J}\in\R^3\),
\(\vct{F}=\vct{E}+i\vct{B}\in\C^3\), and
\(\pv{F}=\vct{F}\cdot\sigv\) is a pure-vector complex paravector, so
\(\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{F})=0\). The spatial derivative conventions are
\begin{align*}
\gradv &= \vthree{\partial_1}{\partial_2}{\partial_3}, \\
\partial_n &= \partial/\partial r_n,\qquad n\in\{1,2,3\}, \\
\gradv^2 &= \partial_1^2+\partial_2^2+\partial_3^2,
\end{align*}
and the differential paravector is
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial}
&= \partial_t+\sigv\cdot\gradv\notag\\
&= \partial_t+\pv{\sigma}_1\partial_1+\pv{\sigma}_2\partial_2+\pv{\sigma}_3\partial_3,\\
\pv{\partial}^{*}
&= \partial_t-\sigv\cdot\gradv.
\end{align*}
These conventions restate Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:gradient-notation},
\eqref{eq:partial-operator}, and \eqref{eq:partial-conjugate}. The
real/imaginary scalar/vector decomposition of
\(\pv{\partial}\pv{Z}\) and \(i\pv{\partial}\pv{Z}\) for an arbitrary
complex field \(\pv{Z}\) was recorded in Subsection
\ref{subsec:differential-complex-fields}. The electric and magnetic fields
can therefore be inserted into the same multiplication rule.

The usual electric and magnetic fields \cite{maxwell1865,jackson1998} are
collected into the complex three-vector \(\vct{F}\) and the field
paravector \(\pv{F}\):
\begin{align*}
\vct{E} &= \vthree{E_{1}}{E_{2}}{E_{3}}
&& \text{real vector electric field},\notag \\
\vct{B} &= \vthree{B_{1}}{B_{2}}{B_{3}}
&& \text{real vector magnetic field},\notag \\
\vct{F} &= \vct{E} + i \vct{B} && \text{electromagnetic field},\notag \\
\pv{F} &= \vct{F} \cdot \sigv = (\vct{E} + i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv, && i = \pv{\sigma}_{1}\pv{\sigma}_{2}\pv{\sigma}_{3}.
\end{align*}
Its sigma conjugate is obtained as
\begin{align*}
\pv{F}^{*} &= (-\vct{E} + i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&= -\vct{F}^{*} \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
The two simplest quadratic field combinations are given by
\begin{align*}
\tfrac12\pv{F}^2
&=\tfrac12(\vct{E}^2-\vct{B}^2)+i\,\vct{E}\cdot\vct{B},\\
\tfrac12\pv{F}\pv{F}^{\Hop}
&=\tfrac12(\vct{E}^2+\vct{B}^2)+(\vct{E}\times\vct{B})\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
The two coefficients in \(\tfrac12\pv{F}^2\) are unchanged by the boosts
and rotations generated continuously from the identity. These
transformations preserve both spatial orientation and the chosen direction
of increasing time; they are the proper, time-orientation-preserving Lorentz
transformations used in
this document. They do not include a spatial reflection
\(\vct{r}\mapsto-\vct{r}\), conventionally called parity. Under parity the
electric field changes sign and the magnetic field does not, so
\(\vct{E}\cdot\vct{B}\) changes sign
\cite{jackson1998}. It is therefore the coefficient of the algebra
pseudoscalar \(i\), rather than an ordinary scalar under reflection.

The second product separates into
\(\tfrac12(\vct{E}^{2}+\vct{B}^{2})\), the electromagnetic energy per unit
volume, and \(\vct{E}\times\vct{B}\), the energy crossing a unit area per
unit time in the chosen frame and units. The latter is conventionally
called the Poynting vector \cite{maxwell1865,jackson1998}.

The real source paravector is defined by
\begin{equation}
\pv{D}:=\rho+\vct{J}\cdot\sigv.
\label{eq:source-paravector}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\pv{D}\in\RPspace\).
Its sigma conjugate is
\begin{equation*}
\pv{D}^{*}=\rho-\vct{J}\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
Here \(\rho\in\R\) is charge per unit volume, called charge density, and
\(\vct{J}\in\R^3\) is charge flow per unit area per unit time, called
current density. Maxwell's equations are then written as
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial}\pv{F} &= \pv{D}^{*}\qquad \text{Maxwell's equations},\notag \\
&= \rho - \vct{J} \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
The companion equation is obtained by sigma conjugation:
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\partial}^{*} \pv{F}^{*} = \pv{D}.
\end{equation*}
In this form, the four classical Maxwell equations are reproduced in one step by the scalar, sigma-number-one, and sigma-number-two parts of \(\pv{\partial}\pv{F}\). The location of each component is shown by expansion of the product.

The following expression is obtained by expansion of
\(\pv{\partial}\pv{F}\) into the four algebra parts:
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial}\pv{F} &= (\partial _{t} + \sigv \cdot \gradv) ( (\vct{E} + i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&= ( \gradv \cdot \vct{E} ),\notag \\
&\quad + ( \gradv \cdot \vct{B} ) i,\notag \\
&\quad + (\partial _{t} \vct{E} - \gradv \times \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&\quad + (\partial _{t} \vct{B} + \gradv \times \vct{E}) \cdot (i \sigv ).
\end{align*}
The first two lines are the real-scalar and \(i\)-scalar parts. The last two
lines are the sigma-number-one and sigma-number-two vector parts. Comparison
with \(\pv{D}^*=\rho-\vct{J}\cdot\sigv\) forces the second and fourth
parts to vanish and fixes the first and third parts.

Accordingly, the component equations of \(\pv{\partial}\pv{F}=\rho-\vct{J}\cdot\sigv\) are
\begin{align*}
\gradv \cdot \vct{E} &= \rho, \\
\gradv \cdot \vct{B} &= 0, \\
\partial_t \vct{E} - \gradv \times \vct{B} &= -\vct{J}, \\
\partial_t \vct{B} + \gradv \times \vct{E} &= 0.
\end{align*}

The following relations are then obtained by separation of the vector components:
\begin{align*}
\gradv \times \vct{B} &= \vct{J} + \partial _{t} \vct{E}, \\
\gradv \times \vct{E} &= -\partial _{t} \vct{B}.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Lorentz Transform of Fields}
The boost law is specialized to the electromagnetic field paravector
\begin{equation*}
\pv{F}=(\vct{E}+i\vct{B})\cdot\sigv .
\end{equation*}
For the boost
\(\pv{L}=\exp(\tfrac12\theta\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)\), the unit vector
\(\vct{u}\) gives the boost direction, \(\beta=\tanh(\theta)\) is its
signed speed, and \(\gamma=\cosh(\theta)\) is the Lorentz factor. The boost
is constant here, so none of these quantities depends on position or time.
The field law is
\[
\pv{F}'=\pv{L}^{-1}\pv{F}\pv{L}=\conj{\pv{L}}\pv{F}\pv{L}.
\]
An inverse on the left and the original element on the right define the
similarity transformation of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:general-field-similarity}.
This differs from the event transformation
\(\pv{T}^{-1}(\,\cdot\,)(\pv{T}^{-1})^{\Hop}\) in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:general-event-congruence}. The final field factor must
therefore be \(\pv{L}\), not \(\conj{\pv{L}}\). A pair of
\(\conj{\pv{L}}\) factors would apply the event law to \(\pv{F}\), and the
result would no longer be a pure-vector paravector. For example, if
\(\pv{F}=a\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv\) for a real scalar amplitude \(a\), then
\[
\conj{\pv{L}}\pv{F}\pv{L}=\pv{F},
\qquad
\conj{\pv{L}}\pv{F}\conj{\pv{L}}
=\gamma a\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv-\gamma\beta a,
\]
and a scalar part appears in the second expression. Application of the
general complex-vector result
\eqref{eq:hyperbolic-similarity} to
\(\vct{V}=\vct{E}+i\vct{B}\), the field and its
\(\eqfunc{par}\) and \(\eqfunc{prp}\) parts are obtained:
\begin{align*}
\pv{F}' &= \conj{\pv{L}}\pv{F}\pv{L}, \\
\eqfunc{par}( \vct{E} ' ) &= \eqfunc{par}( \vct{E} ), \\
\eqfunc{par}( \vct{B} ' ) &= \eqfunc{par}( \vct{B} ), \\
\eqfunc{prp}( \vct{E} ' ) &= \gamma ( \eqfunc{prp}( \vct{E} ) + \beta \vct{u} \times \vct{B} ), \\
\eqfunc{prp}( \vct{B} ') &= \gamma ( \eqfunc{prp}( \vct{B} ) - \beta \vct{u} \times \vct{E} ).
\end{align*}
Equivalently, the transformed fields can be written in the compact form
\begin{align*}
\vct{E} ' &= \eqfunc{par}(\vct{E}) + \gamma ( \eqfunc{prp}(\vct{E}) + \beta \vct{u} \times \vct{B} ), \\
\vct{B} ' &= \eqfunc{par}(\vct{B}) + \gamma ( \eqfunc{prp}(\vct{B}) - \beta \vct{u} \times \vct{E} ).
\end{align*}
The parallel components are unchanged, while the perpendicular components are mixed by the standard electric-magnetic cross terms.
Because \(\pv{L}\pv{L}^*=1\) and \(\pv{F}^2\) commutes with every algebra
element, the quadratic field combination is preserved:
\begin{equation*}
\pv{F}'^2=\pv{L}^*\pv{F}(\pv{L}\pv{L}^*)\pv{F}\pv{L}
=\pv{L}^*\pv{F}^2\pv{L}=\pv{F}^2.
\end{equation*}
Thus both coefficients of \(\pv{F}^2\) are preserved by the proper,
time-orientation-preserving Lorentz transformations constructed here. Under
the spatial reflection defined
above, its real coefficient is unchanged and its \(i\)-coefficient changes
sign.

For any constant Lorentz element \(\pv{T}\) satisfying
\(\pv{T}^{-1}=\pv{T}^{*\Hop}\), the real potential and source are
transformed by the same two-sided rule as event paravectors. The derivative
is transformed by the complementary factors required for its product with
the field:
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\Phi}'=\pv{T}^{*\Hop}\pv{\Phi}\pv{T}^*,
\qquad
\pv{D}'=\pv{T}^{*\Hop}\pv{D}\pv{T}^*,
\qquad
\pv{\partial}'=\pv{T}^{\Hop}\pv{\partial}\pv{T}.
\end{equation*}
For the boost \(\pv{T}=\pv{L}\), these expressions are reduced to
\[
\pv{\Phi}'=\pv{L}^*\pv{\Phi}\pv{L}^*,
\qquad
\pv{D}'=\pv{L}^*\pv{D}\pv{L}^*,
\qquad
\pv{\partial}'=\pv{L}\pv{\partial}\pv{L}.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial}'\pv{F}'
&=(\pv{T}^{\Hop}\pv{\partial}\pv{T})
(\pv{T}^{*\Hop}\pv{F}\pv{T})\notag\\
&=\pv{T}^{\Hop}(\pv{\partial}\pv{F})\pv{T}\notag\\
&=\pv{T}^{\Hop}\pv{D}^*\pv{T}\notag\\
&=\pv{D}'^*.
\end{align*}
The first and last lines have the same form,
\(\pv{\partial}'\pv{F}'=\pv{D}'^*\), as the unprimed equation
\(\pv{\partial}\pv{F}=\pv{D}^*\). This preservation of the equation's form
when every quantity is transformed is what is meant here by covariance. In
components, the source transformation is
\begin{align*}
\rho'&=\gamma\bigl(\rho-\beta\,\vct{u}\cdot\vct{J}\bigr),\\
\eqfunc{par}(\vct{J}')
&=\gamma\bigl(\eqfunc{par}(\vct{J})-\beta\rho\vct{u}\bigr),\\
\eqfunc{prp}(\vct{J}')&=\eqfunc{prp}(\vct{J}).
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Wave Operator}
The operator conventions were fixed in
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:partial-operator}--\eqref{eq:dalembertian}. The product of
the differential paravector and its conjugate is the scalar operator
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial} &= \partial _{t} + \sigv \cdot \gradv, \\
\pv{\partial}^{*} &= \partial _{t} - \sigv \cdot \gradv, \\
\Box &= \partial _{t}^{2} - \gradv^{2} \\
&= \pv{\partial}\pv{\partial}^{*} = \pv{\partial}^{*}\pv{\partial}.
\end{align*}
This scalar operator is conventionally called the d'Alembertian
\cite{maxwell1865,jackson1998}.
The following relation is also obtained from the sigma algebra:
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\partial}^{2} = \partial _{t}^{2} + \gradv^{2} + 2 \partial _{t} \gradv \cdot \sigv,
\end{equation*}
so \(\pv{\partial}^2\neq\Box\). With the conventions of Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:partial-operator}--\eqref{eq:dalembertian}, the following result is obtained by applying \(\pv{\partial}^*\) to Maxwell's equation:
\begin{align*}
\Box \pv{F}
&=\pv{\partial}^*\pv{\partial}\pv{F}\notag\\
&=\pv{\partial}^*(\rho-\vct{J}\cdot\sigv)\notag\\
&=(\partial_t\rho+\gradv\cdot\vct{J})\notag\\
&\quad+\bigl(-\partial_t\vct{J}-\gradv\rho
+i\gradv\times\vct{J}\bigr)\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
The companion relation is obtained by applying \(\pv{\partial}\) to the
star-conjugate Maxwell equation \(\pv{\partial}^*\pv{F}^*=\pv{D}\):
\begin{equation*}
\Box\pv{F}^*=\pv{\partial}\pv{D}
=\pv{\partial}(\rho+\vct{J}\cdot\sigv),
\end{equation*}
which is Eq.\ \eqref{eq:wave-F-conjugate} in the compact development.
Because \(\pv{F}\) is pure vector, the scalar part of the left-hand side vanishes. The scalar and vector parts are therefore given by
\begin{align*}
\partial_t\rho+\gradv\cdot\vct{J}&=0,\\
\Box\vct{E}&=-\partial_t\vct{J}-\gradv\rho,\\
\Box\vct{B}&=\gradv\times\vct{J}.
\end{align*}
A region with no charge density and no current density,
\(\rho=0\) and \(\vct{J}=0\), is called vacuum here. In such a region each
field component satisfies the ordinary wave equation:
\begin{align*}
\Box \vct{E} &= 0
\quad\eqtext{implies}\quad
\partial _{t}^{2} \vct{E} = \gradv^{2} \vct{E}, \\
\Box \vct{B} &= 0
\quad\eqtext{implies}\quad
\partial _{t}^{2} \vct{B} = \gradv^{2} \vct{B}.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Potential Fields}
A real scalar field \(V\) and a real vector field \(\vct{A}\) are collected
into the real potential paravector
\(\pv{\Phi}=V+\vct{A}\cdot\sigv\). Direct multiplication by the
differential paravector gives
\begin{align*}
\pv{F}^{*} &= (-\vct{E} + i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
\pv{\Phi} &= V + \vct{A} \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} &= \partial _{t} V + \gradv \cdot \vct{A},\notag \\
&\quad + ( \partial _{t} \vct{A} + \gradv V ) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&\quad + i ( \gradv \times \vct{A} ) \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
This expansion contains one real scalar part and one pure-vector complex
part. The scalar part is assigned the short name \(S\):

\begin{align*}
S &= \eqfunc{scalar}( \pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} ),\notag \\
&= \partial _{t} V + \gradv \cdot \vct{A}.
\end{align*}
The component identities extracted from the pure-vector part are
\begin{align*}
\vct{E} &= - \partial _{t} \vct{A} - \gradv V, \\
\vct{B} &= \gradv \times \vct{A}.
\end{align*}
The identity is explicit:
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} &= S + \pv{F}^{*}, \\
\pv{F}^{*} &= \pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} - S, \\
\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{F}^{*}) &= \eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} - S) = 0, \\
\eqfunc{vector}(\pv{F}^{*}) &= \eqfunc{vector}( \pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} ).
\end{align*}
The electromagnetic field is therefore exactly the non-scalar part of
\(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}\). A change of potential that alters only \(S\)
and leaves this field unchanged is derived below.
\input{sections/em_tables_potential.tex}

The differential product \(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}\) is evaluated first; the involutions are then applied to the resulting field. No right-acting differential operator is implied.
Equivalently, without introducing \(S\), the relation can be written as
\begin{equation*}
\pv{F}^{*}
=\bigl(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}
-(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi})^{*\Hop}\bigr)/2.
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Sources from the Potential}
Charge and current can be recovered directly from the potential.
From the previously defined potential \(\pv{\Phi}=V+\vct{A}\cdot\sigv\), scalar part \(S=\partial_t V+\gradv\cdot\vct{A}\), and identity \(\pv{F}^{*}=\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}-S\), the following relation is obtained:
\begin{align*}
\pv{F} &= \pv{\partial}^{*} \pv{\Phi}^{*} - S,\notag \\
\pv{\partial}\pv{F}
&= \pv{\partial}\pv{\partial}^{*}\pv{\Phi}^{*}
- \pv{\partial} S,\notag \\
&= \Box \pv{\Phi}^{*} - \pv{\partial} S,\notag \\
&= ( \Box V - \partial _{t} S) + (- \Box \vct{A} - \gradv S) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&= \rho - \vct{J} \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
The source components are therefore obtained as
\begin{align*}
\rho &= \Box V - \partial _{t} S, \\
\vct{J} &= \Box \vct{A} + \gradv S.
\end{align*}
Thus charge density is determined by the scalar equation and current density
by the vector equation.

The condition \(S=0\), or
\(\partial_tV+\gradv\cdot\vct{A}=0\), is conventionally called the Lorenz
gauge \cite{jackson1998}. Under this condition the relations reduce to
\begin{align*}
\partial _{t} V &= -\gradv \cdot \vct{A}, \\
\rho &= \Box V, \\
\vct{J} &= \Box \vct{A}.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Gauge Transform of the Potential}
The previously defined scalar potential condition is recalled first:
\begin{align*}
\eqfunc{scalar}( \pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi} ) &= S, \\
S &= \partial _{t} V + \gradv \cdot \vct{A}.
\end{align*}
An arbitrary real scalar field \(\lambda\) is chosen. A second potential and
its components are defined by
\begin{align*}
\pv{\Phi}(\lambda)
&=\pv{\Phi}+\pv{\partial}^*\lambda,\\
V(\lambda)&=V+\partial_t\lambda,\\
\vct{A}(\lambda)&=\vct{A}-\gradv\lambda.
\end{align*}
Function inputs are used here so that this change is not confused with the
prime used for a change of inertial frame. The potential gradient is defined
separately by
\begin{equation}
(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi})(\lambda)
:=\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}(\lambda).
\label{eq:gauge-potential-gradient}
\end{equation}
By direct differentiation, the following relation is obtained:
\begin{equation*}
(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi})(\lambda)
=\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}+\Box\lambda.
\end{equation*}
Since \(\Box\lambda\) is again a scalar field, only the scalar part is
changed. Its transformed value was defined in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:gauge-scalar-part}:
\begin{equation*}
S(\lambda)=S+\Box\lambda.
\end{equation*}
The unchanged field is obtained by substitution:
\begin{align*}
\conj{\pv{F}(\lambda)}
&=\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}(\lambda)-S(\lambda)\notag\\
&=\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}+\Box\lambda-S-\Box\lambda\notag\\
&=\conj{\pv{F}}.
\end{align*}
The unchanged field stated in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:gauge-field} therefore follows:
\begin{equation*}
\pv{F}(\lambda)=\pv{F}.
\end{equation*}
Thus different potentials related by the displayed \(\lambda\) change
produce exactly the same electric and magnetic fields. Such a change is
called a gauge transformation, and the existence of these physically
equivalent potentials is called gauge freedom \cite{jackson1998}. If
\(S=0\) before and after the change, then
\(\Box\lambda=0\); this is the complete condition for the displayed change
to preserve the Lorenz gauge.

\subsubsection{Four-Force}
The energy--momentum paravector and the relation between coordinate time and
proper time are
\begin{align*}
m \pv{U} &= m\frac{\dd \pv{X}}{\dd s},\notag \\
&= m \gamma + m \gamma \vct{v} \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
The corresponding derivative conversion is
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd}{\dd s}=\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}.
\end{equation*}
The scalar part of \(\dd(m\pv{U})/\dd t\) is the rate of change of energy,
or power. Its vector part is the rate of change of momentum, or
three-force.

The scalar equation is
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}=q\vct{v}\cdot\vct{E}.
\end{equation*}
Here \(\vct{E}\) denotes the electric field. This equation states that the
energy gained per unit time equals charge times electric field dotted with
velocity; it is the relativistic work law. No magnetic term appears because
\(\vct{v}\cdot(\vct{v}\times\vct{B})=0\). The vector equation is
\begin{align*}
\vct{P} &= m \gamma \vct{v}, \\
\frac{\dd\vct{P}}{\dd t} &= q \vct{E} + q \vct{v} \times \vct{B}.
\end{align*}
The right side is conventionally called the Lorentz force
\cite{lorentz1904,jackson1998}. Together, the scalar and vector equations
are assembled into
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd(m\pv{U})}{\dd t}
=q\vct{v}\cdot\vct{E}
+q(\vct{E}+\vct{v}\times\vct{B})\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
The paravector content is exposed explicitly by the expansion
\begin{align*}
\pv{F}\pv{U} / \gamma &= ((\vct{E} + i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv ) ( 1 + \vct{v} \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&= \vct{E} \cdot \sigv + i \vct{B} \cdot \sigv + (\vct{E} \cdot \sigv ) (\vct{v} \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&\quad + i (\vct{B} \cdot \sigv )(\vct{v} \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&= \vct{E} \cdot \sigv + i \vct{B} \cdot \sigv + \vct{E} \cdot \vct{v},\notag \\
&\quad + i (\vct{E} \times \vct{v}) \cdot \sigv + i \vct{B} \cdot \vct{v} - (\vct{B} \times \vct{v}) \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
The product \(\pv{F}\pv{U}\) separates into real and imaginary parts.
Four-force is a real paravector, so the real projection is required. It
contains exactly the work and force terms displayed above; the remaining
imaginary components are not part of the electromagnetic force law.
\input{sections/em_tables_force.tex}

The required real projection is therefore obtained as
\begin{align*}
\eqfunc{real}( \pv{F}\pv{U} )
&= \tfrac{1}{2} ( \pv{F}\pv{U} + \pv{U}\pv{F}^{\Hop} ),\notag \\
&= \gamma (\vct{v} \cdot \vct{E} + ( \vct{E} + \vct{v} \times \vct{B} ) \cdot \sigv ).
\end{align*}
This real paravector also has the orthogonality required by
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:proper-acceleration-orthogonality}:
\begin{align*}
\eqfunc{scalar}\bigl(\conj{\pv{U}}\,
\eqfunc{real}(\pv{F}\pv{U})\bigr)
&=\gamma^2\bigl(\vct{v}\cdot\vct{E}
-\vct{v}\cdot(\vct{E}+\vct{v}\times\vct{B})\bigr)\notag\\
&=0.
\end{align*}
The rate of change of four-momentum with respect to proper time is called
four-force. It is therefore
\begin{align*}
m\frac{\dd \pv{U}}{\dd s}
&= q \eqfunc{real}( \pv{F}\pv{U} ),\notag \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2} q \big( \pv{F}\pv{U} + \pv{U}\pv{F}^{\Hop} \big).
\end{align*}
The equivalent event-path form is
\begin{equation*}
m\frac{\dd^2\pv{X}}{\dd s^{2}}
=q\,\eqfunc{real}\!\left(\pv{F}\frac{\dd \pv{X}}{\dd s}\right)
\qquad \text{four-force}.
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Action Derivation of the Lorentz Force}
The free-particle action is supplemented by a scalar product of charge,
potential, and four-velocity. From Eq.\ \eqref{eq:em-lagrangian}, together
with the
potential \(\pv{\Phi}=V+\vct{A}\cdot\sigv\), four-velocity
\(\pv{U}=\dd \pv{X}/\dd s\), and \(\gamma=\dd t/\dd s\), the action is
obtained in the form
\begin{align*}
\text{action}
&=-m\int\dd s-q\int\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{\Phi}^*\pv{U})\,\dd s\notag\\
&=\int\left[-\frac{m}{\gamma}
-q\,\eqfunc{scalar}\!\left((V-\vct{A}\cdot\sigv)
(1+\vct{v}\cdot\sigv)\right)\right]\dd t\notag\\
&=\int\left(-\frac{m}{\gamma}-qV+q\vct{v}\cdot\vct{A}\right)\dd t.
\end{align*}
The coordinate-time Lagrangian is therefore
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{L}=-\frac{m}{\gamma}-q(V-\vct{v}\cdot\vct{A}).
\end{equation*}
Adding the potential through these terms is conventionally called minimal
electromagnetic coupling \cite{jackson1998,greiner2000}. In the present
notation, it means exactly the displayed addition to the free-particle
Lagrangian.

Differentiation with respect to velocity gives the canonical momentum:
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\vct{v}}
&= m \gamma \vct{v} + q \vct{A},\notag \\
&= \vct{P} + q \vct{A}.
\end{align*}
Its time derivative and spatial gradient are
\begin{align*}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\vct{v}}\right)
&= \frac{\dd\vct{P}}{\dd t} + q \partial _{t} \vct{A}
+ q (\vct{v} \cdot \gradv) \vct{A}, \\
\gradv \mathcal{L} &= - q \gradv V + q \gradv (\vct{v} \cdot \vct{A}).
\end{align*}
The directly verified vector identity
\begin{equation*}
(\vct{v}(t) \cdot \gradv) \vct{A}(t, \vct{r}) = \gradv ( \vct{v} \cdot \vct{A} ) - \vct{v} \times ( \gradv \times \vct{A} ).
\end{equation*}
holds because \(\vct{v}(t)\) has no spatial dependence when
\(\gradv\) is applied. It is used in the Euler--Lagrange equation
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\vct{v}}\right)
=\gradv\mathcal L.
\end{equation*}
The result is
\begin{align*}
\frac{\dd\vct{P}}{\dd t} &= - q \gradv V - q \partial _{t} \vct{A},\notag \\
&\quad + q ( \gradv (\vct{v} \cdot \vct{A}) - (\vct{v} \cdot \gradv) \vct{A} ),\notag \\
&= q ( -\gradv V - \partial _{t}\vct{A} + \vct{v} \times ( \gradv \times \vct{A} ) ),\notag \\
&= q ( \vct{E} + \vct{v} \times \vct{B} ),\notag \\
&= \frac{m}{\gamma}\eqfunc{vector}\!\left(\frac{\dd \pv{U}}{\dd s}\right).
\end{align*}
The Hamiltonian defined in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:hamiltonian-definition} is
\begin{align*}
\mathcal H
&=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\vct{v}}\cdot\vct{v}
-\mathcal L\notag\\
&=(m\gamma\vct{v}+q\vct{A})\cdot\vct{v}
+\frac{m}{\gamma}+qV-q\vct{v}\cdot\vct{A}\notag\\
&=m\gamma\left(\vct{v}^2+\frac{1}{\gamma^2}\right)+qV\notag\\
&=m\gamma+qV.
\end{align*}
Thus \(\mathcal H\) is given by the mechanical relativistic energy plus the scalar-potential energy. Along a solution,
\begin{align*}
\frac{\dd\mathcal H}{\dd t}
&=-\partial_t\mathcal L
=q\partial_tV-q\vct{v}\cdot\partial_t\vct{A},\\
\frac{\dd\mathcal H}{\dd t}
&=\frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}
+q\partial_tV+q\vct{v}\cdot\gradv V.
\end{align*}
The following relation is obtained by equating these expressions:
\begin{align*}
\frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}
&=q\vct{v}\cdot(-\gradv V-\partial_t\vct{A})\notag\\
&=q\vct{v}\cdot\vct{E},
\end{align*}
in agreement with the scalar component of the Lorentz-force equation.

\subsubsection{Energy-Density and Poynting Paravector}
The scalar energy per unit volume and the vector energy flow per unit area
per unit time are packaged into one paravector by direct multiplication:

\begin{align*}
\pv{F} &= (\vct{E} + i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
\pv{F}^{\Hop} &= (\vct{E} - i \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
\pv{T} &= \tfrac{1}{2} \pv{F}\pv{F}^{\Hop},\notag \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2} (\vct{E}^{2} + \vct{B}^{2})
+ (\vct{E} \times \vct{B}) \cdot \sigv,\\
\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{T})&=\tfrac12(\vct{E}^2+\vct{B}^2),\qquad
\eqfunc{vector}(\pv{T})=\vct{E}\times\vct{B}.
\end{align*}
The differential operator used below is recalled as
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\partial} = \partial _{t} + \sigv \cdot \gradv.
\end{equation*}
The paravector
\(\pv{T}=\tfrac12\pv{F}\herm{\pv{F}}\in\RPspace\) is the energy density and
flow measured relative to the scalar time direction represented by the
identity \(1\). To obtain the corresponding energy--momentum output for an
arbitrary real input direction \(\pv{a}\in\RPspace\), the function
\begin{equation}
\pv{T}(\pv{a})
:=\frac12\pv{F}\,\pv{a}\,\herm{\pv{F}}.
\label{eq:stress-energy-map}
\end{equation}
is used. Its scalar and vector output gives the energy and momentum flow
associated with the selected direction \(\pv{a}\). Taken for every input
direction, this linear function contains the energy density, momentum
density, energy flow, and momentum flow. Momentum flow is also called
electromagnetic stress, or force per unit area. This complete collection is
conventionally called the electromagnetic stress-energy tensor
\cite{hestenes1966,jackson1998,doran2003}.

The time-direction input reproduces the energy-density and flow paravector:
\[
\pv{T}(1)=\tfrac12(\vct{E}^2+\vct{B}^2)
+(\vct{E}\times\vct{B})\cdot\sigv.
\]
For a purely spatial input
\(\pv{a}=\vct{a}\cdot\sigv\), direct multiplication gives
\begin{align*}
\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{T}(\pv{a}))
&=-\vct{a}\cdot(\vct{E}\times\vct{B}),\\
\eqfunc{vector}(\pv{T}(\pv{a}))
&=\vct{E}(\vct{E}\cdot\vct{a})
+\vct{B}(\vct{B}\cdot\vct{a})
-\tfrac12(\vct{E}^2+\vct{B}^2)\vct{a}.
\end{align*}
The first line is the energy flow through the selected spatial direction,
with the sign fixed by the one-positive, three-negative interval
convention. The second line supplies the corresponding momentum flow.

The argument-free notation is reserved for its value at the identity:
\[
\pv{T}=\pv{T}(1)=\frac12\pv{F}\herm{\pv{F}}.
\]
Thus the displayed energy-density and flow paravector is one output of the
full function, not the full collection by itself.
Indeed, for \(\pv{a}'=\pv{L}^*\pv{a}\pv{L}^*\),
\(\pv{F}'=\pv{L}^*\pv{F}\pv{L}\), and
\((\pv{F}')^{\Hop}=\pv{L}\pv{F}^{\Hop}\pv{L}^*\),
\begin{equation}
\pv{T}'(\pv{a}')
:=\frac12\pv{F}'\pv{a}'(\pv{F}')^{\Hop}.
\label{eq:transformed-stress-energy-map}
\end{equation}
Substitution of the transformed field and input gives
\begin{equation}
\pv{T}'(\pv{a}')
=\pv{L}^*\left(\frac12\pv{F}\pv{a}\pv{F}^{\Hop}\right)\pv{L}^*
=\pv{L}^*\pv{T}(\pv{a})\pv{L}^*.
\label{eq:stress-energy-map-covariance}
\end{equation}
Thus the output transforms by the same event-paravector rule as its input.
Equivalently, the function has the same relation between physical inputs and
outputs in both frames.

The scalar energy balance is obtained immediately from Maxwell's equations:
\begin{align*}
\partial_t\eqfunc{scalar}(\pv{T})
+\gradv\cdot\eqfunc{vector}(\pv{T})
&=\vct{E}\cdot\partial_t\vct{E}
 +\vct{B}\cdot\partial_t\vct{B}\notag\\
&\quad+\gradv\cdot(\vct{E}\times\vct{B})\notag\\
&=-\vct{J}\cdot\vct{E}.
\end{align*}
The equation states that any decrease of field energy in a region is
accounted for by energy flowing through its boundary and work done on
charge. This local energy balance is conventionally called Poynting's
theorem \cite{jackson1998}. The two quadratic field products and their
components are collected below. The quantities preserved by the proper

chosen frame are displayed without adding a field equation for
