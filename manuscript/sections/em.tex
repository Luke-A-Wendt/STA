\subsubsection{Electricity and Magnetism}
This subsection collects the differential notation, the electromagnetic field paravector, its simplest quadratic combinations, and the component form of Maxwell's equation in one place. Here \(\rho,S\in\R\), \(\mathbf E,\mathbf B,\mathbf J\in\R^3\), \(\mathbf F=\mathbf E+i\mathbf B\in\C^3\), and \(F=\mathbf F\cdot\sigv\in\varnothing\oplus\C^3\). The spatial derivative conventions are
\begin{align*}
\gradv &= \{\partial_1,\partial_2,\partial_3\}, \\
\partial_i &:= \partial/\partial r_i, \\
\gradv^2 &= \partial_1^2+\partial_2^2+\partial_3^2,
\end{align*}
and the differential paravector is
\begin{align*}
\partial
&= \partial_t+\sigv\cdot\gradv\notag\\
&= \partial_t+\sigma_1\partial_1+\sigma_2\partial_2+\sigma_3\partial_3,\\
\partial^*
&= \partial_t-\sigv\cdot\gradv.
\end{align*}
The generic real/imaginary scalar/vector decomposition of \(\partial F\) and \(i\partial F\) has already been recorded in Subsection \ref{subsec:differential-complex-fields}. With that operator bookkeeping fixed, the electric and magnetic fields can be combined without introducing any new multiplication rule.

Collect the electric and magnetic fields into the complex three-vector \(\mathbf F\) and the field paravector \(F\):
\begin{align*}
\mathbf E &= \{E_{1}, E_{2}, E_{3}\} && \text{real vector electric field},\notag \\
\mathbf B &= \{B_{1}, B_{2}, B_{3}\} && \text{real vector magnetic field},\notag \\
\mathbf F &= \mathbf E + i \mathbf B && \text{electromagnetic field},\notag \\
F &= \mathbf F \cdot \sigv = (\mathbf E + i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv, && i = \sigma _{1}\sigma _{2}\sigma _{3}.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
F^{*} &= (-\mathbf E + i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&= -\mathbf F^{*} \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
The two simplest quadratic field combinations are
\begin{align*}
\tfrac12F^2
&=\tfrac12(\mathbf E^2-\mathbf B^2)+i\,\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B,\\
\tfrac12F F^{\mathrm R}
&=\tfrac12(\mathbf E^2+\mathbf B^2)+(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
Thus \(\tfrac12F^2\) contains the Lorentz invariants, while \(\tfrac12F F^{\mathrm R}\) separates into the energy density and the Poynting vector.

Define the real source paravector by
\begin{align*}
D &= \rho + \mathbf J \cdot \sigv \in \R\oplus\R^3, \\
D^{*} &= \rho - \mathbf J \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
Here \(\rho\in\R\) is the scalar charge density and \(\mathbf J\in\R^3\) is the current-density vector. Maxwell's equations then read
\begin{align*}
\partial F &= D^{*}\qquad \text{Maxwell's equations},\notag \\
&= \rho - \mathbf J \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
\partial ^{*} F^{*} = D.
\end{equation*}
In this form, the scalar, sigma-number-one, and sigma-number-two parts of \(\partial F\) reproduce the four classical Maxwell equations in one step. Expanding the product shows where each component sits.

Expanding \(\partial F\) into scalar, vector, and bivector parts gives
\begin{align*}
\partial F &= (\partial _{t} + \sigv \cdot \gradv) ( (\mathbf E + i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&= ( \gradv \cdot \mathbf E ),\notag \\
&\quad + ( \gradv \cdot \mathbf B ) i,\notag \\
&\quad + (\partial _{t} \mathbf E - \gradv \times \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&\quad + (\partial _{t} \mathbf B + \gradv \times \mathbf E) \cdot (i \sigv ).
\end{align*}
The first two lines are the scalar and pseudoscalar parts. The last two lines are the sigma-number-one and sigma-number-two vector parts. Matching these components with \(D^*=\rho-\mathbf J\cdot\sigv\) immediately forces the magnetic-divergence term and Faraday term to vanish while fixing the electric source terms.

Accordingly, the component equations of \(\partial F=\rho-\mathbf J\cdot\sigv\) are
\begin{align*}
\gradv \cdot \mathbf E &= \rho, \\
\gradv \cdot \mathbf B &= 0, \\
\partial_t \mathbf E - \gradv \times \mathbf B &= -\mathbf J, \\
\partial_t \mathbf B + \gradv \times \mathbf E &= 0.
\end{align*}

Separating the vector components then gives
\begin{align*}
\gradv \times \mathbf B &= \mathbf J + \partial _{t} \mathbf E, \\
\gradv \times \mathbf E &= -\partial _{t} \mathbf B.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Lorentz Transform of Fields}
This subsection specializes the boost law to the electromagnetic field paravector
\begin{equation*}
F=(\mathbf E+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv .
\end{equation*}
For a boost
\(L=\exp(\tfrac12\theta\,\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)\) with \(\mathbf u^{2}=1\), \(\beta=\tanh(\theta)\), and \(\gamma=\cosh(\theta)\), the transformed field and its parallel/perpendicular parts relative to \(\mathbf u\) obey
\begin{align*}
F' &= L^{*} F L, \\
\eqtext{par}( \mathbf E ' ) &= \eqtext{par}( \mathbf E ), \\
\eqtext{par}( \mathbf B ' ) &= \eqtext{par}( \mathbf B ), \\
\eqtext{prp}( \mathbf E ' ) &= \gamma ( \eqtext{prp}( \mathbf E ) + \beta \mathbf u \times \mathbf B ), \\
\eqtext{prp}( \mathbf B ') &= \gamma ( \eqtext{prp}( \mathbf B ) - \beta \mathbf u \times \mathbf E ).
\end{align*}
Equivalently, the transformed fields can be written in the compact form
\begin{align*}
\mathbf E ' &= \eqtext{par}(\mathbf E) + \gamma ( \eqtext{prp}(\mathbf E) + \beta \mathbf u \times \mathbf B ), \\
\mathbf B ' &= \eqtext{par}(\mathbf B) + \gamma ( \eqtext{prp}(\mathbf B) - \beta \mathbf u \times \mathbf E ), \\
\partial ' &= L^{*} \partial L, \\
\partial ' F' &= L^{*} (\rho - \mathbf J \cdot \sigv ) L.
\end{align*}
The parallel components are unchanged, while the perpendicular components mix by the standard electric-magnetic cross terms. This is the component form of the single algebraic boost law \(F'=\conj{L}FL\), together with the induced transformed operator relation \(\partial'F'=L^*(\rho-\mathbf J\cdot\sigv)L\).

\subsubsection{Wave Operator and d’Alembertian}
This subsection collects the differential-operator conventions and their sourced wave consequences in one place:
\begin{align*}
\partial &= \partial _{t} + \sigv \cdot \gradv, \\
\partial ^{*} &= \partial _{t} - \sigv \cdot \gradv, \\
\Box &:= \partial _{t}^{2} - \gradv^{2} \\
&= \partial \partial ^{*} = \partial ^{*}\partial \\
&= \langle\!\langle \partial \rangle\!\rangle^{2}.
\end{align*}
With the sigma algebra one also has
\begin{equation*}
\partial ^{2} = \partial _{t}^{2} + \gradv^{2} + 2 \partial _{t} \gradv \cdot \sigv,
\end{equation*}
so \(\partial^2\neq\Box\). Different sources may instead adopt the metric signature \((- ,+,+,+)\) or use a different box notation, including \(\Box^2\), for related wave operators. The sourced wave equations are
\begin{equation*}
\Box F = \conj{\partial}\partial F = \conj{\partial} (\rho - \mathbf J \cdot \sigv ).
\end{equation*}
\begin{align*}
\square F^{*} &= \partial (\rho + \mathbf J \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&= ( \square \mathbf E + i \square \mathbf B ) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&= (\partial _{t} \rho + \gradv \cdot \mathbf J) + (-\partial _{t} \mathbf J - \gradv \rho + i (\gradv \times \mathbf J)) \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
\partial _{t} \rho = - \gradv \cdot \mathbf J\qquad \text{conservation of charge}.
\end{equation*}
The scalar part of \(\square F^*\) is therefore the continuity equation, while Eq.\ \eqref{eq:wave-F} and the vector part of \(\square F^*\) give the sourced wave equations for \(\mathbf E\) and \(\mathbf B\). In vacuum both source terms disappear and each field component satisfies the ordinary wave equation.

In vacuum, where \(\rho=0\) and \(\mathbf J=0\), this reduces to
\begin{align*}
\square \mathbf E &= 0 \Rightarrow \partial _{t}^{2} \mathbf E = \gradv^{2} \mathbf E, \\
\square \mathbf B &= 0 \Rightarrow \partial _{t}^{2} \mathbf B = \gradv^{2} \mathbf B.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Potential Fields}
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:potential-gradient} and \eqref{eq:E-B-from-potential} put gauge freedom, source reconstruction, and the electric/magnetic field split in one place. Here \(V,S,\rho,\lambda\in\R\), \(\mathbf A,\mathbf E,\mathbf B,\mathbf J\in\R^3\), \(\Phi\in\R\oplus\R^3\), \(\mathbf F\in\C^3\), and \(F\in\varnothing\oplus\C^3\):
\begin{align*}
F^{*} &= (-\mathbf E + i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
\Phi &= V + \mathbf A \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
\partial \Phi &= \partial _{t} V + \gradv \cdot \mathbf A,\notag \\
&\quad + ( \partial _{t} \mathbf A + \gradv V ) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&\quad + i ( \gradv \times \mathbf A ) \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
This expansion separates the potential gradient into one real scalar part and one pure-vector field part. The scalar piece becomes the Lorenz-gauge scalar \(S\), and the remaining vector piece becomes the electromagnetic field:

\begin{align*}
S &:= \eqtext{scalar}( \partial \Phi ),\notag \\
&= \partial _{t} V + \gradv \cdot \mathbf A.
\end{align*}
The component identities extracted from \(\partial\Phi\) are
\begin{align*}
\mathbf E &= - \partial _{t} \mathbf A - \gradv V, \\
\mathbf B &= \gradv \times \mathbf A.
\end{align*}
The identity is explicit:
\begin{align*}
\partial \Phi &= S + F^{*}, \\
F^{*} &= \partial \Phi - S, \\
\eqtext{scalar}(F^{*}) &= \eqtext{scalar}(\partial \Phi - S) = 0, \\
\eqtext{vector}(F^{*}) &= \eqtext{vector}( \partial \Phi ).
\end{align*}
The field is therefore the non-scalar part of \(\partial\Phi\). Written this way, gauge freedom changes only the scalar part \(S\) and leaves the field paravector itself unchanged.
\input{sections/em_tables_potential.tex}

The differential operator acts before the conjugate and reverse operators are applied, so
\begin{equation*}
(\partial \Phi )^{*\mathrm{R}} \neq \Phi ^{*\mathrm{R}} \partial ^{*\mathrm{R}}.
\end{equation*}
Equivalently, without introducing \(S\), one may write
\begin{equation*}
F^{*} = (\partial \Phi - (\partial \Phi )^{*\mathrm{R}}) / 2.
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Density from Potential}
This subsection collects the derivation of the charge density and current density directly from the potential field in one place.
Using the previously defined potential \(\Phi=V+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv\), scalar part \(S=\partial_t V+\gradv\cdot\mathbf A\), and identity \(F^{*}=\partial\Phi-S\), one obtains
\begin{align*}
F &= \partial ^{*} \Phi ^{*} - S,\notag \\
\partial F &= \partial \partial ^{*} \Phi ^{*} - \partial S,\notag \\
&= \square \Phi ^{*} - \partial S,\notag \\
&= ( \square V - \partial _{t} S) + (- \square \mathbf A - \gradv S) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&= \rho - \mathbf J \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
\rho &= \square V - \partial _{t} S, \\
\mathbf J &= \square \mathbf A + \gradv S.
\end{align*}
These are the component source-reconstruction formulas hidden inside the compact Maxwell equation. The scalar equation determines charge density from the potential data, and the vector equation determines current density from the same data.

In Lorenz gauge, where \(S=0\), these relations reduce to
\begin{align*}
\partial _{t} V &= -\gradv \cdot \mathbf A, \\
\rho &= \square V, \\
\mathbf J &= \square \mathbf A.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Gauge Transform of the Potential}
\begin{align*}
\eqtext{scalar}( \partial \Phi ) &= S, \\
S &= \partial _{t} V + \gradv \cdot \mathbf A.
\end{align*}
Let \(\lambda\) be a scalar gauge function. Then
\begin{align*}
\Phi ' &= \Phi + \partial ^{*} \lambda,\notag \\
\partial \Phi ' &= \partial (\Phi + \partial ^{*}\lambda ),\notag \\
&= \partial \Phi + \partial (\partial ^{*}\lambda ),\notag \\
&= \partial \Phi + \square \lambda.
\end{align*}
Since \(\square\lambda\) is again a scalar field, only the scalar part changes:
\begin{align*}
\eqtext{scalar}( \partial \Phi ' ) &= S + \square \lambda, \\
\eqtext{vector}( \partial \Phi ' ) &= \eqtext{vector}( \partial \Phi ), \\
F' &= F.
\end{align*}
This makes the gauge split explicit: \(\Phi\) changes by a derivative of a real scalar, the scalar part of \(\partial\Phi\) shifts by \(\square\lambda\), and the field paravector remains unchanged.

\subsubsection{4-Force (Proper Force)}
This subsection collects the relativistic work law, the Lorentz-force law, the component split of \(FU/\gamma\), and the final proper-force equation in one place.
\begin{align*}
m U &= m dX/ds,\notag \\
&= m \gamma + m \gamma \mathbf v \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
d/ds = \gamma d/dt.
\end{equation*}
The scalar part of \(d(mU)/dt\) is the power equation and the vector part is the three-force equation. Writing both pieces in one paravector equation makes the usual split visible without abandoning the relativistic form.

The time component gives the relativistic work law,
\begin{equation*}
d/dt (m \gamma ) = q \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf E.
\end{equation*}
Here \(\mathbf E\) denotes the electric field, not the energy operator \(E\), and the magnetic field contributes no work term in the scalar component. The spatial component gives the relativistic Lorentz force law,
\begin{align*}
\mathbf P &:= m \gamma \mathbf v, \\
d\mathbf P/dt &= q \mathbf E + q \mathbf v \times \mathbf B.
\end{align*}
Together these assemble into
\begin{equation*}
d/dt (m U) = q \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf E + (q \mathbf E + q \mathbf v \times \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv.
\end{equation*}
To expose the paravector content explicitly, expand
\begin{align*}
F U / \gamma &= ((\mathbf E + i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv ) ( 1 + \mathbf v \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&= \mathbf E \cdot \sigv + i \mathbf B \cdot \sigv + (\mathbf E \cdot \sigv ) (\mathbf v \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&\quad + i (\mathbf B \cdot \sigv )(\mathbf v \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&= \mathbf E \cdot \sigv + i \mathbf B \cdot \sigv + \mathbf E \cdot \mathbf v,\notag \\
&\quad + i (\mathbf E \times \mathbf v) \cdot \sigv + i \mathbf B \cdot \mathbf v - (\mathbf B \times \mathbf v) \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
The product \(FU\) naturally separates into real and imaginary parts. The real part reproduces the physical Lorentz-force content, while the imaginary part exchanges electric and magnetic roles and is therefore not used in the force law itself.
\input{sections/em_tables_force.tex}

\begin{align*}
\eqtext{real}( F U ) &= \tfrac{1}{2} ( F U + U F^{\mathrm{R}} ),\notag \\
&= \gamma (\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf E + ( \mathbf E + \mathbf v \times \mathbf B ) \cdot \sigv ).
\end{align*}
The imaginary part of \(FU\) exchanges the electric and magnetic roles and is therefore not the part used in the physical force law:
\begin{align*}
m dU/ds &= q \eqtext{real}( F U ),\notag \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2} q \big( F U + U F^{\mathrm{R}} \big).
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
m ddX/ds^{2} = q \eqtext{real}( F dX/ds )\qquad \text{4-force}.
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Lagrangian Derivation of the Lorentz Force}
This subsection collects the minimally coupled action, the Euler--Lagrange derivation of the Lorentz force, the Hamiltonian, and the corresponding power identity in one place.
Starting from the Lagrangian of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:em-lagrangian}, together with the potential \(\Phi=V+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv\), proper velocity \(U=dX/ds\), and \(\gamma=dt/ds\), the action takes the form
\begin{align*}
\text{action} &= -m \int ds - q \int \eqtext{scalar}( \Phi ^{*} U ) ds,\notag \\
&= \int ( -m / \gamma - q \eqtext{scalar}( \Phi ^{*} U / \gamma )) dt,\notag \\
&= \int ( -m / \gamma,\notag \\
&\quad - q \eqtext{scalar}( (V - \mathbf A \cdot \sigv ) (1 + \mathbf v \cdot \sigv )) ) dt,\notag \\
&= \int ( -m / \gamma - q V + q \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf A ) dt.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{L} = -m / \gamma - q ( V - \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf A )\qquad \text{Lagrangian}.
\end{equation*}
This is the standard minimal-coupling Lagrangian written in the present paravector notation. The canonical momentum is shifted by the vector potential, exactly as in the ordinary component derivation.

The canonical momentum is
\begin{align*}
\delv{} \mathcal{L} &= m \gamma \mathbf v + q \mathbf A,\notag \\
&= \mathbf P + q \mathbf A.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
d/dt \delv{} \mathcal{L} &= d\mathbf P/dt + q \partial _{t} \mathbf A + q (\mathbf v \cdot \gradv) \mathbf A, \\
\gradv \mathcal{L} &= - q \gradv V + q \gradv (\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf A).
\end{align*}
Using the vector-calculus identity
\begin{equation*}
(\mathbf v(t) \cdot \gradv) \mathbf A(t, \mathbf r) = \gradv ( \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf A ) - \mathbf v \times ( \gradv \times \mathbf A ).
\end{equation*}
the Euler--Lagrange equation \(d/dt \delv{} \mathcal{L} = \gradv \mathcal{L}\) yields
\begin{align*}
d\mathbf P/dt &= - q \gradv V - q \partial _{t} \mathbf A,\notag \\
&\quad + q ( \gradv (\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf A) - (\mathbf v \cdot \gradv) \mathbf A ),\notag \\
&= q ( -\gradv V - \partial _{t}\mathbf A + \mathbf v \times ( \gradv \times \mathbf A ) ),\notag \\
&= q ( \mathbf E + \mathbf v \times \mathbf B ),\notag \\
&= m \eqtext{vector}( dU/ds ) / \gamma.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
\mathcal{H} &= (\delv{} \mathcal{L}) \cdot \mathbf v - \mathcal{L},\notag \\
&= m \gamma \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf v + q \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf A + m / \gamma.
\end{align*}
Adding the explicit potential contribution \(qV-q\,\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf A\) gives
\begin{align*}
&= m \gamma ( \mathbf v^{2} + 1 / \gamma ^{2} ) + q V,\notag \\
&= m \gamma ( \mathbf v^{2} + 1 - \mathbf v^{2} ) + q V,\notag \\
&= m \gamma + q V.
\end{align*}
The resulting Hamiltonian is the relativistic energy plus the scalar potential term. Using the Hamiltonian identity \(dH/dt=-\partial_t \mathcal L\),
\begin{align*}
dH/dt &= - \partial _{t} \mathcal{L},\notag \\
d\mathcal{H}/dt &= d(m \gamma )/dt + q dV/dt,\notag \\
&= d(m \gamma )/dt + q \partial _{t}V + q \mathbf v \cdot \gradv V.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
\partial _{t} H &= q \partial _{t} V - q \mathbf v \cdot \partial _{t} \mathbf A,\notag \\
d(m \gamma )/dt &= q \mathbf v \cdot ( -\gradv V - \partial _{t} \mathbf A ),\notag \\
&= q \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf E.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Stress-Energy of the Electromagnetic Field}
This subsection collects the frame-dependent energy-density/Poynting paravector built from \(F\), together with the differentiated and source-coupled identities that accompany it. The caution in the main text remains in force: \(T=\tfrac12F\rev{F}\) is a useful paravector, but it is not by itself the full electromagnetic stress-energy tensor:

\begin{align*}
F &= (\mathbf E + i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
F^{\mathrm{R}} &= (\mathbf E - i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
T &= \tfrac{1}{2} F F^{\mathrm{R}},\notag \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2} (\mathbf E^{2} + \mathbf B^{2}) + (\mathbf E \times \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&= T_{s} + T_{\mathbf v} \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
\partial = \partial _{t} + \sigv \cdot \gradv.
\end{equation*}
\noindent\flag{This is a frame-dependent energy-density/Poynting paravector, not by itself the full electromagnetic stress-energy tensor.}\par
Using the generic paravector product rule,
\begin{align*}
\partial (ZZ') &= (\partial Z) Z' + Z (\partial Z') + [\sigv , Z] \cdot \gradv Z',\notag \\
\partial T &= \tfrac{1}{2} \partial \big(F F^{\mathrm{R}}\big),\notag \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\partial F\big) F^{\mathrm{R}} + \tfrac{1}{2}F \big(\partial F^{\mathrm{R}}\big) \notag \\
&\quad + \tfrac{1}{2}[\sigv , F] \cdot \gradv F^{\mathrm{R}}.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
D &= \rho + \mathbf J \cdot \sigv\qquad \text{density paravector}, \\
D^{*} &= \rho - \mathbf J \cdot \sigv, \\
\partial &= \partial _{t} + \sigv \cdot \gradv, \\
\partial F &= D^{*}, \\
F^{2} &= (\mathbf E^{2} - \mathbf B^{2}) + 2 i (\mathbf E \cdot \mathbf B)\qquad \text{scalar}.
\end{align*}

Applying the same product rule to \(F^2\) gives
\begin{align*}
\partial (ZZ') &= (\partial Z) Z' + Z (\partial Z') + [\sigv , Z] \cdot \gradv Z',\notag \\
\partial \big(F^{2}\big) &= \big(\partial F\big) F + F \big(\partial F\big) \notag \\
&\quad + [\sigv , F] \cdot \gradv F,\notag \\
&= D^{*} F + F D^{*} + [\sigv , F] \cdot \gradv F.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
[\sigv , F] &= 2 i \mathbf F \times \sigv,\notag \\
[\sigv , F] \cdot \gradv F &= -2 i \mathbf F \cdot (\gradv \times \mathbf F),\notag \\
&\quad -2 \big( (\gradv \cdot \mathbf F)\mathbf F - \tfrac{1}{2} \gradv \mathbf F^{2} \big) \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
\square F^{2} = \partial ^{*} \partial \big(F^{2}\big)\qquad \text{scalar}.
\end{equation*}
The component decompositions needed in this discussion are collected directly below: Tables \ref{tab:components-partial-t}, \ref{tab:components-partial-fsquare}, \ref{tab:components-fdstar}, \ref{tab:components-dstarf}, and \ref{tab:components-dstarf-plus-fdstar} give the components of \(\partial T\), \(\partial F^2\), \(F D^*\), \(D^*F\), and \(D^*F+F D^*\), respectively. Together with \(F^2\) and \(T=\tfrac12F\rev{F}\), these formulas isolate the standard electromagnetic invariants \(\mathbf E^2-\mathbf B^2\) and \(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B\), the energy-density/Poynting split, and the source-coupled terms generated by Maxwell's equation \(\partial F=D^*\).
