\subsubsection{Electricity and Magnetism}
This subsection expands the compact field definitions in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:F-Phi} and \eqref{eq:maxwell} into explicit scalar, vector, and bivector parts. Rationalized Heaviside--Lorentz units are used, with \(c=1\). Here \(\rho,S\in\R\), \(\mathbf E,\mathbf B,\mathbf J\in\R^3\), \(\mathbf F=\mathbf E+i\mathbf B\in\C^3\), and \(F=\mathbf F\cdot\sigv\) is a pure-vector complex paravector, so \(\eqtext{scalar}(F)=0\). The spatial derivative conventions are
\begin{align*}
\gradv &= \{\partial_1,\partial_2,\partial_3\}, \\
\partial_i &:= \partial/\partial r_i, \\
\gradv^2 &= \partial_1^2+\partial_2^2+\partial_3^2,
\end{align*}
and the differential paravector is
\begin{align*}
\partial
&= \partial_t+\sigv\cdot\gradv\notag\\
&= \partial_t+\sigma_1\partial_1+\sigma_2\partial_2+\sigma_3\partial_3,\\
\partial^*
&= \partial_t-\sigv\cdot\gradv.
\end{align*}
The generic real/imaginary scalar/vector decomposition of \(\partial F\) and \(i\partial F\) has already been recorded in Subsection \ref{subsec:differential-complex-fields}. With that operator bookkeeping fixed, the electric and magnetic fields can be combined without introducing any new multiplication rule.

Collect the electric and magnetic fields into the complex three-vector \(\mathbf F\) and the field paravector \(F\):
\begin{align*}
\mathbf E &= \{E_{1}, E_{2}, E_{3}\} && \text{real vector electric field},\notag \\
\mathbf B &= \{B_{1}, B_{2}, B_{3}\} && \text{real vector magnetic field},\notag \\
\mathbf F &= \mathbf E + i \mathbf B && \text{electromagnetic field},\notag \\
F &= \mathbf F \cdot \sigv = (\mathbf E + i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv, && i = \sigma _{1}\sigma _{2}\sigma _{3}.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
F^{*} &= (-\mathbf E + i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&= -\mathbf F^{*} \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
The two simplest quadratic field combinations are
\begin{align*}
\tfrac12F^2
&=\tfrac12(\mathbf E^2-\mathbf B^2)+i\,\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B,\\
\tfrac12F F^{\mathrm R}
&=\tfrac12(\mathbf E^2+\mathbf B^2)+(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
Thus \(\tfrac12F^2\) contains the proper-Lorentz invariants, while \(\tfrac12F F^{\mathrm R}\) separates into the energy density and the Poynting vector. Under parity, \(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B\) changes sign and is therefore a pseudoscalar rather than a scalar invariant of the full Lorentz group.

Define the real source paravector by
\begin{align*}
D &= \rho + \mathbf J \cdot \sigv \in \R\oplus\R^3, \\
D^{*} &= \rho - \mathbf J \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
Here \(\rho\in\R\) is the scalar charge density and \(\mathbf J\in\R^3\) is the current-density vector. Maxwell's equations then read
\begin{align*}
\partial F &= D^{*}\qquad \text{Maxwell's equations},\notag \\
&= \rho - \mathbf J \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
\partial ^{*} F^{*} = D.
\end{equation*}
In this form, the scalar, sigma-number-one, and sigma-number-two parts of \(\partial F\) reproduce the four classical Maxwell equations in one step. Expanding the product shows where each component sits.

Expanding \(\partial F\) into scalar, pseudoscalar, vector, and bivector parts gives
\begin{align*}
\partial F &= (\partial _{t} + \sigv \cdot \gradv) ( (\mathbf E + i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&= ( \gradv \cdot \mathbf E ),\notag \\
&\quad + ( \gradv \cdot \mathbf B ) i,\notag \\
&\quad + (\partial _{t} \mathbf E - \gradv \times \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&\quad + (\partial _{t} \mathbf B + \gradv \times \mathbf E) \cdot (i \sigv ).
\end{align*}
The first two lines are the scalar and pseudoscalar parts. The last two lines are the sigma-number-one and sigma-number-two vector parts. Matching these components with \(D^*=\rho-\mathbf J\cdot\sigv\) immediately forces the magnetic-divergence term and Faraday term to vanish while fixing the electric source terms.

Accordingly, the component equations of \(\partial F=\rho-\mathbf J\cdot\sigv\) are
\begin{align*}
\gradv \cdot \mathbf E &= \rho, \\
\gradv \cdot \mathbf B &= 0, \\
\partial_t \mathbf E - \gradv \times \mathbf B &= -\mathbf J, \\
\partial_t \mathbf B + \gradv \times \mathbf E &= 0.
\end{align*}

Separating the vector components then gives
\begin{align*}
\gradv \times \mathbf B &= \mathbf J + \partial _{t} \mathbf E, \\
\gradv \times \mathbf E &= -\partial _{t} \mathbf B.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Lorentz Transform of Fields}
This subsection specializes the boost law to the electromagnetic field paravector
\begin{equation*}
F=(\mathbf E+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv .
\end{equation*}
For a constant inertial boost
\(L=\exp(\tfrac12\theta\,\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)\) with \(\mathbf u^{2}=1\), \(\beta=\tanh(\theta)\), and \(\gamma=\cosh(\theta)\), the field transforms by the general similarity \eqref{eq:general-field-similarity}, even though event and current paravectors transform by the congruence \eqref{eq:general-event-congruence}. In the inverse-first convention the left spin action is \(L^{-1}=L^*\), so a bivector transforms as
\[
F'=L^{-1}FL=L^*FL.
\]
The final factor is \(L\), rather than \(L^*\), because a bivector carries
the adjoint action \(T^{-1}(\,\cdot\,)T\). A double-\(L^*\) sandwich would
instead apply the event congruence to \(F\) and would not preserve its
pure-vector form. For example, if
\(F=E_\parallel\,\mathbf u\cdot\sigv\), then
\[
L^*FL=F,
\qquad
L^*FL^*
=\gamma E_\parallel\,\mathbf u\cdot\sigv-\gamma\beta E_\parallel,
\]
and the second expression has an inadmissible scalar part. Applying the
general complex-vector result \eqref{eq:hyperbolic-similarity} to
\(\mathbf V=\mathbf E+i\mathbf B\) gives the field and its
parallel/perpendicular parts:
\begin{align*}
F' &= L^{*} F L, \\
\eqtext{par}( \mathbf E ' ) &= \eqtext{par}( \mathbf E ), \\
\eqtext{par}( \mathbf B ' ) &= \eqtext{par}( \mathbf B ), \\
\eqtext{prp}( \mathbf E ' ) &= \gamma ( \eqtext{prp}( \mathbf E ) + \beta \mathbf u \times \mathbf B ), \\
\eqtext{prp}( \mathbf B ') &= \gamma ( \eqtext{prp}( \mathbf B ) - \beta \mathbf u \times \mathbf E ).
\end{align*}
Equivalently, the transformed fields can be written in the compact form
\begin{align*}
\mathbf E ' &= \eqtext{par}(\mathbf E) + \gamma ( \eqtext{prp}(\mathbf E) + \beta \mathbf u \times \mathbf B ), \\
\mathbf B ' &= \eqtext{par}(\mathbf B) + \gamma ( \eqtext{prp}(\mathbf B) - \beta \mathbf u \times \mathbf E ).
\end{align*}
The parallel components are unchanged, while the perpendicular components mix by the standard electric-magnetic cross terms.
Because \(LL^*=1\) and \(F^2\) is central, the field invariants are preserved directly:
\begin{equation*}
F'^2=L^*F(LL^*)FL=L^*F^2L=F^2.
\end{equation*}
Thus the real part of \(F^2\) is a scalar and its imaginary part is a pseudoscalar under parity; both are invariant under proper Lorentz transformations.

For any constant normalized Lorentz element \(T\), the potential and source
are event-type paravectors, while the derivative carries the complementary
action:
\begin{equation*}
\Phi'=T^{*\mathsf R}\Phi T^*,
\qquad
D'=T^{*\mathsf R}DT^*,
\qquad
\partial'=T^{\mathsf R}\partial T.
\end{equation*}
For the boost \(T=L\), these reduce to
\[
\Phi'=L^*\Phi L^*,
\qquad
D'=L^*DL^*,
\qquad
\partial'=L\partial L.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{align*}
\partial'F'
&=(T^{\mathsf R}\partial T)(T^{*\mathsf R}FT)\notag\\
&=T^{\mathsf R}(\partial F)T\notag\\
&=T^{\mathsf R}D^*T\notag\\
&=D'^*.
\end{align*}
Thus Maxwell's equation is covariant. In components the current transformation is
\begin{align*}
\rho'&=\gamma\bigl(\rho-\beta\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf J\bigr),\\
\eqtext{par}(\mathbf J')
&=\gamma\bigl(\eqtext{par}(\mathbf J)-\beta\rho\mathbf u\bigr),\\
\eqtext{prp}(\mathbf J')&=\eqtext{prp}(\mathbf J).
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Wave Operator and d'Alembertian}
The operator conventions were fixed already in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:partial-operator}--\eqref{eq:dalembertian}. Here we use them only to derive the sourced wave consequences and to distinguish the scalar wave operator \(\Box\) from the full square \(\partial^2\):
\begin{align*}
\partial &= \partial _{t} + \sigv \cdot \gradv, \\
\partial ^{*} &= \partial _{t} - \sigv \cdot \gradv, \\
\Box &:= \partial _{t}^{2} - \gradv^{2} \\
&= \partial \partial ^{*} = \partial ^{*}\partial.
\end{align*}
With the sigma algebra one also has
\begin{equation*}
\partial ^{2} = \partial _{t}^{2} + \gradv^{2} + 2 \partial _{t} \gradv \cdot \sigv,
\end{equation*}
so \(\partial^2\neq\Box\). With the conventions of Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:partial-operator}--\eqref{eq:dalembertian}, applying \(\partial^*\) to Maxwell's equation gives
\begin{align*}
\Box F
&=\partial^*\partial F\notag\\
&=\partial^*(\rho-\mathbf J\cdot\sigv)\notag\\
&=(\partial_t\rho+\gradv\cdot\mathbf J)\notag\\
&\quad+\bigl(-\partial_t\mathbf J-\gradv\rho
+i\gradv\times\mathbf J\bigr)\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
Because \(F\) is pure vector, the scalar part of the left-hand side vanishes. The scalar and vector parts therefore give
\begin{align*}
\partial_t\rho+\gradv\cdot\mathbf J&=0,\\
\Box\mathbf E&=-\partial_t\mathbf J-\gradv\rho,\\
\Box\mathbf B&=\gradv\times\mathbf J.
\end{align*}
In vacuum both source terms disappear and each field component satisfies the ordinary wave equation.

In vacuum, where \(\rho=0\) and \(\mathbf J=0\), this reduces to
\begin{align*}
\Box \mathbf E &= 0 \Rightarrow \partial _{t}^{2} \mathbf E = \gradv^{2} \mathbf E, \\
\Box \mathbf B &= 0 \Rightarrow \partial _{t}^{2} \mathbf B = \gradv^{2} \mathbf B.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Potential Fields}
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:potential-gradient} and \eqref{eq:E-B-from-potential} put gauge freedom, source reconstruction, and the electric/magnetic field split in one place. Here \(V,S,\rho,\lambda\in\R\), \(\mathbf A,\mathbf E,\mathbf B,\mathbf J\in\R^3\), \(\Phi\in\R\oplus\R^3\), \(\mathbf F\in\C^3\), and \(F\) is the pure-vector complex paravector defined above:
\begin{align*}
F^{*} &= (-\mathbf E + i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
\Phi &= V + \mathbf A \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
\partial \Phi &= \partial _{t} V + \gradv \cdot \mathbf A,\notag \\
&\quad + ( \partial _{t} \mathbf A + \gradv V ) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&\quad + i ( \gradv \times \mathbf A ) \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
This expansion separates the potential gradient into one real scalar part and one pure-vector field part. The scalar piece becomes the Lorenz-gauge scalar \(S\), and the remaining vector piece becomes the electromagnetic field:

\begin{align*}
S &:= \eqtext{scalar}( \partial \Phi ),\notag \\
&= \partial _{t} V + \gradv \cdot \mathbf A.
\end{align*}
The component identities extracted from \(\partial\Phi\) are
\begin{align*}
\mathbf E &= - \partial _{t} \mathbf A - \gradv V, \\
\mathbf B &= \gradv \times \mathbf A.
\end{align*}
The identity is explicit:
\begin{align*}
\partial \Phi &= S + F^{*}, \\
F^{*} &= \partial \Phi - S, \\
\eqtext{scalar}(F^{*}) &= \eqtext{scalar}(\partial \Phi - S) = 0, \\
\eqtext{vector}(F^{*}) &= \eqtext{vector}( \partial \Phi ).
\end{align*}
The field is therefore the non-scalar part of \(\partial\Phi\). Under the gauge change below, only the scalar part of \(\partial\Phi\) changes; the field paravector remains unchanged.

The differential product \(\partial\Phi\) is evaluated first; the involutions then act on the resulting field. No right-acting differential operator is implied.
Equivalently, without introducing \(S\), one may write
\begin{equation*}
F^{*} = (\partial \Phi - (\partial \Phi )^{*\mathrm{R}}) / 2.
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Density from Potential}
This subsection collects the derivation of the charge density and current density directly from the potential field in one place.
Using the previously defined potential \(\Phi=V+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv\), scalar part \(S=\partial_t V+\gradv\cdot\mathbf A\), and identity \(F^{*}=\partial\Phi-S\), one obtains
\begin{align*}
F &= \partial ^{*} \Phi ^{*} - S,\notag \\
\partial F &= \partial \partial ^{*} \Phi ^{*} - \partial S,\notag \\
&= \Box \Phi ^{*} - \partial S,\notag \\
&= ( \Box V - \partial _{t} S) + (- \Box \mathbf A - \gradv S) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&= \rho - \mathbf J \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
\rho &= \Box V - \partial _{t} S, \\
\mathbf J &= \Box \mathbf A + \gradv S.
\end{align*}
These are the component source-reconstruction formulas hidden inside the compact Maxwell equation. The scalar equation determines charge density from the potential data, and the vector equation determines current density from the same data.

In Lorenz gauge, where \(S=0\), these relations reduce to
\begin{align*}
\partial _{t} V &= -\gradv \cdot \mathbf A, \\
\rho &= \Box V, \\
\mathbf J &= \Box \mathbf A.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Gauge Transform of the Potential}
\begin{align*}
\eqtext{scalar}( \partial \Phi ) &= S, \\
S &= \partial _{t} V + \gradv \cdot \mathbf A.
\end{align*}
Let \(\lambda\) be a real scalar gauge function. To distinguish a gauge change from the boost primes used above, write
\begin{align*}
\Phi&\longmapsto\Phi+\partial^*\lambda,\\
\partial\Phi&\longmapsto\partial\Phi+\Box\lambda.
\end{align*}
Since \(\Box\lambda\) is again a scalar field, only the scalar part changes:
\begin{align*}
S&\longmapsto S+\Box\lambda,\\
F&\longmapsto F.
\end{align*}
This makes the gauge split explicit. Transformations that preserve Lorenz gauge satisfy the residual condition \(\Box\lambda=0\).

\subsubsection{Four-Force}
This subsection collects the relativistic work law, the Lorentz-force law, the component split of \(FU/\gamma\), and the four-force equation in one place.
\begin{align*}
m U &= m\frac{\dd X}{\dd s},\notag \\
&= m \gamma + m \gamma \mathbf v \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd}{\dd s}=\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}.
\end{equation*}
The scalar part of \(\dd(mU)/\dd t\) is the power equation and the vector part is the three-force equation. Writing both pieces in one paravector equation makes the usual split visible without abandoning the relativistic form.

The time component gives the relativistic work law,
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}=q\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf E.
\end{equation*}
Here \(\mathbf E\) denotes the electric field, not the energy operator \(E\), and the magnetic field contributes no work term in the scalar component. The spatial component gives the relativistic Lorentz force law,
\begin{align*}
\mathbf P &:= m \gamma \mathbf v, \\
\frac{\dd\mathbf P}{\dd t} &= q \mathbf E + q \mathbf v \times \mathbf B.
\end{align*}
Together these assemble into
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd(mU)}{\dd t}
=q\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf E
+q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
To expose the paravector content explicitly, expand
\begin{align*}
F U / \gamma &= ((\mathbf E + i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv ) ( 1 + \mathbf v \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&= \mathbf E \cdot \sigv + i \mathbf B \cdot \sigv + (\mathbf E \cdot \sigv ) (\mathbf v \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&\quad + i (\mathbf B \cdot \sigv )(\mathbf v \cdot \sigv ),\notag \\
&= \mathbf E \cdot \sigv + i \mathbf B \cdot \sigv + \mathbf E \cdot \mathbf v,\notag \\
&\quad + i (\mathbf E \times \mathbf v) \cdot \sigv + i \mathbf B \cdot \mathbf v - (\mathbf B \times \mathbf v) \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
The product \(FU\) naturally separates into real and imaginary parts. The real part reproduces the physical Lorentz-force content, while the imaginary part exchanges electric and magnetic roles and is therefore not used in the force law itself.
\input{sections/em_tables_force.tex}

\begin{align*}
\eqtext{real}( F U ) &= \tfrac{1}{2} ( F U + U F^{\mathrm{R}} ),\notag \\
&= \gamma (\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf E + ( \mathbf E + \mathbf v \times \mathbf B ) \cdot \sigv ).
\end{align*}
The physical four-force is therefore
\begin{align*}
m\frac{\dd U}{\dd s} &= q \eqtext{real}( F U ),\notag \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2} q \big( F U + U F^{\mathrm{R}} \big).
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
m\frac{\dd^2X}{\dd s^{2}}
=q\,\eqtext{real}\!\left(F\frac{\dd X}{\dd s}\right)
\qquad \text{four-force}.
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Lagrangian Derivation of the Lorentz Force}
This subsection collects the minimally coupled action, the Euler--Lagrange derivation of the Lorentz force, the Hamiltonian, and the corresponding power identity in one place.
Starting from the Lagrangian of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:em-lagrangian}, together with the potential \(\Phi=V+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv\), four-velocity \(U=\dd X/\dd s\), and \(\gamma=\dd t/\dd s\), the action takes the form
\begin{align*}
\text{action}
&=-m\int\dd s-q\int\eqtext{scalar}(\Phi^*U)\,\dd s\notag\\
&=\int\left[-\frac{m}{\gamma}
-q\,\eqtext{scalar}\!\left((V-\mathbf A\cdot\sigv)
(1+\mathbf v\cdot\sigv)\right)\right]\dd t\notag\\
&=\int\left(-\frac{m}{\gamma}-qV+q\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf A\right)\dd t.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{L}=-\frac{m}{\gamma}-q(V-\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf A).
\end{equation*}
This is the standard minimal-coupling Lagrangian written in the present paravector notation. The canonical momentum is shifted by the vector potential, exactly as in the ordinary component derivation.

The canonical momentum is
\begin{align*}
\delv{} \mathcal{L} &= m \gamma \mathbf v + q \mathbf A,\notag \\
&= \mathbf P + q \mathbf A.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\delv{} \mathcal{L} &= \frac{\dd\mathbf P}{\dd t} + q \partial _{t} \mathbf A + q (\mathbf v \cdot \gradv) \mathbf A, \\
\gradv \mathcal{L} &= - q \gradv V + q \gradv (\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf A).
\end{align*}
Using the vector-calculus identity
\begin{equation*}
(\mathbf v(t) \cdot \gradv) \mathbf A(t, \mathbf r) = \gradv ( \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf A ) - \mathbf v \times ( \gradv \times \mathbf A ).
\end{equation*}
the Euler--Lagrange equation \((\dd/\dd t)\delv{} \mathcal{L} = \gradv \mathcal{L}\) yields
\begin{align*}
\frac{\dd\mathbf P}{\dd t} &= - q \gradv V - q \partial _{t} \mathbf A,\notag \\
&\quad + q ( \gradv (\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf A) - (\mathbf v \cdot \gradv) \mathbf A ),\notag \\
&= q ( -\gradv V - \partial _{t}\mathbf A + \mathbf v \times ( \gradv \times \mathbf A ) ),\notag \\
&= q ( \mathbf E + \mathbf v \times \mathbf B ),\notag \\
&= \frac{m}{\gamma}\eqtext{vector}\!\left(\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\right).
\end{align*}
The Hamiltonian is
\begin{align*}
\mathcal H
&=(\delv{}\mathcal L)\cdot\mathbf v-\mathcal L\notag\\
&=(m\gamma\mathbf v+q\mathbf A)\cdot\mathbf v
+\frac{m}{\gamma}+qV-q\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf A\notag\\
&=m\gamma\left(\mathbf v^2+\frac{1}{\gamma^2}\right)+qV\notag\\
&=m\gamma+qV.
\end{align*}
Thus \(\mathcal H\) is the mechanical relativistic energy plus the scalar-potential energy. Along a solution,
\begin{align*}
\frac{\dd\mathcal H}{\dd t}
&=-\partial_t\mathcal L
=q\partial_tV-q\mathbf v\cdot\partial_t\mathbf A,\\
\frac{\dd\mathcal H}{\dd t}
&=\frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}
+q\partial_tV+q\mathbf v\cdot\gradv V.
\end{align*}
Equating these expressions gives
\begin{align*}
\frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}
&=q\mathbf v\cdot(-\gradv V-\partial_t\mathbf A)\notag\\
&=q\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf E,
\end{align*}
in agreement with the scalar component of the Lorentz-force equation.

\subsubsection{Energy-Density and Poynting Paravector}
Equation \eqref{eq:energy-momentum-paravector} packages the electromagnetic energy density and Poynting vector into one paravector. The algebra below records its components and the scalar energy balance implied by Maxwell's equation \eqref{eq:maxwell}:

\begin{align*}
F &= (\mathbf E + i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
F^{\mathrm{R}} &= (\mathbf E - i \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
T &= \tfrac{1}{2} F F^{\mathrm{R}},\notag \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2} (\mathbf E^{2} + \mathbf B^{2}) + (\mathbf E \times \mathbf B) \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
&= T_{s} + T_{\mathbf v} \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
\partial = \partial _{t} + \sigv \cdot \gradv.
\end{equation*}
The paravector \(T=\tfrac12F\rev{F}\) is the energy density and flux measured relative to the chosen time direction. It is not, by itself, the full electromagnetic stress-energy tensor. The covariant content is the linear map on real paravectors \(a\in\R\oplus\R^3\),
\begin{equation*}
a\longmapsto\frac12F\,a\,\rev{F},
\end{equation*}
whose value at \(a=1\) is the displayed paravector \(T\) \cite{hestenes1966,doran2003}.
Indeed, for \(a'=L^*aL^*\), \(F'=L^*FL\), and \((F')^{\mathrm R}=LF^{\mathrm R}L^*\),
\begin{equation*}
\frac12F'a'(F')^{\mathrm R}
=L^*\left(\frac12FaF^{\mathrm R}\right)L^*,
\end{equation*}
so the map has the required event-paravector covariance.

Maxwell's equations immediately give the scalar energy balance:
\begin{align*}
\partial_tT_s+\gradv\cdot T_{\mathbf v}
&=\mathbf E\cdot\partial_t\mathbf E
+\mathbf B\cdot\partial_t\mathbf B
+\gradv\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\notag\\
&=-\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E.
\end{align*}
This is Poynting's theorem in the present units: the field-energy loss equals the work done on charge.
