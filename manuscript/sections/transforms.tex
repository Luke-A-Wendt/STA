\subsection{Lorentz Boost}
A coordinate system in which force-free motion has constant velocity is
called an inertial frame. A Lorentz boost relates two inertial frames whose
spatial origins move at constant relative velocity
\cite{lorentz1904,einstein1905,minkowski1908}. The velocity of the primed
frame relative to the unprimed frame is written
\(\beta\vct{u}\) in the sign convention used below. Thus \(\vct{u}\) gives
the direction, \(\vct{u}^{2}=1\), and the signed speed \(\beta\) satisfies
\(|\beta|<1\). The parameters and the corresponding algebra element are
\begin{align*}
\theta &:= \operatorname{arctanh}(\beta ),\notag \\
\beta &= \tanh(\theta ),\notag \\
\gamma &:= \cosh(\theta ),\notag \\
\gamma \beta &= \sinh(\theta ),\notag \\
\vct{u} &:= \vcol{u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}},
\qquad \vct{u}^{2} = 1,\notag \\
\pv{L}(\theta ,\vct{u}) &:= \exp( \tfrac{1}{2} \theta (\vct{u} \cdot \sigv ) ),\notag \\
&= \cosh( \tfrac{1}{2} \theta ) + \sinh( \tfrac{1}{2} \theta ) (\vct{u} \cdot \sigv ).
\end{align*}
The parameter \(\theta\), defined by
\(\beta=\tanh(\theta)\), is conventionally called rapidity, and
\(\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}\) is the Lorentz factor. The functions
\(\cosh(\theta)\) and \(\sinh(\theta)\) replace the cosine and sine of a
spatial rotation, which is why \(\pv{L}\) is called a hyperbolic transform
element \cite{baylis1999,doran2003}. The element defined in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:boost-element} has the
inverse
\begin{equation*}
\pv{L}^{-1} = \exp( -\tfrac{1}{2} \theta (\vct{u} \cdot \sigv ) ).
\end{equation*}
Here \(\theta,\gamma,\beta\in\R\), \(\vct{u}\in\R^3\), and
\(\pv{L}\in\Pspace(\R)\). Direct application of the two algebra operations
gives
\(\pv{L}^{-1}=\pv{L}^{*}=\pv{L}^{*\mathsf H}\). Elements with the same
direction \(\vct{u}\), conventionally called collinear boosts, commute:
\begin{equation*}
\com{\pv{L}(\theta ,\vct{u})}{\pv{L}(\theta ',\vct{u})}=0.
\end{equation*}
Consequently, boosts along that same direction are combined by addition of
\(\theta\). With \(\beta'=\tanh(\theta')\) and
\(\gamma'=\cosh(\theta')\),
\begin{align*}
\pv{L}(\theta,\vct{u})\pv{L}(\theta',\vct{u})
&=\pv{L}(\theta+\theta',\vct{u}),\\
\tanh(\theta+\theta')&=\frac{\beta+\beta'}{1+\beta\beta'},\\
\cosh(\theta+\theta')&=\gamma\gamma'(1+\beta\beta').
\end{align*}
The coordinates assigned to one fixed event by the two frames are now
compared. This interpretation, in which the event is unchanged while its
coordinate description changes, is called a passive boost. With the signs
fixed by \(\beta\vct{u}\) above, the real event paravector
\(\pv{X}=t+\vct{r}\cdot\sigv\), with \(t\in\R\),
\(\vct{r}\in\R^3\), and \(\pv{X}\in\Pspace(\R)\), is transformed as
\begin{align*}
\pv{X} &= t + \vct{r} \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
\pv{X}' &= \pv{L}^{-1}\pv{X}(\pv{L}^{-1})^{\mathsf H},\notag\\
&=\pv{L}^{*} \pv{X} \pv{L}^{*},\notag \\
&= t' + \vct{r}' \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
This is the event transformation
\(\pv{X}'=\pv{T}^{-1}\pv{X}(\pv{T}^{-1})^{\mathsf H}\) from
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:general-event-congruence}, specialized to
\(\pv{T}=\pv{L}\). A product of the form
\(\pv{A}\pv{X}\pv{A}^{\mathsf H}\) is conventionally called a congruence.
It preserves
\(\herm{\pv{X}'}=\pv{X}'\) whenever
\(\herm{\pv{X}}=\pv{X}\), which is the Hermitian condition that makes an
event paravector real.
An active reading would keep the coordinate frame fixed and move the event;
the inverse transformation, obtained by replacing \(\theta\) with
\(-\theta\), would then describe the corresponding motion. Only the passive
reading is used in the component formulas below
\cite{einstein1905,baylis1999}.
\begin{align*}
t' &= t \cosh(\theta ) - \sinh(\theta ) (\vct{u} \cdot \vct{r}),\notag \\
&= \gamma ( t - \beta (\vct{u} \cdot \vct{r}) ).
\end{align*}
The vector part is made explicit by the axis functions in
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:par-function} and \eqref{eq:prp-function}, through which
\(\vct{r}\) is split into
components parallel and perpendicular to the chosen direction:
\begin{align*}
\eqtext{par}(\vct{r}) &= (\vct{r} \cdot \vct{u}) \vct{u}\qquad \text{parallel to } \vct{u},\notag \\
\eqtext{prp}(\vct{r}) &= \vct{r} - \eqtext{par}(\vct{r})\qquad \text{perpendicular to } \vct{u},\notag \\
\vct{r} &= \eqtext{par}( \vct{r} ) + \eqtext{prp}( \vct{r} ),\notag \\
\eqtext{par}( \vct{r}' ) &= \gamma ( \eqtext{par}( \vct{r} ) - \beta t \vct{u}),\notag \\
\eqtext{prp}( \vct{r}' ) &= \eqtext{prp}( \vct{r} ),\notag \\
\vct{r}' &= \eqtext{par}( \vct{r}' ) + \eqtext{prp}( \vct{r} ' ),\notag \\
&= \eqtext{prp}( \vct{r} ) + \gamma ( \eqtext{par}( \vct{r} ) - \beta t \vct{u}),\notag \\
&= \vct{r} +( \gamma ( \vct{r} \cdot \vct{u} - \beta t ) - \vct{r} \cdot \vct{u} ) \vct{u}.
\end{align*}
Equivalently, the full transformed paravector is obtained in one line by direct multiplication:
\begin{equation*}
\pv{L}^*\pv{X}\pv{L}^*
=\gamma\bigl(t-\beta\,\vct{u}\cdot\vct{r}\bigr)
+\Bigl(\eqtext{prp}(\vct{r})
+\gamma\bigl(\eqtext{par}(\vct{r})-\beta t\vct{u}\bigr)\Bigr)\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
Thus the component parallel to \(\vct{u}\) is mixed with the scalar part while the perpendicular component is left unchanged.
In the small-parameter limit \(\beta\to 0\),
\begin{equation*}
\gamma \to 1, t' \to t, \vct{r}' \to \vct{r}.
\end{equation*}
Preservation of the interval is then obtained directly from
\(\pv{L}^{-1}=\pv{L}^{*}\):
\begin{align*}
\detf{\pv{X}'} &= \pv{X}'\conj{\pv{X}'},\notag \\
&= (\pv{L}^{*} \pv{X} \pv{L}^{*}) (\pv{L}^{*} \pv{X} \pv{L}^{*})^{*},\notag \\
&= \pv{L}^{*} \pv{X} \pv{L}^{*} \pv{L} \pv{X}^{*} \pv{L},\notag \\
&= \pv{L}^{*} \pv{X} \pv{X}^{*} \pv{L},\notag \\
&= \detf{\pv{X}} \pv{L}^{*} \pv{L},\notag \\
&= \detf{\pv{X}}.
\end{align*}

Length contraction is obtained only after the two endpoint events to be
compared are specified. A rod is taken to have fixed endpoint positions in
the unprimed frame; this frame is therefore the rod's rest frame. The rod is
aligned with \(\vct{u}\). Its two endpoints are denoted by the function
inputs \((1)\) and \((2)\), so
\[
\eqtext{prp}\bigl(\vct{r}(2)-\vct{r}(1)\bigr)=0,
\qquad
l:=\left\|\eqtext{par}\bigl(\vct{r}(2)-\vct{r}(1)\bigr)\right\|.
\]
Thus \(l\) is the separation of the endpoints in the frame where both
positions are fixed. A length measured in the primed frame must instead use
two endpoint events occurring at the same primed time, so
\(t'(2)-t'(1)=0\). The following relation is then obtained from the inverse
boost:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
&\eqtext{par}\bigl(\vct{r}(2)-\vct{r}(1)\bigr)\\
&\quad=\gamma\,
\eqtext{par}\bigl(\vct{r}'(2)-\vct{r}'(1)\bigr)\\
&\qquad+\gamma\beta\bigl(t'(2)-t'(1)\bigr)\vct{u}\\
&\quad=\gamma\,
\eqtext{par}\bigl(\vct{r}'(2)-\vct{r}'(1)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
and therefore the primed length is
\begin{equation*}
l':=\left\|\eqtext{par}\bigl(\vct{r}'(2)-\vct{r}'(1)\bigr)\right\|
=\frac{l}{\gamma}.
\end{equation*}
The perpendicular length is unchanged.

The four-momentum of a massive particle is acted on by the same congruence:
\begin{align*}
(m\pv{U})'&=\pv{L}^*(m\pv{U})\pv{L}^*\notag\\
&=E'+\vct{P}'\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
Consequently,
\begin{align*}
E'&=\gamma\bigl(E-\beta\,\vct{u}\cdot\vct{P}\bigr),\\
\eqtext{par}(\vct{P}')
&=\gamma\bigl(\eqtext{par}(\vct{P})-\beta E\vct{u}\bigr),\\
\eqtext{prp}(\vct{P}')&=\eqtext{prp}(\vct{P}).
\end{align*}
Because the same real-paravector transformation is used as for \(\pv{X}\),
\(E^2-\vct{P}^2=m^2\) is preserved. As defined in the four-momentum
section, this equation is the mass shell condition: it is the restriction
that relates the allowed energy and momentum of a particle of mass \(m\).

\subsection{Compositions of Boosts and Rotors}
The ordered products and their inverses are listed. The order remains significant even when a simple inverse rule is satisfied separately by each factor.

For one hyperbolic element and one circular element,
\begin{align*}
\pv{T} &= \pv{L} \pv{R}, \\
\pv{T}^{-1} &= \pv{T}^{*\mathsf H} = \pv{R}^{*\mathsf H} \pv{L}^{*\mathsf H} = \pv{R}^{\mathsf H} \pv{L}^{*}, \\
\pv{T} &= \pv{R} \pv{L}, \\
\pv{T}^{-1} &= \pv{T}^{*\mathsf H} = \pv{L}^{*\mathsf H} \pv{R}^{*\mathsf H} = \pv{L}^{*} \pv{R}^{\mathsf H}.
\end{align*}
For two factors of the same type,
\begin{align*}
\pv{T} &= \pv{R}\pv{R}', \\
\pv{T}^{-1} &= \pv{T}^{*\mathsf H}
=\herm{\pv{R}'}\herm{\pv{R}}, \\
\pv{T} &= \pv{L}\pv{L}', \\
\pv{T}^{-1} &= \pv{T}^{*\mathsf H}
=\conj{\pv{L}'}\conj{\pv{L}}.
\end{align*}
These formulas are simple order identities, but the inverse of each ordered product is fixed by them without ambiguity.
