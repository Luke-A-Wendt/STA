\subsubsection*{Hyperbolic Directional Transforms}
The identities in this subsection unpack Eq.\ \eqref{eq:boost} and its component consequences.
They are placed next to the circular identities because both come from the same exponential calculus, with sigma-number-one generators producing hyperbolic functions instead of circular ones.
The auxiliary parameters \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) are introduced first because they shorten the resulting formulas:
\begin{align*}
\theta &= \operatorname{arctanh}(\beta ),\notag \\
\beta &= \tanh(\theta ),\notag \\
\gamma &= \cosh(\theta ),\notag \\
\gamma \beta &= \sinh(\theta ),\notag \\
\mathbf u &= (u_{1}, u_{2}, u_{3}), \mathbf u^{2} = 1,\notag \\
L(\theta ,\mathbf u) &:= \exp( \tfrac{1}{2} \theta (\mathbf u \cdot \sigv ) ),\notag \\
&= \cosh( \tfrac{1}{2} \theta ) + \sinh( \tfrac{1}{2} \theta ) (\mathbf u \cdot \sigv ).
\end{align*}
\begin{equation*}
L^{-1} = \exp( -\tfrac{1}{2} \theta (\mathbf u \cdot \sigv ) ).
\end{equation*}
Here \(\theta,\beta,\gamma\in\R\), \(\mathbf u\in\R^3\) is a unit real vector, and \(L\in\R\oplus\R^3\) is the hyperbolic transform element. Its inverse satisfies \(L^{-1}=L^{*}=L^{*\mathrm{R}}\). Elements along the same axis commute:
\begin{equation*}
[ L(\theta ,\mathbf u), L(\theta ',\mathbf u) ] = 0.
\end{equation*}
Consequently, collinear transforms combine by addition of the parameter \(\theta\):
\begin{align*}
L(\theta ', \mathbf u) &= L(\theta _{1}, \mathbf u) L(\theta _{2}, \mathbf u), \\
\beta ' &= (\beta _{1} + \beta _{2}) / (1 + \beta _{1} \beta _{2}), \\
\gamma ' &= \gamma _{1} \gamma _{2} (1 + \beta _{1} \beta _{2}).
\end{align*}
Acting on the real paravector \(X=t+\mathbf r\cdot\sigv\), with \(t\in\R\), \(\mathbf r\in\R^3\), and \(X\in\R\oplus\R^3\), the transform gives
\begin{align*}
X &= t + \mathbf r \cdot \sigv,\notag \\
X' &= L^{*} X L,\notag \\
&= t' + \mathbf r' \cdot \sigv.
\end{align*}
\begin{align*}
t' &= t \cosh(\theta ) - \sinh(\theta ) (\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf r),\notag \\
&= \gamma ( t - \beta (\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf r) ).
\end{align*}
The vector part is clearest after splitting \(\mathbf r\) into components parallel and perpendicular to the chosen direction:
\begin{align*}
\eqtext{par}(\mathbf r) &:= (\mathbf r \cdot \mathbf u) \mathbf u\qquad \text{parallel to } \mathbf u,\notag \\
\eqtext{prp}(\mathbf r) &:= \mathbf r - \eqtext{par}(\mathbf r)\qquad \text{perpendicular to } \mathbf u,\notag \\
\mathbf r &= \eqtext{par}( \mathbf r ) + \eqtext{prp}( \mathbf r ),\notag \\
\eqtext{par}( \mathbf r' ) &= \gamma ( \eqtext{par}( \mathbf r ) - \beta t \mathbf u),\notag \\
\eqtext{prp}( \mathbf r' ) &= \eqtext{prp}( \mathbf r ),\notag \\
\mathbf r' &= \eqtext{par}( \mathbf r' ) + \eqtext{prp}( \mathbf r ' ),\notag \\
&= \eqtext{prp}( \mathbf r ) + \gamma ( \eqtext{par}( \mathbf r ) - \beta t \mathbf u),\notag \\
&= \mathbf r +( \gamma ( \mathbf r \cdot \mathbf u - \beta t ) - \mathbf r \cdot \mathbf u ) \mathbf u.
\end{align*}
Thus Eq.\ \eqref{eq:boost} gives the hyperbolic split of scalar and vector parts: the component parallel to \(\mathbf u\) mixes with the scalar part while the perpendicular component is unchanged.
In the small-parameter limit \(\beta\to 0\),
\begin{equation*}
\gamma \Rightarrow 1, t' \Rightarrow t, \mathbf r' \Rightarrow \mathbf r.
\end{equation*}
The real scalar paravector metric then follows directly from \(L^{-1}=L^{*}\):
\begin{align*}
\langle\!\langle X' \rangle\!\rangle^{2} &= X' X'^{*},\notag \\
&= (L^{*} X L) (L^{*} X L)^{*},\notag \\
&= L^{*} X L L^{*} X^{*} L,\notag \\
&= L^{*} X X^{*} L,\notag \\
&= \langle\!\langle X \rangle\!\rangle^{2} L^{*} L,\notag \\
&= \langle\!\langle X \rangle\!\rangle^{2}.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection*{Compositions of Hyperbolic and Circular Transforms}
The ordered products are listed here with their inverses. The order matters even when each factor separately has a simple inverse rule.

For one hyperbolic element and one circular element,
\begin{align*}
T &= L R, \\
T^{-1} &= T^{*\mathrm{R}} = R^{*\mathrm{R}} L^{*\mathrm{R}} = R^{\mathrm{R}} L^{*}, \\
T &= R L, \\
T^{-1} &= T^{*\mathrm{R}} = L^{*\mathrm{R}} R^{*\mathrm{R}} = L^{*} R^{\mathrm{R}}.
\end{align*}
For two factors of the same type,
\begin{align*}
T &= R_{1} R_{2}, \\
T^{-1} &= T^{*\mathrm{R}} = R_{2}^{\mathrm{R}} R_{1}^{\mathrm{R}}, \\
T &= L_{1} L_{2}, \\
T^{-1} &= T^{*\mathrm{R}} = L_{2}^{*}L_{1}^{*}.
\end{align*}
These formulas are simple order identities, but they matter because they fix the inverse of each ordered product without ambiguity.
