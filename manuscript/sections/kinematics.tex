\subsubsection*{Spacetime Paravectors and Interval Recovery}
Starting from the displacement and interval formulas in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:dX} and \eqref{eq:interval}, the real paravector
\[
\dd X=\dd t+\dd\mathbf r\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
\conj{\dd X}=\dd t-\dd\mathbf r\cdot\sigv,
\]
is the algebraic form of an infinitesimal space-time displacement. The temporal and spatial pieces are recovered directly by
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\dd X+\conj{\dd X}}{2}=\dd t,
\qquad
\frac{\dd X-\conj{\dd X}}{2}=\dd\mathbf r\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
Multiplying the displacement by its conjugate gives the interval again,
\begin{equation*}
\langle\!\langle \dd X\rangle\!\rangle^{2}=\dd X\conj{\dd X}=\dd t^{2}-\dd\mathbf r^{2}=\dd s^{2}.
\end{equation*}
This is the precise point at which the algebraic Minkowski form from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:minkowski-form} becomes the space-time metric of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:interval}. For a non-null displacement, the inverse exists and is
\begin{equation*}
(\dd X)^{-1}=\frac{\conj{\dd X}}{\dd s^{2}}.
\end{equation*}
Null displacements are singled out algebraically because this inverse breaks down exactly when \(\dd s^{2}=0\).

\subsubsection*{Interval Classes and Proper Time}
Equation \eqref{eq:interval} gives the three standard interval classes without any additional geometric machinery:
\begin{align*}
\dd s^2>0 &\;\Rightarrow\; \text{time-like},\notag \\
\dd s^2=0 &\;\Rightarrow\; \text{null},\notag \\
\dd s^2<0 &\;\Rightarrow\; \text{space-like}.
\end{align*}
For a time-like worldline, the parameter \(s\) is the proper time in natural units. Writing \(\mathbf v=\dd\mathbf r/\dd t\) then gives
\begin{equation*}
\dd s=\sqrt{1-\mathbf v^2}\,\dd t,
\qquad
\tau=\int \dd s=\int \sqrt{1-\mathbf v^2}\,\dd t.
\end{equation*}
In SI units the same relation is \(ds=c\,d\tau\). The advantage of the paravector notation is that the interval classification and the proper-time formula both come from the single algebraic identity \(\dd X\,\conj{\dd X}=dt^2-d\mathbf r^2\).

\subsubsection*{Proper Velocity and Proper Acceleration in Components}
Starting from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:proper-velocity}, the proper-velocity paravector can be read componentwise as
\begin{align*}
U&=\frac{\dd X}{\dd s}=\gamma+\gamma\mathbf v\cdot\sigv,\notag \\
\eqtext{scalar}(U)&=\gamma,\qquad
\eqtext{vector}(U)=\gamma\mathbf v.
\end{align*}
Its normalization \(\langle\!\langle U\rangle\!\rangle^2=1\) implies \(U^{-1}=U^*\). Differentiating with respect to \(s\) yields
\begin{equation*}
\eqtext{scalar}(\conj{U}A)=0,
\qquad
A:=\frac{\dd U}{\dd s},
\end{equation*}
so proper acceleration is Minkowski-orthogonal to proper velocity.

If \(\mathbf a=\dd\mathbf v/\dd t\), then
\begin{equation*}
A=
\gamma^{4}(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a)
+\Bigl(\gamma^{2}\mathbf a+\gamma^{4}(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a)\mathbf v\Bigr)\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
Its quadratic form is
\begin{equation*}
\langle\!\langle A\rangle\!\rangle^2
=-\gamma^4\Bigl(\mathbf a^2+\gamma^2(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a)^2\Bigr),
\end{equation*}
which is strictly space-like unless the acceleration vanishes. Splitting \(\mathbf a\) into parts parallel and perpendicular to \(\mathbf v\) makes the same structure more explicit:
\begin{equation*}
\langle\!\langle A\rangle\!\rangle^2
=-\gamma^4\Bigl(\mathbf a_{\perp}^{2}+\gamma^{2}\mathbf a_{\parallel}^{2}\Bigr).
\end{equation*}
This is the geometric content later used by the Lorentz-force law.

\subsubsection*{Proper Momentum and the Mass Shell}
Multiplying proper velocity by the rest mass produces the energy--momentum paravector,
\begin{equation*}
mU=E+\mathbf P\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
E=m\gamma,
\qquad
\mathbf P=m\gamma\mathbf v.
\end{equation*}
At rest one has \(\mathbf v=0\), hence \(E=m\) in natural units and \(E=mc^{2}\) in SI units. The kinetic energy is therefore \(E-m\).

Because \(\langle\!\langle U\rangle\!\rangle^2=1\), the mass shell follows immediately:
\begin{equation*}
\langle\!\langle mU\rangle\!\rangle^2
=(E+\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)(E-\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)
=E^{2}-\mathbf P^{2}=m^{2}.
\end{equation*}
For \(m=0\), this reduces to \(E^{2}=\mathbf P^{2}\), which is the null case appropriate to massless motion.

\subsubsection*{Action Principle, Hamiltonian, and Low-Velocity Expansion}
The free action is
\begin{equation*}
S_{\mathrm{free}}=-m\int \dd s=\int \mathcal L_{\mathrm{free}}\,\dd t,
\qquad
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{free}}=-\frac{m}{\gamma}.
\end{equation*}
Differentiating with respect to \(\mathbf v\) gives the canonical momentum again,
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\partial \mathcal L_{\mathrm{free}}}{\partial \mathbf v}
=m\gamma\mathbf v
=\mathbf P.
\end{equation*}
The Hamiltonian is then
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal H
=\mathbf v\cdot\frac{\partial \mathcal L_{\mathrm{free}}}{\partial \mathbf v}-\mathcal L_{\mathrm{free}}
=m\gamma\mathbf v^{2}+\frac{m}{\gamma}
=m\gamma
=E,
\end{equation*}
so the action principle reproduces both the relativistic momentum and the relativistic energy inside one calculation.

The low-velocity series
\begin{align*}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{free}}&=-m+\frac12 m\mathbf v^2+O(\mathbf v^4),\notag \\
\mathbf P&=m\mathbf v+O(\mathbf v^{3}),\notag \\
\mathcal H&=m+\frac12 m\mathbf v^{2}+O(\mathbf v^{4})
\end{align*}
shows exactly how the relativistic action contains the Newtonian kinetic term together with the additive rest-energy constant. Classical mechanics therefore appears as the approximation, not as the starting point.
