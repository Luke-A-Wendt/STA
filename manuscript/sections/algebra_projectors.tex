\subsubsection{Functions of \texorpdfstring{\(\vct{u}\cdot\sigv\)}{u.sigma}}
Multiplication by \(\vct{u}\cdot\sigv\) gives \(+1\) on one projector
part and \(-1\) on the other, as shown in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projectors}.
Consequently, a scalar function is applied by assigning its value at \(+1\)
to the first part and its value at \(-1\) to the second. This is the
projector-based function rule defined in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:functional-calculus}:
\begin{align*}
f(\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)
&= f(+)\pv{\Pi}(+) + f(-)\pv{\Pi}(-)\notag \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(f(+)+f(-)\bigr)
+ \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(f(+)-f(-)\bigr)\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
These are the basic projector formulas for a unit-direction element.
Square roots and logarithms can have more than one value. A consistent
choice of one such value is called a branch. The independent square-root
choices on the two projector parts are recorded by the sign function
\begin{equation}
\epsilon(\pm):=\eqtext{an independently chosen member of }\{+1,-1\}.
\label{eq:branch-sign-function}
\end{equation}
The resulting identities are
\begin{align*}
\sqrt{\vct{u}\cdot\sigv}
&= \epsilon(+)\pv{\Pi}(+) + \epsilon(-) i\,\pv{\Pi}(-), \\
\log(\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)
&= 2 i \pi\bigl(n\,\pv{\Pi}(+) + (\tfrac12+m)\,\pv{\Pi}(-)\bigr), \\
(z+\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)^{-1}
&= \frac{1}{z+1}\pv{\Pi}(+) + \frac{1}{z-1}\pv{\Pi}(-)\notag \\
&= \frac{z-\vct{u}\cdot\sigv}{z^{2}-1}, \\
\frac{i-\vct{u}\cdot\sigv}{i+\vct{u}\cdot\sigv}
&= i\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv, \\
\exp(\theta\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)
&= \cosh(\theta)+\sinh(\theta)\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv, \\
\exp(i\theta\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)
&= \cos(\theta)+i\sin(\theta)\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv,
\end{align*}
where \(m\) and \(n\) are integers selecting the independent logarithm
values, and the inverse formula requires \(z\neq\pm1\). The two values from
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:branch-sign-function} are independent; the
conventional choices \(\sqrt{+1}=1\) and \(\sqrt{-1}=i\) are obtained
when \(\epsilon(+)=\epsilon(-)=+1\). These identities are obtained by applying
the chosen scalar value separately on the two projector-selected parts.
The corresponding domains for real and complex paravectors are stated with
the exponential and logarithm formulas.

\subsubsection{Functions of \pv{X}}
The scalar, vector, and unit-direction data are specified by
\begin{align*}
\pv{X} &= t + \vct{r}\cdot\sigv, \\
t &= \eqtext{scalar}(\pv{X})\in\R, \\
\vct{r} &= \eqtext{vector}(\pv{X})\in\R^{3}, \\
\vct{u} &:= \vct{r}/\lVert\vct{r}\rVert \qquad \text{when }\vct{r}\neq 0.
\end{align*}
If \(\vct{r}\neq 0\), its decomposition is
\(\vct{r}=\lVert\vct{r}\rVert \vct{u}\), with \(\vct{u}^{2}=1\).
The two scalar values associated with the two projector parts are defined by
\begin{equation}
\lambda(\pv{X},\pm):=t\pm\lVert\vct{r}\rVert.
\label{eq:real-paravector-eigenvalues}
\end{equation}
They are called eigenvalues because multiplication by \(\pv{X}\) gives each
projector back multiplied by the corresponding scalar, as verified below.
For \(\vct{r}\neq\vct{0}\), the quadratic relation is
\begin{align*}
\pv{X}^{2} - 2 t \pv{X} + ( t^{2} - \vct{r}^{2} ) &= 0,\notag \\
0 &= (\pv{X}-\lambda(\pv{X},-))(\pv{X}-\lambda(\pv{X},+))\notag \\
&= \pv{X}^{2} - \lambda(\pv{X},-)\pv{X}\notag \\
&\quad - \pv{X}\lambda(\pv{X},+) + \lambda(\pv{X},-)\lambda(\pv{X},+).
\end{align*}
No degree-one polynomial with scalar coefficients can vanish on this
non-scalar \(\pv{X}\). The displayed quadratic is therefore the
lowest-degree scalar-coefficient polynomial satisfied by \(\pv{X}\), which
is its minimal polynomial. If \(\vct{r}=\vct{0}\), then \(\pv{X}=t\), the
minimal polynomial is instead \(x-t\), and every function reduces directly
to \(f(\pv{X})=f(t)\). Here \(x\) is only the placeholder input of the
polynomial. The projector formulas below apply to
\(\vct{r}\neq\vct{0}\).
The projector function from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projectors} gives the
corresponding decomposition directly:
\begin{align*}
\pv{\Pi}(\pm) &= \tfrac12(1\pm \vct{u}\cdot\sigv), \\
\pv{X} &= \lambda(\pv{X},+)\pv{\Pi}(+)
+\lambda(\pv{X},-)\pv{\Pi}(-), \\
\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\pv{X} &= \lambda(\pv{X},\pm)\pv{\Pi}(\pm).
\end{align*}
If \(f\) is defined at the two eigenvalues, then
\begin{align*}
f(\pv{X})
&=f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},+)\bigr)\pv{\Pi}(+)
+f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},-)\bigr)\pv{\Pi}(-)\notag\\
&=\tfrac12\left(
f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},+)\bigr)+f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},-)\bigr)\right)\notag\\
&\quad+\tfrac12\left(
f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},+)\bigr)-f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},-)\bigr)\right)
\vct{u}\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
Its scalar and vector parts are therefore
\begin{align*}
\eqtext{scalar}(f(\pv{X}))
&=\tfrac12\left(
f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},+)\bigr)+f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},-)\bigr)\right),\\
\eqtext{vector}(f(\pv{X}))
&=\tfrac12\left(
f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},+)\bigr)-f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},-)\bigr)\right)\vct{u}.
\end{align*}
For complex-valued \(f\), both parts may be complex.
The projector summary obtained from
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:real-paravector-eigenvalues} and \eqref{eq:projectors} may
therefore be recorded as
\begin{align*}
\lambda(\pv{X},\pm)&=t\pm\lVert\vct{r}\rVert,\notag\\
\vct{u}&=\vct{r}/\lVert\vct{r}\rVert,\notag\\
\pv{\Pi}(\pm)&=\tfrac12(1\pm\vct{u}\cdot\sigv),\notag\\
\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\pv{X}&=\lambda(\pv{X},\pm)\pv{\Pi}(\pm),\notag\\
\pv{X}&=\lambda(\pv{X},+)\pv{\Pi}(+)+\lambda(\pv{X},-)\pv{\Pi}(-),\notag\\
f(\pv{X})&=f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},+)\bigr)\pv{\Pi}(+)+f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},-)\bigr)\pv{\Pi}(-).
\end{align*}
The standard examples are
\begin{align*}
\exp(\pv{X})
&= \exp(t)\Bigl(\cosh(\lVert\vct{r}\rVert)+\sinh(\lVert\vct{r}\rVert)\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv\Bigr), \\
\sqrt{\pv{X}}
&= \epsilon(+)\sqrt{\lambda(\pv{X},+)}\,\pv{\Pi}(+)\notag\\
&\quad+\epsilon(-)\sqrt{\lambda(\pv{X},-)}\,\pv{\Pi}(-),\notag\\
&\qquad \epsilon(+),\epsilon(-)\in\{+1,-1\}, \\
\log(\pv{X})
&= \log(\lambda(\pv{X},+))\,\pv{\Pi}(+)
+\log(\lambda(\pv{X},-))\,\pv{\Pi}(-), \\
\pv{X}^{-1}
&= \frac{\pv{\Pi}(+)}{\lambda(\pv{X},+)}
+ \frac{\pv{\Pi}(-)}{\lambda(\pv{X},-)}\notag \\
&= \frac{\adjf{\pv{X}}}{\detf{\pv{X}}}
= \frac{\conj{\pv{X}}}{t^{2}-\vct{r}^{2}}, \\
\pv{X}^{n}
&= \lambda(\pv{X},+)^{n}\pv{\Pi}(+)
+\lambda(\pv{X},-)^{n}\pv{\Pi}(-)\notag \\
&= \tfrac12\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},+)^{n}+\lambda(\pv{X},-)^{n}\bigr)\notag\\
&\quad+\tfrac12\bigl(\lambda(\pv{X},+)^{n}-\lambda(\pv{X},-)^{n}\bigr)
\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
The scalar square roots and logarithms must be taken with specified choices
of their possible values. A square root that is itself a real paravector exists when
\(\lambda(\pv{X},\pm)\geq0\), equivalently
\(t\geq\lVert\vct{r}\rVert\); otherwise the
displayed roots are complex. The displayed logarithm is defined only when
\(\lambda(\pv{X},\pm)\neq0\). When both values are positive, the ordinary
single-valued real logarithm is
used; this choice is conventionally called the real principal logarithm.
The condition
\(\lambda(\pv{X},+)\lambda(\pv{X},-)\neq0\) is required for the inverse.
The power formula is valid for
\(n\geq0\), and also for negative integers when
\(\lambda(\pv{X},+)\lambda(\pv{X},-)\neq0\).
For two paravectors written with projector pairs along possibly different
axes, the unit axis of \(\pv{X}'\) is denoted by \(\vct{u}'\). The second projector pair is
the full specialization of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projectors},
\begin{equation}
\pv{\Pi}'(\pm):=\tfrac12(1\pm\vct{u}'\cdot\sigv).
\label{eq:second-axis-projectors}
\end{equation}
Here \((\vct{u}')^2=1\). Applying the eigenvalue function separately to the
two paravectors gives
\begin{align*}
f(\pv{X})g(\pv{X}')
&=\Bigl(f(\lambda(\pv{X},+))\pv{\Pi}(+)
+f(\lambda(\pv{X},-))\pv{\Pi}(-)\Bigr)\notag\\
&\quad\times\Bigl(g(\lambda(\pv{X}',+))\pv{\Pi}'(+)
+g(\lambda(\pv{X}',-))\pv{\Pi}'(-)\Bigr),
\end{align*}
so
\begin{align*}
f(\pv{X})g(\pv{X}')
&=f(\lambda(\pv{X},+))g(\lambda(\pv{X}',+))
\,\pv{\Pi}(+)\pv{\Pi}'(+)\notag\\
&\quad+f(\lambda(\pv{X},+))g(\lambda(\pv{X}',-))
\,\pv{\Pi}(+)\pv{\Pi}'(-)\notag\\
&\quad+f(\lambda(\pv{X},-))g(\lambda(\pv{X}',+))
\,\pv{\Pi}(-)\pv{\Pi}'(+)\notag\\
&\quad+f(\lambda(\pv{X},-))g(\lambda(\pv{X}',-))
\,\pv{\Pi}(-)\pv{\Pi}'(-).
\end{align*}
The mixed projector products are
\begin{align*}
\pv{\Pi}(+)\pv{\Pi}'(+)
&= \tfrac14(1+\vct{u}\cdot\vct{u}')\notag\\
&\quad + \tfrac14\bigl(\vct{u}'+\vct{u}+i(\vct{u}\times\vct{u}')\bigr)\cdot\sigv, \\
\pv{\Pi}(+)\pv{\Pi}'(-)
&= \tfrac14(1-\vct{u}\cdot\vct{u}')\notag\\
&\quad + \tfrac14\bigl(-\vct{u}'+\vct{u}-i(\vct{u}\times\vct{u}')\bigr)\cdot\sigv, \\
\pv{\Pi}(-)\pv{\Pi}'(+)
&= \tfrac14(1-\vct{u}\cdot\vct{u}')\notag\\
&\quad + \tfrac14\bigl(\vct{u}'-\vct{u}-i(\vct{u}\times\vct{u}')\bigr)\cdot\sigv, \\
\pv{\Pi}(-)\pv{\Pi}'(-)
&= \tfrac14(1+\vct{u}\cdot\vct{u}')\notag\\
&\quad + \tfrac14\bigl(-\vct{u}'-\vct{u}+i(\vct{u}\times\vct{u}')\bigr)\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
The hyperbolic tangent \(\tanh(x)\) is the ratio
\(\sinh(x)/\cosh(x)\). The function \(\operatorname{arctanh}(x)\) is its
inverse on the real interval \(-1<x<1\). As a useful example, the following
rational expression is itself a projector exponential:
\begin{align*}
\frac{1-\vct{r}\cdot\sigv}{1+\vct{r}\cdot\sigv}
&= \exp\!\bigl(-2\,\operatorname{arctanh}(\vct{r}\cdot\sigv)\bigr)\notag \\
&= \exp\!\bigl(-2\,\operatorname{arctanh}(\lVert\vct{r}\rVert)\bigr)\pv{\Pi}(+)\notag \\
&\quad + \exp\!\bigl(+2\,\operatorname{arctanh}(\lVert\vct{r}\rVert)\bigr)\pv{\Pi}(-).
\end{align*}
The rational expression is defined only when
\(\lVert\vct{r}\rVert\neq1\).
For a real-valued \(\operatorname{arctanh}\) and the displayed real
exponentials, the additional condition
\(\lVert\vct{r}\rVert<1\) is required; otherwise a complex branch must be
selected from the possible complex values. When \(\vct{r}=\vct{0}\), the
rational expression and exponential are both \(1\), and no axis is needed.

\subsubsection{Quadratic Relation and Projector Decomposition}
The function formulas, projector identities, and exponential identities
are determined by the following quadratic relation for a complex
paravector, as shown by Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:paravector-product} and
\eqref{eq:functional-calculus}:
\begin{align*}
\pv{Z} &= S + \vct{V} \cdot \sigv \in \Pspace(\C), \\
\pv{Z}^{2} - 2 S \pv{Z} + ( S^{2} - \vct{V}^{2} ) &= 0.
\end{align*}
When \(\vct{V}^{2}\neq0\), a value \(z\) with \(z^2=\vct{V}^2\) is chosen. The two
scalar values associated with the two projector parts are defined by
\begin{equation}
\lambda(\pv{Z},\pm):=S\pm z.
\label{eq:complex-paravector-eigenvalues}
\end{equation}
The corresponding projector function is defined by
\begin{equation}
\pv{\Pi}(\pv{Z},\pm):=\frac12\left(1\pm
\frac{\vct{V}\cdot\sigv}{z}\right).
\label{eq:complex-paravector-projectors}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{align*}
( \pv{Z} - \lambda(\pv{Z},-) )
( \pv{Z} - \lambda(\pv{Z},+) ) &= 0,\\
\pv{\Pi}(\pv{Z},\pm)\pv{Z}
&=\lambda(\pv{Z},\pm)\pv{\Pi}(\pv{Z},\pm),\\
\pv{Z}&=\lambda(\pv{Z},+)\pv{\Pi}(\pv{Z},+)\notag\\
&\quad+\lambda(\pv{Z},-)\pv{\Pi}(\pv{Z},-),\\
f(\pv{Z})&=f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{Z},+)\bigr)\pv{\Pi}(\pv{Z},+)
 \notag\\
&\quad+f\bigl(\lambda(\pv{Z},-)\bigr)\pv{\Pi}(\pv{Z},-)
\end{align*}
for any scalar function defined on the two values. Because multiplication
by \(\pv{Z}\) gives each projector back multiplied by its associated scalar,
these scalars are the eigenvalues of \(\pv{Z}\). The two labels are
merely exchanged when the sign of \(z\) is changed.
Since \(\vct{V}^{2}\neq0\) makes \(\pv{Z}\) non-scalar, the displayed
quadratic is its minimal polynomial.

The case \(\vct{V}^2=0\) must be treated separately. If
\(\vct{V}=\vct{0}\), then \(\pv{Z}=S\) is scalar, its minimal polynomial
is \(x-S\), and \(f(\pv{Z})=f(S)\). If
\(\vct{V}\neq\vct{0}\), define
\(\pv{N}:=\vct{V}\cdot\sigv\). Then \(\pv{N}^2=0\). A nonzero element whose
positive power is zero is called nilpotent. In this case
\((\pv{Z}-S)^2=0\), so the minimal polynomial is \((x-S)^2\).
\(\pv{Z}=S+\pv{N}\) cannot be changed to a diagonal matrix by an invertible
change of basis, so it is not diagonalizable. For a function with a
convergent power series about \(S\),
\begin{equation*}
f(\pv{Z})=f(S)+f'(S)\pv{N}.
\end{equation*}
Every term containing \(\pv{N}^2\) or a higher power vanishes, which proves
the formula directly. It is the exact replacement for the two-projector
formula when the two roots become equal.
