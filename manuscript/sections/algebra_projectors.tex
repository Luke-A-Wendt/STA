\subsubsection*{Functions of a Unit-Direction Element}
The projector pair \(\Pi(\pm)\) gives the two-root decomposition of the unit-direction element \(\mathbf u\cdot\sigv\). The corresponding eigenvalue data are
\begin{align*}
\Pi(\pm) &= \tfrac{1}{2}(1\pm \mathbf u\cdot\sigv), \\
\Pi(\pm)^{2} &= \Pi(\pm), \\
\Pi(+)\Pi(-) &= 0, \\
\Pi(+) + \Pi(-) &= 1, \\
(\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)\Pi(\pm) &= \pm \Pi(\pm),
\end{align*}
so \(\mathbf u\cdot\sigv\) has the two eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\). Any function with a convergent power series therefore collapses to those two scalar values:
\begin{align*}
f(\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)
&= f(+1)\Pi(+) + f(-1)\Pi(-)\notag \\
&= \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(f(+1)+f(-1)\bigr)
+ \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(f(+1)-f(-1)\bigr)\,\mathbf u\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
These are the basic projector formulas for a unit-direction element. The first examples below are chosen to illustrate the calculus before the dedicated exponential and logarithm subsections:
\begin{align*}
\sqrt{\mathbf u\cdot\sigv} &= \pm \Pi(+) \pm i\,\Pi(-), \\
(z+\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)^{-1}
&= \frac{1}{z+1}\Pi(+) + \frac{1}{z-1}\Pi(-)\notag \\
&= \frac{z-\mathbf u\cdot\sigv}{z^{2}-1}, \\
\frac{i-\mathbf u\cdot\sigv}{i+\mathbf u\cdot\sigv} &= i\,\mathbf u\cdot\sigv,
\end{align*}
where the square root, inverse, and Cayley-type transform are obtained by direct substitution into Eq.\ \eqref{eq:functional-calculus}. The exponential and logarithm cases are developed separately below so that the general projector method is in place first.

\subsubsection*{Functions of a Paravector}
Let
\begin{align*}
X &= t + \mathbf r\cdot\sigv, \\
t &= \eqtext{scalar}(X)\in\mathbb R, \\
\mathbf r &= \eqtext{vector}(X)\in\mathbb R^{3}.
\end{align*}
If \(\mathbf r\neq 0\), write \(\mathbf r=\lVert\mathbf r\rVert \mathbf u\) with \(\mathbf u^{2}=1\). Then the minimal polynomial of \(X\) is
\begin{align*}
X^{2} - 2 t X + ( t^{2} - \mathbf r^{2} ) &= 0,\notag \\
\lambda(\pm) &= t \pm \lVert\mathbf r\rVert,\notag \\
0 &= (X-\lambda(-))(X-\lambda(+)),
\end{align*}
so \(X\) has the projector decomposition
\begin{align*}
\Pi(\pm) &= \tfrac12(1\pm \mathbf u\cdot\sigv), \\
X &= \lambda(+)\Pi(+) + \lambda(-)\Pi(-), \\
\Pi(\pm)X &= \lambda(\pm)\Pi(\pm).
\end{align*}
Any analytic function of \(X\) is therefore obtained by applying the scalar function to the two eigenvalues:
\begin{align*}
f(X)
&= f\bigl(\lambda(+)\bigr)\Pi(+) + f\bigl(\lambda(-)\bigr)\Pi(-)\notag \\
&= \tfrac12\Bigl(f\bigl(\lambda(+)\bigr)+f\bigl(\lambda(-)\bigr)\Bigr)\notag \\
&\quad + \tfrac12\Bigl(f\bigl(\lambda(+)\bigr)-f\bigl(\lambda(-)\bigr)\Bigr)\,\mathbf u\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
If \(f(X)=t'+\mathbf r'\cdot\sigv\), then
\begin{align*}
t' &= \tfrac12\Bigl(f\bigl(\lambda(+)\bigr)+f\bigl(\lambda(-)\bigr)\Bigr), \\
\lVert\mathbf r'\rVert &= \tfrac12\Bigl(f\bigl(\lambda(+)\bigr)-f\bigl(\lambda(-)\bigr)\Bigr), \\
\mathbf r' &= \lVert\mathbf r'\rVert\,\mathbf u.
\end{align*}
Before turning to exponentials and logarithms, the same construction already gives the useful square-root, inverse, and power formulas:
\begin{align*}
\sqrt{X} &= \pm \sqrt{\lambda(+)}\,\Pi(+) \pm \sqrt{\lambda(-)}\,\Pi(-), \\
X^{-1}
&= \frac{\Pi(+)}{\lambda(+)} + \frac{\Pi(-)}{\lambda(-)}\notag \\
&= \frac{X^{*}}{t^{2}-\mathbf r^{2}}, \\
X^{n} &= \lambda(+)^{n}\Pi(+) + \lambda(-)^{n}\Pi(-).
\end{align*}
When two different paravectors are diagonalized along different directions, the only new ingredient is the mixed projector product. Let
\(X=t+\lVert\mathbf r\rVert\,\mathbf u\cdot\sigv\) and
\(X'=t'+\lVert\mathbf r'\rVert\,\mathbf u'\cdot\sigv\) be decomposed along different axes. Their functions then multiply by combining the two projector families:
\begin{equation*}
f(X)g(X')
= \sum_{s,s'=\pm} f\bigl(\lambda(s)\bigr)\,g\bigl(\lambda'(s')\bigr)\,\Pi(s)\Pi'(s').
\end{equation*}
The mixed projector products are
\begin{equation*}
\Pi(s)\Pi'(s')
= \tfrac14\Bigl(
1 + ss'\,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u'
+ \bigl(s\mathbf u + s'\mathbf u' + ss' i(\mathbf u\times\mathbf u')\bigr)\cdot\sigv
\Bigr),
\end{equation*}
with \(s,s'\in\{+1,-1\}\). This compact formula reproduces all four explicit same-side and cross-side products.
As a useful example, the Cayley-type transform is itself a projector exponential:
\begin{align*}
\frac{1-\mathbf r\cdot\sigv}{1+\mathbf r\cdot\sigv}
&= \exp\!\bigl(-2\,\operatorname{arctanh}(\mathbf r\cdot\sigv)\bigr)\notag \\
&= e^{-2\operatorname{arctanh}(\lVert\mathbf r\rVert)}\Pi(+)
+ e^{+2\operatorname{arctanh}(\lVert\mathbf r\rVert)}\Pi(-).
\end{align*}

\subsubsection*{Minimal Polynomial and Projector Decomposition}
The two-root data of a complex paravector are collected here because the later function formulas, projector identities, and exponential identities all depend on this algebraic factorization; see Eq.\ \eqref{eq:functional-calculus}:
\begin{align*}
Z &= S + \mathbf V \cdot \sigv \in \C\oplus\C^{3}, \\
Z^{2} - 2 S Z + ( S^{2} - \mathbf V^{2} ) &= 0, \\
\lambda (\pm ) &= S \pm \sqrt{ \mathbf V^{2} }, \\
( Z - \lambda (-) ) ( Z - \lambda (+) ) &= 0.
\end{align*}
When \(\mathbf V^{2}\neq 0\), these two eigenvalues provide the projector data needed for the same functional calculus used in the real-paravector case.
