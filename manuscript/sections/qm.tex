\subsubsection*{Spin-0 Wave Mechanics and Frequency Splitting}
Equation \eqref{eq:kg-free} is most transparent when read mode by mode. For a plane-wave ansatz
\begin{equation*}
\Psi(t,\mathbf r)=c\,e^{-i\omega t+i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r},
\end{equation*}
the Klein--Gordon equation becomes \(\omega^2=\mathbf k^2+m^2\). The operator \(\Omega\) in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:frequency-split} therefore acts on each Fourier mode by multiplication with the positive root
\begin{equation*}
\omega_{\mathbf k}=\sqrt{\mathbf k^2+m^2}.
\end{equation*}
This is the analytic content of the formal factorization
\[
\partial_t^2+\Omega^2=(\partial_t-i\Omega)(\partial_t+i\Omega).
\]
The decomposition \(\Psi=\Psi(+)+\Psi(-)\) is thus not merely notation: it separates the positive- and negative-frequency sectors of the scalar theory in the same way that the Fourier transform separates the two energy branches \cite{greiner2000}.

\subsubsection*{Root Operators and Fourier Interpretation}
The square-root operators in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:frequency-split} are easiest to interpret in Fourier space. If
\begin{equation*}
\Psi=\sum_{n} c_{n}e^{i(\omega_{n}t+\mathbf k_{n}\cdot\mathbf r)},
\end{equation*}
then
\begin{equation*}
\square\Psi=\sum_{n}(\mathbf k_{n}^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2})c_{n}e^{i(\omega_{n}t+\mathbf k_{n}\cdot\mathbf r)},
\qquad
\Omega\Psi=\sum_{n}\sqrt{m^{2}+\mathbf k_{n}^{2}}\,c_{n}e^{i(\omega_{n}t+\mathbf k_{n}\cdot\mathbf r)}.
\end{equation*}
The factorization
\begin{equation*}
\square+m^{2}=(m-i\sqrt{\square})(m+i\sqrt{\square})
\end{equation*}
is therefore a shorthand for multiplying each Fourier mode by the corresponding dispersion function. The apparently formal root operator is nothing more than the spectral calculus already introduced in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:functional-calculus}, now applied to the wave operator.

\subsubsection*{Minimal Coupling and Operator Factorization}
Once the potential \(\Phi\) has been introduced, it is more economical to couple at the operator level than at the component level. Acting on a smooth test field \(\Psi\), the operators from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:E-P-operators} satisfy
\begin{equation*}
[E,P_m]\Psi=iqE_m\Psi,
\qquad
[P_m,P_n]\Psi=iq(\partial_mA_n-\partial_nA_m)\Psi.
\end{equation*}
These commutators are the exact place where the electromagnetic field enters the coupled wave mechanics: electric components arise from time-space commutators, while magnetic components arise from the noncommutativity of spatial covariant momenta.

Expanding the squared operators themselves gives
\begin{align*}
E^2\Psi &= \left(-\partial_t^2-iq\,\partial_tV-2iqV\partial_t+q^2V^2\right)\Psi, \\
\mathbf P^2\Psi &= \left(-\gradv^2+iq\,\gradv\cdot\mathbf A+2iq\,\mathbf A\cdot\gradv+q^2\mathbf A^2\right)\Psi.
\end{align*}
Subtracting these expressions and then adding \(m^2\Psi\) is the direct operator expansion behind Eq.\ \eqref{eq:kg-expanded}.

Substituting those commutators into the product of first-order factors yields
\begin{equation*}
(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)(E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)\Psi
=\bigl(E^2-\mathbf P^2-iqF\bigr)\Psi,
\end{equation*}
while reversing the order gives
\begin{equation*}
(E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)\Psi
=\bigl(E^2-\mathbf P^2-iqF^*\bigr)\Psi.
\end{equation*}
This is the structural statement encoded in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:EP-factorization}. The second-order Klein--Gordon operator is recovered from the square of a first-order object, and the field term carried by \(F\) is exactly what remains from the noncommuting covariant momenta.

\subsubsection*{Free Spin-\texorpdfstring{$\frac12$}{1/2} Factorization}
Before the electromagnetic field is turned on, the Dirac block map already shows how the scalar and spinor theories are related. With
\begin{equation*}
D_{0}:=W(i\partial)-m\I,
\qquad
D_{0}':=W(i\partial)+m\I,
\end{equation*}
one has
\begin{equation*}
D_{0}'D_{0}=-(\square+m^{2})\I.
\end{equation*}
Thus every free Dirac solution satisfies the Klein--Gordon equation componentwise. Conversely, if \(\Psi'\) is a scalar Klein--Gordon solution, then
\begin{equation*}
\Psi=
\begin{Bmatrix}
\Psi'\\[2pt]
\dfrac{i}{m}\,\partial^*\Psi'
\end{Bmatrix}
\end{equation*}
solves the free Dirac system. The Dirac equation is therefore a controlled first-order factorization of the same relativistic mass shell rather than a disconnected model.

\subsubsection*{The Dirac Block System, Gauge Covariance, and Component Form}
The block map \(W(Z)\) is useful because it converts a paravector \(Z\) into a first-order matrix operator whose square is diagonal:
\[
W(Z)^2=\operatorname{diag}(Z\crev{Z},\crev{Z}Z).
\]
With
\[
Z=i\partial-q\conj{\Phi}=E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P,
\]
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled} becomes a first-order system for the two block components \(\Psi_1\) and \(\Psi_2\):
\begin{equation*}
(i\partial-q\Phi^*)\Psi_2-m\Psi_1=0,
\qquad
(i\partial^*-q\Phi)\Psi_1-m\Psi_2=0.
\end{equation*}
At the block-operator level this means
\begin{equation*}
W(i\partial-q\conj{\Phi})^2
=-\operatorname{diag}\!\Big((\partial+iq\conj{\Phi})(\conj{\partial}+iq\Phi),
(\conj{\partial}+iq\Phi)(\partial+iq\conj{\Phi})\Big).
\end{equation*}
Multiplying by the mass-conjugate operator \(D'=W(Z)+m\I\) gives
\begin{equation*}
D'D\Psi=
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2-iqF^*,
-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2+iqF\bigr)\Psi.
\end{equation*}
The upper and lower components satisfy Klein--Gordon-type equations with opposite field couplings, exactly as recorded in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-square}. The sign pattern matters, which is why the caution recorded in Section \ref{sec:flags} is retained explicitly.

Gauge covariance is then immediate. If
\[
\Phi'=\Phi+\conj{\partial}\lambda,
\qquad
\Psi=e^{iq\lambda}\Psi',
\]
then the phase derivative produced by \(i\partial\) is cancelled by the shift in the potential, leaving the form of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled} unchanged. The direct component actions of \(\partial\), \(\partial^*\), \(\Phi\), \(\Phi^*\), \(iqF\), and \(iqF^*\) are recorded in Tables~\ref{tab:components-partial-psi}--\ref{tab:components-iqfstar-psi}; those tables supply the explicit two-component formulas corresponding to this block system.

\subsubsection*{Low-Energy Scalar and Spinor Limits}
To reach the nonrelativistic scalar theory, remove the fast rest-energy oscillation by writing \(\Psi'=e^{-imt}\Psi\). Substituting into the minimally coupled Klein--Gordon equation gives
\begin{equation*}
0=\bigl(-E^{2}-2mE+\mathbf P^{2}\bigr)\Psi,
\end{equation*}
so at leading order in \(1/m\),
\begin{equation*}
E\Psi\approx \frac{\mathbf P^{2}}{2m}\Psi.
\end{equation*}
Solving for \(i\partial_t\Psi\) yields the Schr\"odinger form
\begin{equation*}
i\partial_t\Psi
=\left(\frac{(-i\gradv-q\mathbf A)^2}{2m}+qV\right)\Psi+O(1/m^{2}).
\end{equation*}
The scalar low-energy limit is therefore already encoded in the relativistic theory.

For the spin-\(\tfrac12\) system, one performs the same rest-phase extraction and then eliminates the small component iteratively. At leading order this yields
\begin{equation*}
2mE\Psi=(\mathbf P^2-q\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)\Psi+O(1/m),
\end{equation*}
which is Eq.\ \eqref{eq:pauli-operator}. Solving for \(i\partial_t\Psi\) gives Eq.\ \eqref{eq:pauli}: the kinetic Schr\"odinger operator is supplemented by the Zeeman term \(-\tfrac{q}{2m}\mathbf B\cdot\sigv\) \cite{pauli1927,sakurai2017}. The derivation is important because it shows that spin coupling is not appended by hand; it survives as the leading field correction left behind by the relativistic first-order theory.

For a uniform static magnetic field \(\mathbf B=\|\mathbf B\|\mathbf u\), the projector family from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projectors} diagonalizes the Zeeman term:
\begin{equation*}
(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)\Pi(\pm)=\pm \|\mathbf B\|\,\Pi(\pm).
\end{equation*}
The decomposition \(\Psi=\Psi(+)+\Psi(-)\) with \(\Psi(\pm)=\Pi(\pm)\Psi\) therefore separates the Pauli equation into the two scalar Schr\"odinger-type equations shown in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:pauli-spin-split}. It is a clear example of a purely algebraic construction acquiring an immediate physical interpretation.
