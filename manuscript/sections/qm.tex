\subsubsection{Relativistic Quantum-Mechanical Setting}
One fixed operator language is used throughout the wave-equation section. With the differential conventions from Section \ref{sec:differential},
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial}&=\partial_t+\sigv\cdot\gradv,\\
\conj{\pv{\partial}}&=\partial_t-\sigv\cdot\gradv,\\
\conj{(i\pv{\partial})}&=-i\conj{\pv{\partial}}.
\end{align*}
The d'Alembertian is
\begin{align*}
\Box
&=\partial_t^2-\gradv^2\notag\\
&=\pv{\partial}\conj{\pv{\partial}}
=\conj{\pv{\partial}}\pv{\partial}.
\end{align*}
All fields are acted on by the differential operators to their left. In an ordered
product, the action of the rightmost factor is applied first, and the written order is retained
whenever potentials and derivatives do not commute.

\subsubsection{Algebra Blocks and the Physical Spinor Carrier}
Throughout the spinful part of the wave-equation section, a wave field is not a
space-time event but an algebra-valued amplitude field.  A generic algebra
block may be written
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\psi}:\R\times\R^3\to\C+\C^3,
\qquad
\pv{\psi}(t,\vct{r})=\psi_s(t,\vct{r})+\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}(t,\vct{r})\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
The location at which the state is evaluated in space-time is specified by the argument \((t,\vct{r})\), while the value \(\pv{\psi}(t,\vct{r})\) is contained in the internal complex-paravector algebra. The real paravector
\begin{equation*}
\pv{X}=t+\vct{r}\cdot\sigv
\end{equation*}
is used to describe an event or interval variable, whereas \(\pv{\psi}\) is a quantum state amplitude defined \emph{over} such events.

At each fixed point, four complex coefficients are contained in an
unrestricted complex paravector. In the Pauli matrix realization it is an arbitrary
\(2\times2\) complex matrix, and its two columns are acted on independently
by left multiplication. Two independent two-entry columns are therefore contained,
not one physical wave-function block. One column is selected without adding
component indices by the fixed selector \(\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})\) from
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:fixed-column-carrier}, and a physical block is restricted by
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\psi}=\pv{\psi}\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column}).
\end{equation*}
The column is selected by the fixed factor on the right. Every displayed operator
is applied on the left, so the restriction is preserved by associativity:
\((\pv{Z}\pv{\psi})\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})
=\pv{Z}(\pv{\psi}\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column}))=\pv{Z}\pv{\psi}\).
The factor \(\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})\) is only a column selector; it
is not a measurement of spin along the third axis.

The scalar and vector coefficients of an algebra block are amplitude channels,
not separately measurable particles or classical subfields.  A bare component
count also does not cause physical spin to be assigned: the transformation law, field
equation, and carrier restriction must be specified together.

The field \(\psi\in\C\) is used in the spin-0 Klein--Gordon subsections. A
two-block field is used in the Dirac construction:
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi=
\begin{Bmatrix}
\bPsi_1\\[2pt]
\bPsi_2
\end{Bmatrix},
\end{equation*}
with \(\bPsi_n=\bPsi_n\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})\). Two complex amplitudes are
then carried by each block, and the four complex amplitudes of one Dirac
field are carried by the pair. If the fixed-column restriction is omitted,
the same equations remain algebraically valid but are applicable to two
independent Dirac fields, one in each matrix column.

\subsubsection{Complex-Paravector Bilinears and Bra-Ket Extraction}
The bra-ket notation is made precise in the paravector setting by the
decompositions
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\pv{\psi}&=\psi_s+\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}\cdot\sigv,
&&
\pv{Z}&=Z_s+\vct{Z}_{\vct{v}}\cdot\sigv,
&
\pv{X}&=X_s+\vct{X}_{\vct{v}}\cdot\sigv,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
with \(\psi_s,Z_s\in\C\), \(\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}},\vct{Z}_{\vct{v}}\in\C^3\),
\(X_s\in\R\), \(\vct{X}_{\vct{v}}\in\R^3\), and with fields acted on by
operator-valued components to their left. The three involutions on the state
are
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\conj{\pv{\psi}}&=\psi_s^*-\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}^*\cdot\sigv,\\
\herm{\pv{\psi}}&=\psi_s^*+\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}^*\cdot\sigv,\\
\cherm{\pv{\psi}}&=\psi_s-\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}\cdot\sigv.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The reason for the \(H\) pairing is made explicit by
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:matrix-operation-dictionary}. If \(\pv{\phi}\) and
\(\pv{\psi}\) are realized by the fixed right projector as the two-entry columns
\(\{a;b\}\) and \(\{c;d\}\), then
\begin{equation}
2\,\eqtext{scalar}(\herm{\pv{\phi}}\pv{\psi})
=\eqtext{tr}(\herm{\pv{\phi}}\pv{\psi})
=\begin{Bmatrix}a^*&b^*\end{Bmatrix}
 \begin{Bmatrix}c\\d\end{Bmatrix}
=a^*c+b^*d.
\label{eq:hermitian-column-pairing}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\herm{\pv{\phi}}\) is precisely the familiar row bra in the
selected-column realization, and
\(2\,\eqtext{scalar}(\herm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\psi})\) is its nonnegative
ordinary column norm. The factor \(2\) is required because intrinsic
scalar extraction is one half of the matrix trace. By summation of this
relation over the two projected Dirac blocks, the density is obtained; the
spatial current is instead obtained from the opposite-order block bilinears
fixed by the Dirac equation.
The basic component products are collected below.

For \(S\in\R\), the Hermitian sandwich is
\begin{equation*}
\herm{\pv{\psi}}S\pv{\psi}
=S\herm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\psi},
\end{equation*}
whose physical selected-column density was defined in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projected-block-density-function}. Its local form is
\begin{equation*}
\rho(\pv{\psi})
=2\,\eqtext{scalar}(\herm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\psi})
=2\bigl(|\psi_s|^2
+\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}^*\cdot\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}\bigr)\geq0.
\end{equation*}
In the matrix realization scalar extraction is one half of the trace, so
\(\rho(\pv{\psi})\) is the conventional selected-column norm. The factor is
retained explicitly in the scalar-extraction formula.
The time-slice norm for a projected Pauli carrier and, after summation over
both blocks, for a Dirac carrier is supplied by this positive scalar. It is not the
conserved Klein--Gordon norm of a scalar relativistic field. The vector part of
\(\herm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\psi}\), however, is not automatically a spatial probability
current.  In fact, under the active spinor convention
\(\pv{\psi}'=\herm{\pv{R}}\pv{\psi}\), it is invariant:
\begin{equation*}
\herm{\pv{\psi}'}\pv{\psi}'
=\herm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{R}\herm{\pv{R}}\pv{\psi}
=\herm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\psi}.
\end{equation*}
Spatial spin or polarization information instead occurs in bilinears with the
opposite order, such as \(\pv{\psi}\herm{\pv{\psi}}\), or in operator sandwiches fixed by
the field equation. More generally, four complex, hence eight real,
coefficient channels are contained in
\(\herm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{\psi}\) before an extraction is chosen. No
generic physical interpretation is assigned to its nonscalar channels.

For a nonzero projected block, the scale is defined by
\begin{equation}
N:=\rho(\pv{\psi}).
\label{eq:projected-block-scale}
\end{equation}
The following useful opposite-order identities are obtained from the fixed-column condition:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\herm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\psi}&=N\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column}),\\
\herm{\left(\frac{\pv{\psi}\herm{\pv{\psi}}}{N}\right)}
&=\frac{\pv{\psi}\herm{\pv{\psi}}}{N},\\
\left(\frac{\pv{\psi}\herm{\pv{\psi}}}{N}\right)^2
&=\frac{\pv{\psi}\herm{\pv{\psi}}}{N}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:projected-block-density}
\end{equation}
Only the selected right column is retained by the first product. In the
matrix realization the second product is the ordinary rank-one pure-state
density matrix; its determinant is zero and twice the vector part of
\(\pv{\psi}\herm{\pv{\psi}}\) is the selected column's sigma-polarization
vector. This is the familiar geometry of a two-entry state, not an additional
paravector degree of freedom.

At fixed time, the spatially integrated bracket defined in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:adjoint-paravector-braket} is
\begin{equation*}
\langle \pv{\phi} \mid \pv{\psi} \rangle
=2\iiint\eqtext{scalar}(\herm{\pv{\phi}}\pv{\psi})\,
\dd r_1\,\dd r_2\,\dd r_3.
\end{equation*}
Conversion of intrinsic scalar extraction, which is one half of the matrix
trace, into the ordinary inner product of the selected two-entry column in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:hermitian-column-pairing} is provided by the factor \(2\). It is therefore a
normalization convention rather than an additional physical factor.
For a projected one-block Pauli carrier, the normalization is its equal-field
specialization,
\begin{equation*}
\langle \pv{\psi}\mid\pv{\psi}\rangle
=\iiint\rho(\pv{\psi})\,\dd r_1\,\dd r_2\,\dd r_3
=1.
\end{equation*}
For a complex-paravector operator \(\pv{\mathcal{O}}\),
\begin{equation*}
\langle \pv{\psi}\mid\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{\psi}\rangle
=2\iiint\eqtext{scalar}(\herm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{\psi})\,
\dd r_1\,\dd r_2\,\dd r_3.
\end{equation*}
For a state that is not already normalized, its normalized scalar matrix
element is the ratio
\begin{equation}
\frac{\langle\pv{\psi}\mid\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{\psi}\rangle}
{\langle\pv{\psi}\mid\pv{\psi}\rangle}.
\label{eq:expectation-map}
\end{equation}
If \(\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{\psi}=\phi_s+\vct{\phi}_{\vct{v}}\cdot\sigv\), then the extracted scalar channel is simply
\begin{equation*}
\eqtext{scalar}(\herm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{\psi})
=\psi_s^*\phi_s+\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}^*\cdot\vct{\phi}_{\vct{v}}.
\end{equation*}
When \(\pv{\mathcal{O}}\) is Hermitian under the spatial integral and the
boundary terms are benign, this map is a real expectation value. For a
general \(\pv{\mathcal{O}}\), it is only a normalized matrix element and may be complex.
For the Dirac field the physical norm is the sum of this same selected-column
pairing over its two restricted blocks; the associated conserved spatial
current is supplied by the field equation. No separately conserved norm is
assigned to either massive Dirac block.

By contrast, for an unrestricted algebra-valued paravector field,
\begin{equation*}
\adjf{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\psi}
=\psi_s^2-\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}\cdot\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}
=\detf{\pv{\psi}}
\end{equation*}
is the determinant quadratic form. It is algebraically useful, but it is not
positive-definite and so it should not be used as the probabilistic
normalization. Moreover, after the fixed-projector restriction to one matrix
column, this determinant vanishes identically because the rank of the resulting
matrix is at most one. In this framework the single bra-ket bracket
\(\langle\cdot\mid\cdot\rangle\) is therefore reserved for the scalar
expectation map built from \(H\).

\subsubsection{Carrier Interpretation and Pairings}
In standard physics, spin is not assigned from component count alone. The physically relevant data are
\begin{equation*}
(\text{transformation law},\ \text{field equation},\ \text{constraints}).
\end{equation*}
Thus the spin-zero Klein--Gordon field is carried by a complex scalar only
after its scalar transformation law and field equation are imposed. A
one-half-unit Dirac block is carried by a selected two-entry column only after
the paired first-order equations are imposed. The spin-one electromagnetic
field is carried by the pure-vector complex paravector \(\pv{F}\) only with
its field transformation law and Maxwell equation. A connection between the present
construction and the standard classification is made by these statements in
words, without introduction of another symbolic notation.

\noindent\textbf{Bilinear pairings.}
For two complex-paravector fields \(\pv{\phi}\) and \(\pv{\psi}\), the three scalar pairings are
\begin{align*}
\eqtext{scalar}(\herm{\pv{\phi}}\pv{\psi})
&=\phi_s^*\psi_s+\vct{\phi}_{\vct{v}}^*\cdot\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}},\\
\eqtext{scalar}(\cherm{\pv{\phi}}\pv{\psi})
&=\phi_s\psi_s-\vct{\phi}_{\vct{v}}\cdot\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}},\\
\eqtext{scalar}(\conj{\pv{\phi}}\pv{\psi})
&=\phi_s^*\psi_s-\vct{\phi}_{\vct{v}}^*\cdot\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}.
\end{align*}
When the two fields agree, the first line is the positive Hermitian pairing;
the second is the determinant extension; and the third is its indefinite
companion.
For \(\pv{\phi}=\pv{\psi}\), the determinant extension reduces to
\begin{equation*}
\eqtext{scalar}(\cherm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\psi})
=\adjf{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\psi}
=\detf{\pv{\psi}}.
\end{equation*}
Only the Hermitian pairing is being used here as the quantum bra-ket pairing.
The equal-argument determinant is the intrinsic quadratic form used in
factorization identities, and the companion pairing is algebraically
well-defined but does not carry an independent standard quantum
interpretation here.

For the spatial bracket already defined in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:adjoint-paravector-braket}, an operator \(\pv{\mathcal{O}}\) is
Hermitian on the chosen fields and
boundary conditions when
\[
\langle\pv{\phi}\mid\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{\psi}\rangle
=\langle\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{\phi}\mid\pv{\psi}\rangle.
\]
For left multiplication by a fixed paravector \(\pv{Z}\), moving the
operator to the first field replaces it by \(\herm{\pv{Z}}\), because
\(\herm{(\herm{\pv{Z}}\pv{\phi})}=\herm{\pv{\phi}}\pv{Z}\).
For a pointwise multiplication operator, \(H\) is exactly the matrix
Hermitian adjoint from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:matrix-operation-dictionary}. For a
differential operator, integration by parts, its domain, and the boundary
terms must also be included before it can be called Hermitian under the
spatial bracket. Nonscalar parts of
\(\herm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{\psi}\) remain available as algebraic bilinears, but a current,
flux, or polarization interpretation must be established separately for the
chosen operator and field equation.

\noindent\textbf{Generalized operator products.}
The following identities are algebraic. Operator composition on a paravector-valued state is described by them, but no physical spin is assigned by them alone.

For operator-valued components, reduction of the commutator to the pure
cross-product law of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:paravector-commutator} no longer occurs.
For compactness, the scalar operator is denoted by \(S\) and the vector
operator by \(\vct{V}\):
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\mathcal{O}}=S+\vct{V}\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
\pv{\mathcal{O}}'=S'+\vct{V}'\cdot\sigv,
\end{equation*}
with the state acted on by component operators to its left; those operators
commute with the fixed basis elements. Then, with dot and cross products taken componentwise
and in the written operator order,
\begin{align}
\com{\pv{\mathcal{O}}}{\pv{\mathcal{O}}'}\pv{\psi}
&=\Bigl(\com{S}{S'}
+\vct{V}\cdot \vct{V}'
-\vct{V}'\cdot \vct{V}\Bigr)\pv{\psi}\notag\\
&\quad+\Bigl(\com{S}{\vct{V}'}
+\com{\vct{V}}{S'}\notag\\
&\qquad\qquad
+i\bigl(\vct{V}\times \vct{V}'
-\vct{V}'\times \vct{V}\bigr)\Bigr)\cdot\sigv\,\pv{\psi},
\label{eq:operator-commutator-action}
\end{align}
where the scalar-vector commutators are understood componentwise. Unlike the
purely algebraic paravector commutator, both scalar and vector parts are
generally contained in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:operator-commutator-action}. Only when
all component operators mutually commute is the following simpler law
obtained:
\begin{equation*}
\com{\pv{\mathcal{O}}}{\pv{\mathcal{O}}'}\pv{\psi}
=2i\bigl(\vct{V}\times \vct{V}'\bigr)\cdot\sigv\,\pv{\psi}.
\end{equation*}

The matching anticommutator is
\begin{align}
\acom{\pv{\mathcal{O}}}{\pv{\mathcal{O}}'}\pv{\psi}
&=(\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{\mathcal{O}}'+\pv{\mathcal{O}}'\pv{\mathcal{O}})\pv{\psi}\notag\\
&=\Bigl(\acom{S}{S'}
+\vct{V}\cdot \vct{V}'
+\vct{V}'\cdot \vct{V}\Bigr)\pv{\psi}\notag\\
&\quad+\Bigl(\acom{S}{\vct{V}'}\notag\\
&\qquad\qquad
+\acom{\vct{V}}{S'}\notag\\
&\qquad\qquad
+i\bigl(\vct{V}\times \vct{V}'
+\vct{V}'\times \vct{V}\bigr)\Bigr)\cdot\sigv\,\pv{\psi},
\label{eq:operator-anticommutator-action}
\end{align}
so that
\begin{align*}
\pv{\mathcal{O}}\pv{\mathcal{O}}'
&=\tfrac12\bigl(\acom{\pv{\mathcal{O}}}{\pv{\mathcal{O}}'}+\com{\pv{\mathcal{O}}}{\pv{\mathcal{O}}'}\bigr).
\end{align*}
This is the natural companion to the operator commutator formula. A covariance
or uncertainty identity can be formed from it only after a state space, expectation
functional, Hermitian operators, and common domains have been specified; no
such physical statement is obtained from this algebraic identity alone.

\noindent\textbf{Carrier closure.}
The carrier spaces that are closed under the respective operators are displayed by the same algebra:
\begin{align}
\pv{\partial}:\C&\to\C+\C^3,\notag\\
\pv{\partial}:\{0\}+\C^3&\to\C+\C^3,\notag\\
\pv{\partial}:\C+\C^3&\to\C+\C^3,\notag\\
\pv{\partial}:(\C+\C^3)\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})
&\to(\C+\C^3)\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column}).
\label{eq:carrier-closure}
\end{align}
Indeed,
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial}\psi_s
&=(\partial_t\psi_s)+(\gradv\psi_s)\cdot\sigv,\\
\pv{\partial}(\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}\cdot\sigv)
&=(\gradv\cdot\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}})
+\bigl(\partial_t\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}+i\,\gradv\times\vct{\psi}_{\vct{v}}\bigr)\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
The full complex-paravector space is the smallest algebra containing the scalar
and vector channels, but the projected fixed-column carrier is smaller.
Two complex amplitudes are carried by the carrier
\((\C+\C^3)\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})\), selected by a fixed right factor, and
closure is preserved because every differential and potential operator is
applied on the left. Closure of the scalar subspace is obtained only after
passage to scalar second-order combinations such as \(\Box+m^2\). A
pure-vector specialization is
algebraically meaningful, but it is not preserved by generic first-order
paravector action; Maxwell equations or separately defined massive-vector
constraints are therefore required if a spin-1 field is to be described by it.
A real-paravector specialization is not a
general quantum carrier because it is not closed under multiplication by the
phase \(\exp(i\theta)\).

With those algebraic preliminaries fixed, a carrier field is chosen first,
followed by the equation and operators under which it is preserved, and then
by the bilinears from which physical observables are defined. Bilinears need
not take values in the original carrier; scalar, vector, or paravector
observables may be obtained.

\noindent\textbf{Scalar carrier.}
The Klein--Gordon field \(\psi\) is a complex scalar. Multiplication by
\(\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projectors} would take it out of
that carrier, so the axis projectors are
not scalar-wave spin projectors. The positive quantity \(|\psi|^2\) is not
the time component of the relativistic conserved current. For \(m>0\), the
mixed current is defined in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:kg-mixed-current}. The following
relations are obtained by direct differentiation:
\begin{align*}
2m\,\partial_t\rho(\phi,\psi)
&=i\left((E^2\phi)^*\psi-\phi^*E^2\psi\right),\\
2m\,\gradv\cdot\vct{j}(\phi,\psi)
&=i\left(\phi^*\vct{P}^2\psi-(\vct{P}^2\phi)^*\psi\right).
\end{align*}
Equation \eqref{eq:kg-continuity-proof} is established by addition of the
lines and application of the two Klein--Gordon equations. For a constant-potential mode
\[
\psi=N\exp(-i\omega t+i\vct{k}\cdot\vct{r}),
\]
where \(N\) is a constant complex amplitude, the component check is
\begin{align*}
E\psi&=(\omega-qV)\psi,
&\vct{P}\psi&=(\vct{k}-q\vct{A})\psi,\\
(\omega-qV)^2&=(\vct{k}-q\vct{A})^2+m^2,\\
\rho(\psi,\psi)
&=\frac{\omega-qV}{m}|N|^2,
&\vct{j}(\psi,\psi)
&=\frac{\vct{k}-q\vct{A}}{m}|N|^2.
\end{align*}
Every potential and current sign is thereby verified.

The free field is obtained by setting \(q=0\). The integrated pairing in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:kg-pairing} is positive after restriction to the
positive-frequency sector, although its local density need not be pointwise
positive for a superposition. That solution sector must be preserved by a
scalar measurement operator, which must be Hermitian under this pairing. The split is
preserved for the free field, or for a stationary background with a
separately established positive-energy subspace; a global frequency split need
not be preserved by a general time-dependent electromagnetic background.
Accordingly, no local position-probability rule is claimed for the
one-particle Klein--Gordon field.

\noindent\textbf{Projected two-block carrier.}
For two projected blocks, the Hermitian pairing is
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-pairing}. For the block operator
\[
\mat{\mathcal{O}}=
\begin{pmatrix}
\pv{\mathcal{O}}_{11}&\pv{\mathcal{O}}_{12}\\
\pv{\mathcal{O}}_{21}&\pv{\mathcal{O}}_{22}
\end{pmatrix},
\]
the operator pairing defined in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-operator-pairing}
is expanded explicitly as
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\langle\bPsi(\eqtext{left})\mid
\mat{\mathcal{O}}\bPsi(\eqtext{right})\rangle\\
&=2\iiint\eqtext{scalar}\!\Big(
\herm{\bPsi_1(\eqtext{left})}
\pv{\mathcal{O}}_{11}\bPsi_1(\eqtext{right})\\
&\qquad
+\herm{\bPsi_1(\eqtext{left})}
\pv{\mathcal{O}}_{12}\bPsi_2(\eqtext{right})\\
&\qquad
+\herm{\bPsi_2(\eqtext{left})}
\pv{\mathcal{O}}_{21}\bPsi_1(\eqtext{right})\\
&\qquad
+\herm{\bPsi_2(\eqtext{left})}
\pv{\mathcal{O}}_{22}\bPsi_2(\eqtext{right})
\Big)\,\dd r_1\,\dd r_2\,\dd r_3.
\end{aligned}
\]
Conversion of scalar extraction in the matrix realization to the ordinary
two-entry column normalization is provided by the factor \(2\). Without the
fixed right projector, two independent copies of every displayed Dirac and
Pauli equation are obtained from the two matrix columns. The conserved density
and current in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-current} are supplied by the coupled
first-order equation rather than by the vector part of a generic Hermitian
product. Equation
\eqref{eq:dirac-expectation} is the corresponding complete two-block
measurement rule.

\noindent\textbf{Pure-vector field carrier.}
The electromagnetic field \(\pv{F}\) is a pure-vector complex paravector.
That pure-vector space is not preserved under left multiplication by the
projectors \(\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\) from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projectors}, so this
two-projector split is not the electromagnetic polarization
split. A free massless electromagnetic field has two independent transverse
polarizations; a massive spin-one vector field would have three. The
invariant field square and the observer-dependent energy and flux paravector
are the bilinears already derived in the electromagnetic section. A generic
Hermitian-product bracket is not substituted for those field observables.

Thus spin is never fixed by component count alone. The carrier, transformation law,
field equation, constraints, and observable bilinears must agree. In the
present notation, the positive wave-function pairing is supplied by \(H\),
the determinant used in factorization is supplied by the star--\(H\) composition, and the
pure conjugate pairing remains an algebraic companion without an additional
physical interpretation.

\subsubsection{Spin-0 Wave Mechanics}
The scalar relativistic wave operator is motivated by the classical
mass-shell identity developed in the kinematic section. Replacement of classical energy
and momentum by differential operators is the standard quantization
prescription and is an additional physical input, not a consequence of the
determinant identity alone. Here \(\psi\in\C\) is a complex scalar field,
\(m\in\R\), and the differential conventions from
Section \ref{sec:differential} are used for the free-particle operators. The
mass-shell identity is
\begin{equation*}
\detf{m\pv{U}}=m^2.
\end{equation*}
Its scalar form is
\begin{equation*}
-E^2+\vct{P}^2+m^2=0.
\end{equation*}
For a free particle,
\begin{equation*}
E=i\partial_t,
\qquad
\vct{P}=-i\gradv,
\end{equation*}
and equivalently
\begin{equation*}
E+\vct{P}\cdot\sigv=i\conj{\pv{\partial}}.
\end{equation*}
Acting on a scalar field \(\psi\), the operator chain is
\begin{align*}
(-E^2+\vct{P}^2+m^2)\psi
&=\bigl(-(i\partial_t)^2-\gradv^2+m^2\bigr)\psi\notag\\
&=\bigl(\partial_t^2-\gradv^2+m^2\bigr)\psi\notag\\
&=(\pv{\partial}\conj{\pv{\partial}}+m^2)\psi\notag\\
&=(\Box+m^2)\psi.
\end{align*}
Thus the free spin-0 relativistic wave equation is
\begin{equation*}
(\Box+m^2)\psi=0
\qquad\text{Klein--Gordon equation}.
\end{equation*}
This is the scalar wave equation recorded in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:kg-free}. The dispersion relation \(\omega^2=\vct{k}^2+m^2\) and the later positive/negative-frequency split are consequences of this same operator identity.

\subsubsection{Positive and Negative Frequency Sectors}
The frequency decomposition is one of the central interpretive steps in the
free scalar theory. On a spatial domain where the positive square root is
defined, the frequency function from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:frequency-operator}
is given by
\begin{align*}
\Omega
&=\sqrt{m^2-\gradv^2}\notag\\
&=\sqrt{\detf{\pv{m+\sigv\cdot\gradv}}}.
\end{align*}
Accordingly,
\begin{align*}
\Box+m^2
&=\partial_t^2+\Omega^2\notag\\
&=\partial_t^2+(m^2-\gradv^2)\notag\\
&=(\partial_t-i\Omega)(\partial_t+i\Omega)\notag\\
&=(\partial_t+i\Omega)(\partial_t-i\Omega).
\end{align*}
The sector definitions in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:frequency-split} are then restated as
\begin{align*}
(\partial_t\pm i\Omega)\psi(\pm)&=0,\\
\psi(\pm,t)&=\exp(\mp i\Omega t)\psi(\pm,0),\\
\psi&=\psi(+)+\psi(-).
\end{align*}
Here the parenthesized sign labels frequency and is not the spin-axis label
in the spin-axis action
\(\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})\) defined by
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projectors}.
When \(\Omega^{-1}\) exists, the two parts of a Klein--Gordon solution at any
time can equivalently be extracted as
\begin{equation*}
\psi(\pm)=\frac12\left(\psi\pm i\Omega^{-1}\partial_t\psi\right).
\end{equation*}
For a massless field the spatial zero mode is treated separately. The
Klein--Gordon pairing has opposite sign on a pure negative-frequency mode and
is not a positive norm on the combined solution space. In a quantized
relativistic field the particle--antiparticle interpretation is supplied by
the field-operator construction, not by reinterpreting this one-particle
density alone \cite{greiner2000}.

\subsubsection{Fourier Meaning of the Frequency Operator}
The nonlocal square root \(\Omega\) is most transparent one spatial Fourier
mode at a time. With the standard phase convention, the field and modes are
written schematically as
\begin{align*}
\psi&=\sum_{n} c_{n}f_{n},\\
f_{n}&=\exp\!\bigl(-i\omega_{n}t+i\vct{k}_{n}\cdot\vct{r}\bigr).
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{align*}
\Box\psi
&=\sum_{n}(\vct{k}_{n}^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2})c_{n}f_{n},\\
(\Box+m^{2})\psi
&=\sum_{n}(\vct{k}_{n}^{2}+m^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2})c_{n}f_{n},\\
\Omega\psi
&=\sum_n\sqrt{m^2+\vct{k}_n^2}\,c_nf_n.
\end{align*}
For modes satisfying the Klein--Gordon dispersion relation,
\begin{equation*}
\vct{k}_{n}^{2}+m^{2}=\omega_{n}^{2},
\qquad
\omega_{n}=\pm\sqrt{\vct{k}_{n}^{2}+m^{2}},
\end{equation*}
and each spatial mode is multiplied by its positive energy magnitude by
\(\Omega\).
The only square-root operator needed in the frequency split is thereby
defined, and an unnecessary branch is not assigned to \(\sqrt{\Box}\).

\subsubsection{Klein--Gordon with Potential Fields}
The charged-particle Lagrangian is taken as the starting point so that the
minimally coupled scalar theory is kept in one self-contained place:
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal L=-\frac{m}{\gamma}-q(V-\vct{v}\cdot\vct{A}).
\end{equation*}
Here \(E\) denotes the energy operator, not the electric field \(\vct{E}\).
The canonical energy and momentum are identified by the classical Lagrangian.
After the standard differential replacement, the covariant operators are
\begin{equation*}
E=i\partial_t-qV,
\qquad
\vct{P}=-i\gradv-q\vct{A}.
\end{equation*}
This differential replacement, together with minimal coupling, is the
standard quantization input.

Acting on a smooth test field \(\psi\), the Leibniz rules needed below are
\begin{align*}
\partial_t(V\psi)&=(\partial_tV)\psi+V\partial_t\psi,\\
\partial_m(A_n\psi)&=(\partial_mA_n)\psi+A_n\partial_m\psi.
\end{align*}
The following expansions are obtained:
\begin{align*}
E^2\psi
&=E(i\partial_t\psi-qV\psi)\notag\\
&=\left(-\partial_t^2-iq\,\partial_tV-2iqV\partial_t+q^2V^2\right)\psi,\\
\vct{P}^2\psi
&=\vct{P}\cdot(-i\gradv\psi-q\vct{A}\psi)\notag\\
&=\left(-\gradv^2+iq\,\gradv\cdot\vct{A}+2iq\,\vct{A}\cdot\gradv+q^2\vct{A}^2\right)\psi.
\end{align*}

The required identities are
\begin{align*}
\Box&=\partial_t^2-\gradv^2,\\
\pv{\Phi}&=V+\vct{A}\cdot\sigv,\\
S&=\partial_tV+\gradv\cdot\vct{A},\\
\detf{\pv{\partial}}&=\Box,\\
\detf{\pv{\Phi}}&=V^2-\vct{A}^2.
\end{align*}
Here \(\detf{\pv{\partial}}=\pv{\partial}\conj{\pv{\partial}}\) is valid because the
coordinate derivatives commute. The coupled energy and momentum operators
below do not commute and therefore remain explicit ordered products rather
than determinants.
The minimally coupled Klein--Gordon equation is obtained in explicit form by substitution of the operator expansions:
\begin{align*}
0&=(-E^2+\vct{P}^2+m^2)\psi\notag\\
&=\Bigl(\Box+iq(\partial_tV+\gradv\cdot\vct{A})+2iq(V\partial_t+\vct{A}\cdot\gradv)\notag\\
&\qquad\qquad-q^2(V^2-\vct{A}^2)+m^2\Bigr)\psi\notag\\
&=\Bigl(\Box+iqS-q^2\detf{\pv{\Phi}}+m^2+2iq(V\partial_t+\vct{A}\cdot\gradv)\Bigr)\psi.
\end{align*}

For the gauge-shifted potential and components defined in
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:gauge}, \eqref{eq:gauge-scalar-potential}, and
\eqref{eq:gauge-vector-potential},
\[
\begin{aligned}
V(\lambda)&=V+\partial_t\lambda,\\
\vct{A}(\lambda)&=\vct{A}-\gradv\lambda,\\
\psi(\lambda)&=\exp(-iq\lambda)\psi.
\end{aligned}
\]
The ordinary product rule is obeyed by the central scalar phase. Consequently,
\begin{align*}
(i\partial_t-qV(\lambda))\psi(\lambda)
&=\exp(-iq\lambda)(i\partial_t-qV)\psi,\\
(-i\gradv-q\vct{A}(\lambda))\psi(\lambda)
&=\exp(-iq\lambda)(-i\gradv-q\vct{A})\psi.
\end{align*}
Equation \eqref{eq:kg-gauge} and the gauge covariance of the coupled
Klein--Gordon equation are established by a second application of either
relation. Substitution in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:kg-mixed-current} also cancels the phase from both current
components.

The electromagnetic fields and commutators are incorporated through
\begin{align*}
\vct{E}&=-\partial_t\vct{A}-\gradv V,\\
\vct{B}&=\gradv\times\vct{A},\\
\pv{F}&=(\vct{E}+i\vct{B})\cdot\sigv,\qquad
\conj{\pv{F}}=(-\vct{E}+i\vct{B})\cdot\sigv,
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
\com{E}{P_m}\psi
&=(i\partial_t-qV)(-i\partial_m\psi-qA_m\psi)\notag\\
&\quad-(-i\partial_m-qA_m)(i\partial_t\psi-qV\psi)\notag\\
&=-iq\bigl((\partial_tA_m)\psi+A_m\partial_t\psi\bigr)\notag\\
&\quad-iq\bigl((\partial_mV)\psi+V\partial_m\psi\bigr)\notag\\
&\quad+iqV\partial_m\psi+iqA_m\partial_t\psi\notag\\
&=-iq(\partial_tA_m+\partial_mV)\psi\notag\\
&=iqE_m\psi,\\
\com{P_m}{P_n}\psi
&=(-i\partial_m-qA_m)\bigl(-i\partial_n\psi-qA_n\psi\bigr)\notag\\
&\quad-(-i\partial_n-qA_n)\bigl(-i\partial_m\psi-qA_m\psi\bigr)\notag\\
&=iq(\partial_mA_n-\partial_nA_m)\psi.
\end{align*}
Hence
\begin{equation*}
\com{E}{\vct{P}}=iq\vct{E},
\qquad
\vct{P}\times\vct{P}=iq\vct{B}.
\end{equation*}
The following ordered products are obtained by substitution of those commutators:
\begin{align*}
(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})(E-\sigv\cdot\vct{P})
&=E^2-\vct{P}^2\notag\\
&\qquad+\sigv\cdot\bigl(-\com{E}{\vct{P}}-i\,\vct{P}\times\vct{P}\bigr)\notag\\
&=E^2-\vct{P}^2-iq\pv{F},\\
(E-\sigv\cdot\vct{P})(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})
&=E^2-\vct{P}^2\notag\\
&\qquad+\sigv\cdot\bigl(\com{E}{\vct{P}}-i\,\vct{P}\times\vct{P}\bigr)\notag\\
&=E^2-\vct{P}^2-iq\conj{\pv{F}}.
\end{align*}
Since
\begin{equation*}
E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P}=i\conj{\pv{\partial}}-q\pv{\Phi},
\qquad
E-\sigv\cdot\vct{P}=i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}}
=(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})^{*\mathsf H},
\end{equation*}
the two ordered star--\(H\) products are
\begin{equation*}
(E\pm\sigv\cdot\vct{P})
(E\pm\sigv\cdot\vct{P})^{*\mathsf H}
=E^2-\vct{P}^2+\sigv\cdot\bigl(\mp\com{E}{\vct{P}}-i\,\vct{P}\times\vct{P}\bigr).
\end{equation*}
Unlike the determinant function defined for commuting complex
coefficients, these operator products retain a vector part.  Operator ordering
therefore matters, and the two first-order products remain distinct.
Consequently,
\begin{align*}
-(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})^{*\mathsf H}
&=(\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi})(\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}}),\\
-(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})^{*\mathsf H}(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})
&=(\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}})(\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi}).
\end{align*}
The same factorizations can be rewritten directly in derivative form:
\begin{align*}
(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})(E-\sigv\cdot\vct{P})\psi
&=-(\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi})(\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}})\psi\notag\\
&=-\Box\psi-iq\,\conj{\pv{\partial}}(\conj{\pv{\Phi}}\psi)\notag\\
&\qquad-iq\,\pv{\Phi}\,\pv{\partial}\psi\notag\\
&\qquad+q^2\detf{\pv{\Phi}}\psi\notag\\
&=-\Box\psi-iqS\psi+q^2\detf{\pv{\Phi}}\psi\notag\\
&\qquad-2iq(V\partial_t+\vct{A}\cdot\gradv)\psi\notag\\
&\qquad-iq\pv{F}\psi,\\
(E-\sigv\cdot\vct{P})(E+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})\psi
&=-(\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}})(\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi})\psi\notag\\
&=-\Box\psi-iq\,\pv{\partial}(\pv{\Phi}\psi)\notag\\
&\qquad-iq\,\conj{\pv{\Phi}}\,\conj{\pv{\partial}}\psi\notag\\
&\qquad+q^2\detf{\pv{\Phi}}\psi\notag\\
&=-\Box\psi-iqS\psi+q^2\detf{\pv{\Phi}}\psi\notag\\
&\qquad-2iq(V\partial_t+\vct{A}\cdot\gradv)\psi\notag\\
&\qquad-iq\conj{\pv{F}}\psi.
\end{align*}
Equivalently,
\begin{align*}
\conj{\pv{\partial}}(\conj{\pv{\Phi}}\psi)+\pv{\Phi}\,\pv{\partial}\psi
&=S\psi+2(V\partial_t+\vct{A}\cdot\gradv)\psi+\pv{F}\psi,\\
\pv{\partial}(\pv{\Phi}\psi)+\conj{\pv{\Phi}}\,\conj{\pv{\partial}}\psi
&=S\psi+2(V\partial_t+\vct{A}\cdot\gradv)\psi+\conj{\pv{F}}\psi,
\end{align*}
and their difference isolates the electric contribution:
\begin{align*}
\bigl(\conj{\pv{\partial}}(\conj{\pv{\Phi}}\psi)+\pv{\Phi}\,\pv{\partial}\psi\bigr)
&-\bigl(\pv{\partial}(\pv{\Phi}\psi)+\conj{\pv{\Phi}}\,\conj{\pv{\partial}}\psi\bigr)\notag\\
&=\bigl(\conj{\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}}-\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}\bigr)\psi\notag\\
&=(\pv{F}-\conj{\pv{F}})\psi\notag\\
&=2(\vct{E}\cdot\sigv)\psi.
\end{align*}

From the definitions in
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:energy-operator} and \eqref{eq:momentum-operator},
\eqref{eq:partial-operator}, \eqref{eq:partial-conjugate}, and
\eqref{eq:F-Phi}, the scalar Klein--Gordon operator can therefore be recovered
from either ordered first-order product:
\begin{align*}
0&=(-E^2+\vct{P}^2+m^2)\psi\notag\\
&=\Bigl(\Box+iqS-q^2\detf{\pv{\Phi}}+m^2+2iq(V\partial_t+\vct{A}\cdot\gradv)\Bigr)\psi\notag\\
&=(\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi})(\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}})\psi-iq\pv{F}\psi+m^2\psi\notag\\
&=(\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}})(\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi})\psi-iq\conj{\pv{F}}\psi+m^2\psi.
\end{align*}
The following identities are satisfied by the ordered first-order products:
\begin{align*}
(E\pm\sigv\cdot\vct{P})(E\mp\sigv\cdot\vct{P})
&=E^2-(\sigv\cdot\vct{P})^2\mp\com{E}{\sigv\cdot\vct{P}}\notag\\
&=E^2-\vct{P}^2-iq(\pm\vct{E}+i\vct{B})\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
The following equation is therefore recovered by taking the scalar part:
\begin{equation*}
0=\eqtext{scalar}\!\bigl((\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}})(\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi})\bigr)\psi+m^2\psi.
\end{equation*}
The Klein--Gordon operator itself is reproduced only by the scalar part; the full
ordered products retain the non-scalar field terms \(-iq\pv{F}\) and
\(-iq\conj{\pv{F}}\) that distinguish the two factorizations. Explicitly,
\[
-iq\pv{F}=(-iq\vct{E}+q\vct{B})\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
-iq\conj{\pv{F}}=(iq\vct{E}+q\vct{B})\cdot\sigv.
\]
These residuals are not new components of the scalar equation. They are the
standard spin--field terms retained by the two ordered Dirac squares below.

\subsubsection{Spin-\texorpdfstring{$\frac12$}{1/2} Wave Mechanics}
Before the electromagnetic field is turned on, a self-contained first-order
version of the relativistic mass shell is obtained from the same auxiliary
\(2\times2\) map in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:W-map}. The two-block field is
considered in the form
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi=
\begin{Bmatrix}
\bPsi_1\\[2pt]
\bPsi_2
\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
For one Dirac field, the following restriction is satisfied by both blocks:
\(\bPsi_n=\bPsi_n\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})\). If they are
left unrestricted, every formula is applied independently to the two matrix
columns, and two identical Dirac fields are therefore described.
From Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:W-map} and \eqref{eq:W-square}, complex linearity of the
auxiliary block map and block diagonality of its square are obtained. Therefore
\begin{align*}
\Wmat(i\pv{\partial})
&=i
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \pv{\partial} \\
\conj{\pv{\partial}} & 0
\end{bmatrix}
=i\Wmat(\pv{\partial}),\\
\Wmat(i\pv{\partial})^2
&=-\eqtext{diag}(\pv{\partial}\conj{\pv{\partial}},
                  \conj{\pv{\partial}}\pv{\partial})\notag\\
&=-\Box\I.
\end{align*}

The free operator in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-free} is
\(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial})-m\I\). Its factorization partner is obtained by
reversing only the sign of the mass. Their product in either order is
\begin{align*}
(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial})+m\I)
(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial})-m\I)
&=\Wmat(i\pv{\partial})^2-m^2\I\notag\\
&=-(\Box+m^2)\I,\notag\\
(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial})-m\I)
(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial})+m\I)
&=-(\Box+m^2)\I.
\end{align*}
Thus the free Dirac equation
\begin{equation*}
(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial})-m\I)\bPsi=0
\end{equation*}
requires the Klein--Gordon operator to be satisfied by each block. No
determinant of algebra-valued blocks is needed: the relevant minimal
polynomial is supplied directly by Eq.\ \eqref{eq:W-square}.

The equivalence with the standard two-coupled-column form is explicit in the
Pauli matrix realization. For a plane wave
\begin{equation*}
\exp(-iEt+i\vct{p}\cdot\vct{r})
\end{equation*}
the selected columns are represented by
\(\{a;b\}\) for \(\bPsi_1\) and
\(\{c;d\}\) for \(\bPsi_2\). The two equations are
\begin{equation*}
(E-\vct{p}\cdot\sigv)\{c;d\}=m\{a;b\},
\qquad
(E+\vct{p}\cdot\sigv)\{a;b\}=m\{c;d\}.
\end{equation*}
The relation \(E^2=\vct{p}^2+m^2\) is obtained from their product. These are the standard two
first-order equations for the four complex entries of one Dirac field.
Two independent copies of this system are obtained by retaining both matrix
columns. With a potential present, the operator equations in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-four-components} are represented by the same four
entries; hence component-by-component agreement with the compact paravector
equation is obtained before squaring.

The two coupled equations are obtained by writing out the free equation:
\begin{align*}
i\pv{\partial}\bPsi_2-m\bPsi_1&=0,\\
i\conj{\pv{\partial}}\bPsi_1-m\bPsi_2&=0.
\end{align*}
Their transformation law is determined by covariance rather than component
count. For a constant determinant-one transform \(\pv{T}\), with
\(\pv{X}'\) given by Eq.\ \eqref{eq:general-event-congruence}, the transformed
blocks from Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-transform} and
\eqref{eq:dirac-transform-lower} obey
\begin{align*}
\bPsi_1'(\pv{X}')&=\pv{T}^{\mathsf H}\bPsi_1(\pv{X}),\\
\bPsi_2'(\pv{X}')&=\pv{T}^{-1}\bPsi_2(\pv{X}).
\end{align*}
The corresponding derivative and potential laws are
\begin{equation}
\pv{\partial}'=\pv{T}^{\mathsf H}\pv{\partial}\pv{T}.
\label{eq:dirac-transform-derivative}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\conj{\pv{\partial}'}
=\pv{T}^{-1}\conj{\pv{\partial}}\conj{\pv{T}}.
\label{eq:dirac-transform-conjugate-derivative}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\pv{\Phi}'=\pv{T}^{-1}\pv{\Phi}\pv{T}^*.
\label{eq:dirac-transform-potential}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\conj{\pv{\Phi}'}
=\pv{T}^{\mathsf H}\conj{\pv{\Phi}}\pv{T}.
\label{eq:dirac-transform-conjugate-potential}
\end{equation}
For a spatial rotor, the same left factor \(\pv{R}^{\mathsf H}\) is assigned
to both blocks. For a boost, \(\pv{L}\) is assigned to the first block and
\(\conj{\pv{L}}\) to the second. Since
\(\detf{\pv{T}}=1\), the identities
\(\pv{T}\pv{T}^{-1}=1\) and
\(\conj{\pv{T}}\pv{T}^{\mathsf H}=1\) imply the following explicit substitutions:
\begin{align*}
(i\pv{\partial}'-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}'})\bPsi_2'
&=\pv{T}^{\mathsf H}
(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}})\bPsi_2
=m\bPsi_1',\\
(i\conj{\pv{\partial}'}-q\pv{\Phi}')\bPsi_1'
&=\pv{T}^{-1}
(i\conj{\pv{\partial}}-q\pv{\Phi})\bPsi_1
=m\bPsi_2'.
\end{align*}
Thus both coupled equations are covariant.
Because all transform factors are applied from the left, the fixed right projector
is preserved.

The real current paravector defined in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-current-paravector} is expanded as
\begin{equation*}
\pv{J}(\bPsi)
=2\left(
\bPsi_2\herm{\bPsi_2}
+\conj{\bigl(\bPsi_1\herm{\bPsi_1}\bigr)}
\right)
=\rho(\bPsi)+\vct{j}(\bPsi)\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
The following transformation is obtained from
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-transform} and \eqref{eq:dirac-transform-lower}
in each product:
\[
\pv{J}(\bPsi')(\pv{X}')
=\pv{T}^{-1}\pv{J}(\bPsi)(\pv{X})(\pv{T}^{-1})^{\mathsf H},
\]
so the current is transformed under the same congruence law as a real event paravector.
For the selected columns used in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-four-components}, the
component form is
\begin{align*}
\rho(\bPsi)&=|a|^2+|b|^2+|c|^2+|d|^2,\\
\vct{j}(\bPsi)&=
\begin{Bmatrix}
2\,\eqtext{real}(c^*d-a^*b)\\
2\,\eqtext{img}(c^*d-a^*b)\\
|c|^2-|d|^2-|a|^2+|b|^2
\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{align*}
These are the conventional Dirac density and current components. The two
uncancelled block balances are Eq.\
\eqref{eq:dirac-continuity-components}; by their addition,
\(\partial_t\rho(\bPsi)+\gradv\cdot\vct{j}(\bPsi)=0\) is established. In particular,
\[
\vct{u}\cdot\vct{j}(\bPsi)
=2\,\eqtext{scalar}\!\left(
\herm{\bPsi_2}(\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)\bPsi_2
-\herm{\bPsi_1}(\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)\bPsi_1\right),
\]
so this is the local matrix-element density for the ordinary two-block
velocity operator. Its spatial integral divided by
\(\langle\bPsi\mid\bPsi\rangle\) is the corresponding velocity expectation.
The constant-time density is a frame component of this covariant current,
not a Lorentz scalar.

For \(m>0\), the following result is obtained by solving the first equation for the upper component and substituting into the second:
\begin{align*}
\bPsi_1&=\frac{i\pv{\partial}\bPsi_2}{m},\\
0&=i\conj{\pv{\partial}}\bPsi_1-m\bPsi_2\notag\\
&=-\frac{\conj{\pv{\partial}}\pv{\partial}\bPsi_2}{m}-m\bPsi_2\notag\\
&=-\frac{(\Box+m^2)\bPsi_2}{m}.
\end{align*}
Hence the lower component is a Klein--Gordon solution, and the upper component is obtained from it by one first-order derivative.

Conversely, a scalar Klein--Gordon solution \(\psi\) and constant
polarization elements
\(\pv{a},\pv{b}\in(\C+\C^3)\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})\) are taken. The
mass-conjugate operator is applied to the projected seed:
\begin{equation}
\bPsi
:=\frac1m\bigl(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial})+m\I\bigr)
\begin{Bmatrix}
\psi\pv{a}\\[2pt]
\psi\pv{b}
\end{Bmatrix}.
\label{eq:dirac-klein-gordon-lift}
\end{equation}
Because the constants occur on the right, only \(\psi\) is acted on by the
derivatives, and the two blocks are
\begin{align*}
\bPsi_1&=\psi\pv{a}
+\frac{i}{m}(\pv{\partial}\psi)\pv{b},\\
\bPsi_2&=\frac{i}{m}(\conj{\pv{\partial}}\psi)\pv{a}
+\psi\pv{b}.
\end{align*}
The following result is obtained from the factorization:
\begin{equation*}
\bigl(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial})-m\I\bigr)\bPsi
=-\frac1m(\Box+m^2)\psi
\begin{Bmatrix}\pv{a}\\ \pv{b}\end{Bmatrix}
=0.
\end{equation*}
The spin polarization is carried by the constants \(\pv{a}\) and \(\pv{b}\);
it is not carried by a bare scalar solution alone. For example, the following
field is obtained with \(\pv{a}=\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})\) and
\(\pv{b}=0\):
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi=
\begin{Bmatrix}
\psi\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})\\[2pt]
\dfrac{i}{m}(\conj{\pv{\partial}}\psi)
 \pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})
\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
This is the first-order lift from the Klein--Gordon mass shell into the chosen
projected Dirac carrier. The two-seed presentation is symmetric but
redundant: on the Klein--Gordon mass shell,
\(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial})+m\I\) has a kernel, so
the same Dirac field can be produced by different seed pairs. For any fixed
nonzero massive momentum mode, the two spin polarizations are already spanned
by one projected seed block. Four new polarization degrees of freedom are
therefore not introduced by the symbols \(\pv{a}\) and \(\pv{b}\).

\subsubsection{Dirac with Potential Field}
The two-block field is again considered:
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi=
\begin{Bmatrix}
\bPsi_1\\[2pt]
\bPsi_2
\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
The one-field interpretation continues to impose
\[
\bPsi_n=\bPsi_n\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column}).
\]
All potential and derivative factors are applied from the left, and the
fixed-column carrier is therefore preserved. The auxiliary block function
already defined in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:W-map} is used.
The Dirac operator with potential is
\begin{equation}
\Dmat:=\Wmat(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}})-m\I.
\label{eq:dirac-potential-operator}
\end{equation}
By linearity of the block function, this definition is equivalently
\begin{equation*}
\Dmat=i\Wmat(\pv{\partial})
-q\Wmat(\conj{\pv{\Phi}})-m\I.
\end{equation*}
Its field equation is Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled}. Because
\begin{align*}
\Wmat(\pv{\partial})^2&=\Box\I,\\
\Wmat(\pv{\partial})\Wmat(\conj{\pv{\Phi}})
&=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \pv{\partial}\\
\conj{\pv{\partial}} & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \conj{\pv{\Phi}}\\
\pv{\Phi} & 0
\end{bmatrix}
=\eqtext{diag}(\pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi},
 \conj{\pv{\partial}}\conj{\pv{\Phi}}),\\
\Wmat(\conj{\pv{\Phi}})\Wmat(\pv{\partial})
&=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \conj{\pv{\Phi}}\\
\pv{\Phi} & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \pv{\partial}\\
\conj{\pv{\partial}} & 0
\end{bmatrix}
=\eqtext{diag}(\conj{\pv{\Phi}}\conj{\pv{\partial}},
 \pv{\Phi}\pv{\partial}),\\
\Wmat(\conj{\pv{\Phi}})^2
&=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \conj{\pv{\Phi}}\\
\pv{\Phi} & 0
\end{bmatrix}^{2}
=\eqtext{diag}(\conj{\pv{\Phi}}\pv{\Phi},
 \pv{\Phi}\conj{\pv{\Phi}})\notag\\
&=\detf{\pv{\Phi}}\I,
\end{align*}
the following result is obtained:
\begin{align*}
\Wmat(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}})^2
&=-\Wmat(\pv{\partial})^2\notag\\
&\qquad-iq\bigl(\Wmat(\pv{\partial})\Wmat(\conj{\pv{\Phi}})
 +\Wmat(\conj{\pv{\Phi}})\Wmat(\pv{\partial})\bigr)\notag\\
&\qquad+q^2\Wmat(\conj{\pv{\Phi}})^2\notag\\
&=-\Box\I-iq\,\eqtext{diag}(
 \pv{\partial}\pv{\Phi}+\conj{\pv{\Phi}}\conj{\pv{\partial}},
 \conj{\pv{\partial}}\conj{\pv{\Phi}}+\pv{\Phi}\pv{\partial})\notag\\
&\qquad+q^2\detf{\pv{\Phi}}\I\notag\\
&=-\eqtext{diag}\!\Big(
(\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}})
(\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi}),\notag\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad
(\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi})
(\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}})
\Big).
\end{align*}
The Dirac equation
\begin{equation*}
\Dmat\bPsi=0
\end{equation*}
is therefore reduced to the two coupled equations
\begin{align*}
-m\bPsi_1+(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}})\bPsi_2&=0,\\
(i\conj{\pv{\partial}}-q\pv{\Phi})\bPsi_1-m\bPsi_2&=0.
\end{align*}
Equivalently, their time evolution is
\begin{align*}
i\partial_t\bPsi_1
&=(qV-\sigv\cdot\vct{P})\bPsi_1+m\bPsi_2,\\
i\partial_t\bPsi_2
&=(qV+\sigv\cdot\vct{P})\bPsi_2+m\bPsi_1.
\end{align*}
The \(H\) operation is applied to these equations, multiplication is
performed on the opposite side, and scalar parts are taken. In
selected-column form the two separate balance equations are written
out in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-continuity-components}. The real potential terms
cancel within each block, while the mass terms are equal and opposite between
the blocks. The following relation is obtained by addition of those two equations:
\begin{equation*}
\partial_t\eqtext{scalar}\!\left(
\herm{\bPsi_1}\bPsi_1+\herm{\bPsi_2}\bPsi_2\right)
+\gradv\cdot\eqtext{vector}\!\left(
\bPsi_2\herm{\bPsi_2}-\bPsi_1\herm{\bPsi_1}\right)=0.
\end{equation*}
The stated density and current are thereby verified, and the distinction
between the vector part of a single \(\herm{\pv{\psi}}\pv{\psi}\) and the
Dirac current is established. The following result is obtained by solving the
first equation for the upper component and substituting into the second:
\begin{align*}
\bPsi_1&=\frac{(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}})\bPsi_2}{m},\\
0&=(i\conj{\pv{\partial}}-q\pv{\Phi})\bPsi_1-m\bPsi_2\notag\\
&=-\frac{\bigl((\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi})
(\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}})+m^2\bigr)\bPsi_2}{m}.
\end{align*}
Thus a Klein--Gordon-type operator with the appropriate ordered field
coupling is satisfied by each block.

The mass-conjugate operator defined in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-mass-conjugate-operator} is
\begin{equation*}
\Dmat'=\Wmat(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}})+m\I.
\end{equation*}
The following result is obtained by multiplication by \(\Dmat'\):
\begin{align*}
0=\Dmat'\Dmat\bPsi
&=\bigl(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}})^2-m^2\I\bigr)\bPsi\notag\\
&=-\eqtext{diag}\!\Big(
(\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}})
(\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi})+m^2,\notag\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad
(\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi})
(\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}})+m^2
\Big)\bPsi.
\end{align*}
Equivalently,
\begin{align*}
0&=\bigl((\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}})
(\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi})+m^2\bigr)\bPsi_1\notag\\
&=\bigl(-E^2+\vct{P}^2+m^2+iq\conj{\pv{F}}\bigr)\bPsi_1,\\
0&=\bigl((\conj{\pv{\partial}}+iq\pv{\Phi})
(\pv{\partial}+iq\conj{\pv{\Phi}})+m^2\bigr)\bPsi_2\notag\\
&=\bigl(-E^2+\vct{P}^2+m^2+iq\pv{F}\bigr)\bPsi_2.
\end{align*}
Since
\[
iq\conj{\pv{F}}=(-iq\vct{E}-q\vct{B})\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
iq\pv{F}=(iq\vct{E}-q\vct{B})\cdot\sigv,
\]
the two equations are exactly the component forms in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-square-components}. Once the square is taken, the
coupled Dirac system is separated into two Klein--Gordon-type equations with the
same magnetic term and opposite electric terms, as required by the two
first-order blocks.

\subsubsection{Gauge Transform of the Dirac System}
For a real scalar gauge field \(\lambda\in\R\), the potential defined in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:gauge} is
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\Phi}(\lambda)=\pv{\Phi}+\conj{\pv{\partial}}\lambda.
\end{equation*}
Because \(\lambda\) is real,
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\partial}\lambda=\conj{\pv{\Phi}(\lambda)-\pv{\Phi}}.
\end{equation*}
The field transformation defined in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-gauge} is
equivalently written as
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi=\exp(iq\lambda)\bPsi(\lambda).
\end{equation*}
The phase factor is a central scalar, so every commutator correction in
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:paravector-leibniz} is zero. The ordinary two-term product rule
is therefore obeyed by the derivative of the phase factor:
\begin{align*}
\pv{\partial}\bigl(\exp(iq\lambda)\bPsi(\lambda)\bigr)
&=\exp(iq\lambda)\bigl(iq\,\pv{\partial}\lambda+\pv{\partial}\bigr)\bPsi(\lambda).
\end{align*}
Therefore,
\begin{align*}
0&=(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}})-m\I)\bPsi\notag\\
&=(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}})-m\I)\exp(iq\lambda)\bPsi(\lambda)\notag\\
&=\exp(iq\lambda)(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial}-q\,\pv{\partial}\lambda-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}})-m\I)\bPsi(\lambda)\notag\\
&=\exp(iq\lambda)(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}(\lambda)-\pv{\Phi}}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}})-m\I)\bPsi(\lambda)\notag\\
&=\exp(iq\lambda)(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}(\lambda)})-m\I)\bPsi(\lambda).
\end{align*}
Hence
\begin{equation*}
(\Wmat(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}(\lambda)})-m\I)\bPsi(\lambda)=0.
\end{equation*}
This is the gauge-covariance statement: the phase change of the Dirac field is exactly compensated by the shift of the potential, so the same form of the coupled Dirac equation is retained in the transformed gauge.

\subsubsection{Low-Energy Spin-0 Limit}
For the minimally coupled scalar theory, the covariant operators are
\begin{equation*}
E=i\partial_t-qV,
\qquad
\vct{P}=-i\gradv-q\vct{A}.
\end{equation*}
The fast rest-mass oscillation is removed by writing
\begin{equation*}
\psi=\exp(-imt)\psi(\eqtext{slow}).
\end{equation*}
Then
\begin{align*}
E^2\psi
&=\exp(-imt)(E+m)^2\psi(\eqtext{slow}),\\
\vct{P}^2\psi
&=\exp(-imt)\vct{P}^2\psi(\eqtext{slow}).
\end{align*}
The following result is obtained by substitution into the minimally coupled Klein--Gordon equation:
\begin{align*}
0&=(-E^2+\vct{P}^2+m^2)\psi\notag\\
&=\exp(-imt)\bigl(-(E+m)^2+\vct{P}^2+m^2\bigr)
\psi(\eqtext{slow}),
\end{align*}
hence
\begin{equation*}
0=(-E^2-2mE+\vct{P}^2)\psi(\eqtext{slow}).
\end{equation*}
For the free theory, or more generally when the operators in the iteration
commute, the following two branches are admitted by the quadratic equation:
\[
E=-m\pm\sqrt{m^2+\vct{P}^2}.
\]
The root near zero is selected by the slow positive-energy assumption:
\begin{align*}
E\psi(\eqtext{slow})
&=\left(-m+\sqrt{m^2+\vct{P}^2}\right)\psi(\eqtext{slow})\notag\\
&=\left(\frac{\vct{P}^2}{2m}-\frac{\vct{P}^4}{8m^3}
+O(\vct{P}^6/m^5)\right)
\psi(\eqtext{slow}).
\end{align*}
The other root begins at \(-2m\) after the rest phase is removed and is
incompatible with the assumed slowly varying positive-energy branch.
For noncommuting or time-dependent potentials, commutators are also contained
in the higher-order terms; the leading low-energy relation remains
\begin{equation*}
E\psi(\eqtext{slow})
\approx \frac{\vct{P}^2}{2m}\psi(\eqtext{slow}).
\end{equation*}
The Schr\"odinger equation is obtained by solving for
\(i\partial_t\psi(\eqtext{slow})\):
\begin{equation*}
i\partial_t\psi(\eqtext{slow})
\approx\left(\frac{(-i\gradv-q\vct{A})^2}{2m}+qV\right)
\psi(\eqtext{slow}).
\end{equation*}
If \(\vct{A}=0\), this reduces to
\begin{equation*}
i\partial_t\psi(\eqtext{slow})
\approx\left(-\frac{\gradv^2}{2m}+qV\right)\psi(\eqtext{slow}).
\end{equation*}
The scalar low-energy limit is therefore already encoded in the relativistic Klein--Gordon theory.

\subsubsection{Low-Energy Spin-\texorpdfstring{$\frac12$}{1/2} and Pauli Limit}
The fixed-column two-block field from
Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-coupled} is used. Its two block equations are
\begin{align*}
(i\pv{\partial}-q\conj{\pv{\Phi}})\bPsi_2-m\bPsi_1&=0,\\
(i\conj{\pv{\partial}}-q\pv{\Phi})\bPsi_1-m\bPsi_2&=0.
\end{align*}
With the covariant operators from
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:energy-operator} and \eqref{eq:momentum-operator},
\begin{equation*}
E=i\partial_t-qV,
\qquad
\vct{P}=-i\gradv-q\vct{A},
\end{equation*}
the system is
\begin{align*}
(E-\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)\bPsi_2-m\bPsi_1&=0,\\
(E+\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)\bPsi_1-m\bPsi_2&=0.
\end{align*}
The slow fields defined in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:slow-field-definition} are related by
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi_n=\exp(-imt)\bPsi_n(\eqtext{slow}).
\end{equation*}
Since
\begin{equation*}
E\bPsi_n=\exp(-imt)(E+m)\bPsi_n(\eqtext{slow}),
\end{equation*}
the system is reduced to
\begin{align*}
(E+m-\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)\bPsi_2(\eqtext{slow})
-m\bPsi_1(\eqtext{slow})&=0,\\
(E+m+\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)\bPsi_1(\eqtext{slow})
-m\bPsi_2(\eqtext{slow})&=0.
\end{align*}
Neither original block is small at rest. With the energy-basis combinations
from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:large-small},
\begin{align*}
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})
&=\frac{\bPsi_1(\eqtext{slow})+\bPsi_2(\eqtext{slow})}{\sqrt{2}},\\
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{small})
&=\frac{\bPsi_2(\eqtext{slow})-\bPsi_1(\eqtext{slow})}{\sqrt{2}}.
\end{align*}
The exact system is obtained by addition and subtraction of the reduced equations:
\begin{align*}
E\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})
&=(\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{small}),\\
(E+2m)\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{small})
&=(\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large}).
\end{align*}
Thus \(\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})\) is the large positive-energy field, and
\(\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{small})\) is suppressed by one power of \(1/m\).
Formally,
\begin{align*}
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{small})
&=(E+2m)^{-1}(\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large}),\\
E\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})
&=(\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)(E+2m)^{-1}
  (\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})\notag\\
&=\frac{(\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)^2}{2m}
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})+O(m^{-2}),
\end{align*}
where energies small compared with \(2m\) and external fields smooth on the
Compton scale are assumed.

The magnetic term is obtained directly from the covariant-momentum commutator:
\begin{align*}
\com{P_m}{P_n}&=iq(\partial_mA_n-\partial_nA_m),\\
\vct{P}\times\vct{P}&=iq\,\vct{B},\\
(\vct{P}\cdot\sigv)^2
&=\vct{P}^2+i(\vct{P}\times\vct{P})\cdot\sigv\notag\\
&=\vct{P}^2-q\,\vct{B}\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
Consequently,
\begin{equation*}
E\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})
=\frac{\vct{P}^2-q\,\vct{B}\cdot\sigv}{2m}
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})
+O(m^{-2}),
\end{equation*}
and the Pauli equation is
\begin{align*}
i\partial_t\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})
&=\left(\frac{(-i\gradv-q\vct{A})^2}{2m}+qV\right)
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})\notag\\
&\quad-\frac{q}{2m}(\vct{B}\cdot\sigv)
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})+O(m^{-2}).
\end{align*}
The spin--orbit, Darwin, and time-dependent corrections at the next order are
generated by electric-field commutators through the noncommutation of
\(E\) and \(\vct{P}\). They must be retained consistently rather than
discarded from the second-order equation for one original block. The leading
spin coupling is therefore a controlled consequence of the Dirac system
\cite{pauli1927,sakurai2017}.

The Pauli current in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:pauli-current} is obtained from the same
controlled reduction. The selected columns of
\(\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})\) and \(\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{small})\) are denoted
by \(\vct{\phi}(\eqtext{large})\) and
\(\vct{\phi}(\eqtext{small})\), respectively. Then
\begin{align*}
\vct{j}(\bPsi)
&=\mherm{\vct{\phi}(\eqtext{large})}\sigv
\vct{\phi}(\eqtext{small})\notag\\
&\quad+\mherm{\vct{\phi}(\eqtext{small})}\sigv
\vct{\phi}(\eqtext{large}),\\
\vct{\phi}(\eqtext{small})
&=\frac{\sigv\cdot\vct{P}}{2m}
\vct{\phi}(\eqtext{large})+O(m^{-2}).
\end{align*}
The product identity
\(\pv{\sigma}_m\pv{\sigma}_n
=\delta_{mn}+i\epsilon_{mnk}\pv{\sigma}_k\) is used in the written order,
and the result is separated into the following vector expression:
\begin{align*}
\vct{j}(\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large}))
&=\frac{1}{2m}\Bigl(
\mherm{\vct{\phi}(\eqtext{large})}
  (\vct{P}\vct{\phi}(\eqtext{large}))\notag\\
&\qquad
+\mherm{(\vct{P}\vct{\phi}(\eqtext{large}))}
  \vct{\phi}(\eqtext{large})\Bigr)\notag\\
&\quad+\frac{\gradv\times
(\mherm{\vct{\phi}(\eqtext{large})}\sigv
 \vct{\phi}(\eqtext{large}))}{2m}
+O(m^{-2}).
\end{align*}
In the first numerator, the three entries are obtained by replacing
\(\vct{P}\) in turn by \(P_1,P_2,P_3\); thus this compact line is exactly
the three component equations.
The first term is the covariant-momentum flow and the second is the
magnetization curl. The latter has zero divergence, so agreement of both the
current and the continuity equation with the standard Pauli limit is obtained.

\subsubsection{Spin Up and Spin Down}
Where \(\vct{B}\neq\vct{0}\), its direction is used as the spin axis:
\begin{equation*}
\vct{u}=\frac{\vct{B}}{\|\vct{B}\|},
\end{equation*}
The projector pair from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projectors} is then used:
\begin{equation*}
\pv{\Pi}(\pm)=\tfrac12(1\pm \vct{u}\cdot\sigv),
\qquad
(\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)\pv{\Pi}(\pm)=\pm\pv{\Pi}(\pm).
\end{equation*}
The Pauli state is decomposed by
\begin{align*}
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large},\pm)
&=\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large}),\\
(\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large},\pm)
&=\pm\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large},\pm),\\
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})
&=\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large},+)+\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large},-),
\end{align*}
so \(\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large},+)\) and
\(\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large},-)\) are the spin-up and spin-down parts along
the field axis. The fixed-column condition
\(\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})
=\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})\) is preserved under
these left projectors.

At leading order, the Pauli equation is written as
\begin{align*}
\vct{P}&=-i\gradv-q\vct{A},\\
i\partial_t\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})
&=\left(\frac{\vct{P}^2}{2m}+qV\right)
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large})\notag\\
&\quad-\frac{q}{2m}\,\|\vct{B}\|(\vct{u}\cdot\sigv)
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large}).
\end{align*}
The following result is obtained by applying \(\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\) to the time evolution:
\begin{align*}
i\partial_t\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large},\pm)
&=\left(\frac{\vct{P}^2}{2m}+qV
-\frac{q}{2m}\|\vct{B}\|\,\vct{u}\cdot\sigv\right)
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large},\pm)\notag\\
&\quad
+\left(i\partial_t\pv{\Pi}(\pm)
+\frac{\com{\pv{\Pi}(\pm)}{\vct{P}^2}}{2m}\right)
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large}).
\end{align*}
Here \(qV\) and the local field-axis term commute pointwise with
\(\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\); spatial variation is incorporated through the displayed
kinetic commutator. The two local spin sectors are coupled by this term
together with \(\partial_t\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\).
If \(\vct{B}\) is uniform and static, then \(\pv{\Pi}(\pm)\) is constant and
the coupling term is zero, so the two spin sectors are decoupled. In that case
\begin{equation*}
(-\vct{B}\cdot\sigv)\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large},\pm)
=\mp \|\vct{B}\|\,\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large},\pm),
\end{equation*}
and the Zeeman effect is represented by
\begin{equation*}
i\partial_t\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large},\pm)
=\left(\frac{\vct{P}^2\mp q\|\vct{B}\|}{2m}+qV\right)
\pv{\phi}(\eqtext{large},\pm).
\end{equation*}
A preferred axis is therefore selected by the field direction, and opposite Zeeman shifts are acquired by the two projector parts.

\subsubsection{Free Energy and Momentum-Axis Checks}
The definitions in
Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-hamiltonian}--\eqref{eq:energy-spin-projectors} are
expanded; no additional projector notation is needed. The following result
is obtained by direct block multiplication:
\begin{align*}
\mat{\mathcal H}^{\,2}
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
(\sigv\cdot\vct{p})^2+m^2
&-m\sigv\cdot\vct{p}+m\sigv\cdot\vct{p}\\
-m\sigv\cdot\vct{p}+m\sigv\cdot\vct{p}
&(\sigv\cdot\vct{p})^2+m^2
\end{pmatrix}\notag\\
&=(m^2+\vct{p}^2)\I
=E(\vct{p})^2\I.
\end{align*}
Here \((\sigv\cdot\vct{p})^2=\vct{p}^2\) is obtained directly from the sigma
product. Because \(m\), \(\vct{p}\), and the sigma matrices are Hermitian,
\(\herm{\mat{\mathcal H}}=\mat{\mathcal H}\). The calligraphic
\(\mat{\mathcal H}\) names this block operator; the superscript \(H\) still
means the Hermitian operation.

The following relations are obtained from the definition of \(\bPsi(e)\) and the square:
\begin{align*}
\mat{\mathcal H}\bPsi(e)
&=\frac12\left(
\mat{\mathcal H}\bPsi
+e\frac{\mat{\mathcal H}^{\,2}\bPsi}{E(\vct{p})}\right)\notag\\
&=\frac12\left(
\mat{\mathcal H}\bPsi+eE(\vct{p})\bPsi\right)
=eE(\vct{p})\bPsi(e),\\
\bPsi(+)+\bPsi(-)&=\bPsi.
\end{align*}
Thus the two displayed fields really are the two energy-sign parts. Since
\(\mat{\mathcal H}\) is Hermitian, zero Hermitian pairing is obtained between
parts with different real eigenvalues.

For \(\vct{p}\neq\vct{0}\), the following relation is obtained from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:energy-spin-projectors}:
\[
(\sigv\cdot\vct{p})\pv{\Pi}(s)
=s\|\vct{p}\|\pv{\Pi}(s).
\]
The projector commutes with \(\sigv\cdot\vct{p}\), and its left action
commutes with the real mass entries of \(\mat{\mathcal H}\). Consequently
\begin{align*}
\mat{\mathcal H}\bPsi(e,s)
&=eE(\vct{p})\bPsi(e,s),\\
(\sigv\cdot\vct{p})\bPsi_n(e,s)
&=s\|\vct{p}\|\bPsi_n(e,s).
\end{align*}
The following especially transparent check is now obtained by writing the
Hamiltonian equation one block at a time:
\begin{align}
m\bPsi_2(e,s)
&=\bigl(eE(\vct{p})+s\|\vct{p}\|\bigr)\bPsi_1(e,s),\notag\\
m\bPsi_1(e,s)
&=\bigl(eE(\vct{p})-s\|\vct{p}\|\bigr)\bPsi_2(e,s).
\label{eq:energy-axis-block-check}
\end{align}
The following relation is recovered by multiplication of these two scalar factors:
\[
m^2
=\bigl(eE(\vct{p})+s\|\vct{p}\|\bigr)
 \bigl(eE(\vct{p})-s\|\vct{p}\|\bigr)
=E(\vct{p})^2-\vct{p}^2.
\]

Within one fixed \((e,s)\) sector, the selected columns are represented
locally by \(\{a;b\}\) for \(\bPsi_1(e,s)\) and
\(\{c;d\}\) for \(\bPsi_2(e,s)\). The momentum-axis equation is then exactly
\begin{align*}
p_3a+(p_1-ip_2)b&=s\|\vct{p}\|a,&
(p_1+ip_2)a-p_3b&=s\|\vct{p}\|b,\\
p_3c+(p_1-ip_2)d&=s\|\vct{p}\|c,&
(p_1+ip_2)c-p_3d&=s\|\vct{p}\|d.
\end{align*}
The following relations are obtained component by component from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:energy-axis-block-check}:
\begin{align*}
mc&=\bigl(eE(\vct{p})+s\|\vct{p}\|\bigr)a,&
md&=\bigl(eE(\vct{p})+s\|\vct{p}\|\bigr)b,\\
ma&=\bigl(eE(\vct{p})-s\|\vct{p}\|\bigr)c,&
mb&=\bigl(eE(\vct{p})-s\|\vct{p}\|\bigr)d.
\end{align*}
These are the usual free Dirac energy and momentum-axis spin relations,
written entirely with the present paravector conventions.
The physical current is also verified directly:
\begin{align*}
\frac{\vct{u}\cdot\vct{j}(\bPsi(e,s))}
{\rho(\bPsi(e,s))}
&=
s\,
\frac{|c|^2+|d|^2-|a|^2-|b|^2}
{|a|^2+|b|^2+|c|^2+|d|^2}
=\frac{e\|\vct{p}\|}{E(\vct{p})},\\
\frac{\vct{j}(\bPsi(e,s))}{\rho(\bPsi(e,s))}
&=\frac{e\vct{p}}{E(\vct{p})}.
\end{align*}
Thus the current divided by the density is the expected relativistic
velocity for each energy-sign mode.

Every displayed operation is applied on the left. Therefore the fixed-column
condition is preserved directly:
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\bigl(\pv{\Pi}(s)\bPsi_n(e)\bigr)
\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})\\
&\qquad=\pv{\Pi}(s)
\bigl(\bPsi_n(e)\pv{\Pi}(\eqtext{column})\bigr)\\
&\qquad=\pv{\Pi}(s)\bPsi_n(e).
\end{aligned}
\]
No second matrix column is introduced. At \(\vct{p}=0\) the momentum
direction, and hence helicity, is undefined; any separately chosen spin axis
may still be used.
