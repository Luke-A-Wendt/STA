\subsubsection{Relativistic Quantum-Mechanical Setting}
The wave-equation section uses one fixed operator language throughout. With the differential conventions from Section \ref{sec:differential},
\begin{align*}
\partial&=\partial_t+\sigv\cdot\gradv,\\
\partial^*&=\partial_t-\sigv\cdot\gradv,\\
(i\partial)^*&=-i\partial^*.
\end{align*}
Recall also that the d'Alembertian is
\begin{align*}
\Box
&:=\partial_t^2-\gradv^2\notag\\
&=\partial\partial^*=\partial^*\partial.
\end{align*}
All differential operators act on the fields to their right.  In an ordered
product the rightmost factor acts first, and the written order is retained
whenever potentials and derivatives do not commute.

\subsubsection{Algebra Blocks and the Physical Spinor Carrier}
Throughout the spinful part of the wave-equation section, a wave field is not a
space-time event but an algebra-valued amplitude field.  A generic algebra
block may be written
\begin{equation*}
\psi:\R\times\R^3\to\C\oplus\C^3,
\qquad
\psi(t,\mathbf r)=\psi_s(t,\mathbf r)+\psi_{\mathbf v}(t,\mathbf r)\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
The argument \((t,\mathbf r)\) tells where the state is evaluated in space-time, while the value \(\psi(t,\mathbf r)\) lives in the internal complex-paravector algebra. This distinction matters. The real paravector
\begin{equation*}
X=t+\mathbf r\cdot\sigv
\end{equation*}
describes an event or interval variable, whereas \(\psi\) is a quantum state amplitude defined \emph{over} such events.

At each fixed point, an unrestricted complex paravector has four complex
coefficients.  In the Pauli matrix realization it is an arbitrary
\(2\times2\) complex matrix, and left multiplication acts independently on
its two columns.  It is therefore the regular algebra module, not one
irreducible two-component spinor.  To select one spinor column without adding
component indices, fix once and for all
\begin{equation*}
\Pi_0:=\frac12(1+\sigma_3)
\end{equation*}
and restrict a physical Weyl block by
\begin{equation*}
\psi=\psi\Pi_0.
\end{equation*}
The set \((\C\oplus\C^3)\Pi_0\) is a minimal left ideal, selected by the
fixed factor on the right.  Every operator used below acts on the left, so the
restriction is preserved.  The factor \(\Pi_0\) is only a column selector; it
is not a measurement of spin along the third axis.

The scalar and vector coefficients of an algebra block are amplitude channels,
not separately measurable particles or classical subfields.  A bare component
count also does not assign physical spin: the transformation law, field
equation, and carrier restriction must be specified together.

The spin-0 Klein--Gordon subsections use \(\psi\in\C\).  The Dirac construction
uses a two-block field
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi=
\begin{Bmatrix}
\bPsi_1\\[2pt]
\bPsi_2
\end{Bmatrix},
\end{equation*}
with \(\bPsi_n=\bPsi_n\Pi_0\).  Each block then carries two complex
amplitudes and the pair carries the four complex amplitudes of one Dirac
field.  If the minimal-ideal restriction is omitted, the same equations remain
algebraically valid but describe two independent Dirac fields, one in each
matrix column.

\subsubsection{Complex-Paravector Bilinears and Bra-Ket Extraction}
To make the bra-ket notation precise in the paravector setting, write
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\psi&=\psi_s+\psi_{\mathbf v}\cdot\sigv,
&
O&=O_s+O_{\mathbf v}\cdot\sigv,\\
Z&=Z_s+Z_{\mathbf v}\cdot\sigv,
&
X&=X_s+X_{\mathbf v}\cdot\sigv,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
with \(\psi_s,Z_s\in\C\), \(\psi_{\mathbf v},Z_{\mathbf v}\in\C^3\),
\(X_s\in\R\), \(X_{\mathbf v}\in\R^3\), and with operator-valued
components acting on fields to their right. The three involutions on the state
are
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\conj{\psi}&=\psi_s^*-\psi_{\mathbf v}^*\cdot\sigv,\\
\rev{\psi}&=\psi_s^*+\psi_{\mathbf v}^*\cdot\sigv,\\
\crev{\psi}&=\psi_s-\psi_{\mathbf v}\cdot\sigv.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The basic component products are collected below.

For \(S\in\R\), the reverse sandwich is
\begin{equation*}
\rev{\psi}S\psi
=S\rev{\psi}\psi,
\end{equation*}
whose scalar part is
\begin{equation*}
\rho_\psi:=\eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\psi}\psi)
=|\psi_s|^2+\psi_{\mathbf v}^*\cdot\psi_{\mathbf v}\geq0.
\end{equation*}
In the matrix realization scalar extraction is one half of the trace, so on a
selected column the conventional column norm is \(2\rho_\psi\). The factor is
shown explicitly below when the physical Dirac density is defined.
This positive scalar supplies the time-slice Hilbert norm.  The vector part of
\(\rev{\psi}\psi\), however, is not automatically a spatial probability
current.  In fact, under the active spinor convention
\(\psi'=\rev{R}\psi\), it is invariant:
\begin{equation*}
\rev{\psi'}\psi'=\rev{\psi}R\rev{R}\psi=\rev{\psi}\psi.
\end{equation*}
Spatial spin or polarization information instead occurs in bilinears with the
opposite order, such as \(\psi\rev{\psi}\), or in operator sandwiches fixed by
the field equation.  More generally, \(\rev{\psi}O\psi\) contains four complex,
hence eight real, coefficient channels before an extraction is chosen.  No
generic physical interpretation is assigned to its nonscalar channels.

At fixed time, the algebra-block normalization is
\begin{equation*}
\langle \psi\mid\psi\rangle
:=\int_{\R^3}\eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\psi}\psi)\,\dd^3r
=\int_{\R^3}\rho_\psi\,\dd^3r
=1.
\end{equation*}
For a complex-paravector operator \(O\), define
\begin{equation*}
\langle \psi\mid O\mid\psi\rangle
:=\int_{\R^3}\eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\psi}O\psi)\,\dd^3r.
\end{equation*}
If the state is not already normalized, the corresponding expectation map is
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb E_\psi(O)
:=
\frac{\int_{\R^3}\eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\psi}O\psi)\,\dd^3r}
{\int_{\R^3}\eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\psi}\psi)\,\dd^3r}.
\end{equation*}
If \(O\psi=\phi_s+\phi_{\mathbf v}\cdot\sigv\), then the extracted scalar channel is simply
\begin{equation*}
\eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\psi}O\psi)
=\psi_s^*\phi_s+\psi_{\mathbf v}^*\cdot\phi_{\mathbf v}.
\end{equation*}
When \(O\) is reverse-self-adjoint under the spatial integral and the boundary
terms are benign, this scalar expectation is real.  For the Dirac field the
physical norm in the conventional column normalization used below is twice the
sum of this expression over its two restricted blocks; the field equation
below supplies the associated conserved spatial current.

By contrast,
\begin{equation*}
\crev{\psi}\psi
=\psi_s^2-\psi_{\mathbf v}\cdot\psi_{\mathbf v}
=\langle\!\langle\psi\rangle\!\rangle^2
\end{equation*}
is the determinant bilinear. It is algebraically useful, but it is not positive-definite and so it should not be used as the probabilistic normalization. In this framework the single bra-ket bracket \(\langle\cdot\mid\cdot\rangle\) is therefore best reserved for the scalar expectation map built from reversion, while \(\langle\!\langle\cdot\rangle\!\rangle^2\) continues to denote the determinant form.

\subsubsection{Carrier Spaces, Pairings, and Spin Assignment}
This subsection sharpens the interpretation of the carrier spaces already used above and connects them to the projector, commutator, and adjoint formulas in Eqs.\ \eqref{eq:projectors}, \eqref{eq:paravector-commutator}, and \eqref{eq:adjoint-paravector-braket}. The goal is to keep the algebraic carrier, the physical spin assignment, and the observable bilinears conceptually separate.
Standard physics does not assign spin from component count alone. The physically relevant data are
\begin{equation*}
(\text{transformation law},\ \text{field equation},\ \text{constraints}).
\end{equation*}
In standard Lorentz language,
\begin{align*}
\text{spin }0:&\quad (0,0),\\
\text{spin }\tfrac12:&\quad (\tfrac12,0)\oplus(0,\tfrac12),\\
\text{spin }1:&\quad (1,0)\ \text{or}\ (0,1)\quad \text{for one chiral }F,\\
&\quad (1,0)\oplus(0,1)\quad \text{for }F\text{ together with its conjugate},\\
&\quad (\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\quad \text{for }A^\mu.
\end{align*}
So the algebraic carrier spaces
\begin{equation*}
\C,\qquad
\C\oplus\C^3,\qquad
\{0\}\oplus\C^3
\end{equation*}
do not determine the physical spin by themselves. They become spin sectors only after the operator law is fixed.

\noindent\textbf{Bilinear pairings.}
The reverse product supplies the positive scalar norm
\begin{equation*}
\rho_\psi:=\eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\psi}\psi)
=|\psi_s|^2+\psi_{\mathbf v}^*\cdot\psi_{\mathbf v}.
\end{equation*}
Its vector part is an algebraic column-space channel, not a universal
space-time current.  Currents are identified only after a field equation and
transformation law have been fixed.
For two fields \(\phi,\psi\in\C\oplus\C^3\), the three scalar pairings are
\begin{align*}
\eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\phi}\psi)
&=\phi_s^*\psi_s+\phi_{\mathbf v}^*\cdot\psi_{\mathbf v}
&& \text{Hermitian-type pairing},\\
\eqtext{scalar}(\crev{\phi}\psi)
&=\phi_s\psi_s-\phi_{\mathbf v}\cdot\psi_{\mathbf v}
&& \text{metric pairing},\\
\eqtext{scalar}(\conj{\phi}\psi)
&=\phi_s^*\psi_s-\phi_{\mathbf v}^*\cdot\psi_{\mathbf v}
&& \text{indefinite companion}.
\end{align*}
For \(\phi=\psi\), the metric pairing reduces to
\begin{equation*}
\eqtext{scalar}(\crev{\psi}\psi)
=\crev{\psi}\psi
=\langle\!\langle\psi\rangle\!\rangle^2.
\end{equation*}
Only the reverse pairing is being used here as the quantum bra-ket pairing. The determinant pairing is the intrinsic quadratic form used in factorization identities, and the companion pairing is algebraically well-defined but does not carry an independent standard quantum interpretation here.

Accordingly, for the spatially integrated bracket
\begin{equation}
\langle \phi \mid \psi \rangle
:=\int_{\R^3}\eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\phi}\psi)\,\dd^3r,
\qquad
\langle \phi \mid O\psi \rangle
=\langle O^\dagger\phi \mid \psi \rangle,
\label{eq:adjoint-paravector-braket}
\end{equation}
the operator adjoint \(O^\dagger\) is the reverse-adjoint determined by this
pairing, up to the usual boundary terms when derivatives are present. For left
multiplication by a fixed paravector \(Z\), the adjoint is left multiplication
by \(\rev{Z}\), since
\(\rev{(\rev{Z}\phi)}=\rev{\phi}Z\).  Nonscalar parts of
\(\rev{\psi}O\psi\) remain available as algebraic bilinears, but a current,
flux, or polarization interpretation must be established separately for the
chosen operator and field equation.

\noindent\textbf{Generalized operator products.}
The following identities are algebraic. They describe operator composition on a paravector-valued state, but by themselves they do not assign a physical spin.

For operator-valued components, the commutator no longer collapses to the pure cross-product law of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:paravector-commutator}. Write
\begin{equation*}
O=O_s+O_{\mathbf v}\cdot\sigv,
\qquad
O'=O_s'+O_{\mathbf v}'\cdot\sigv,
\end{equation*}
with component operators commuting with the fixed basis elements and acting on
the state to their right. Then, with dot and cross products taken componentwise
and in the written operator order,
\begin{align}
\com{O}{O'}\psi
&=\Bigl(\com{O_s}{O_s'}
+O_{\mathbf v}\cdot O_{\mathbf v}'
-O_{\mathbf v}'\cdot O_{\mathbf v}\Bigr)\psi\notag\\
&\quad+\Bigl(\com{O_s}{O_{\mathbf v}'}
+\com{O_{\mathbf v}}{O_s'}\notag\\
&\qquad\qquad
+i\bigl(O_{\mathbf v}\times O_{\mathbf v}'
-O_{\mathbf v}'\times O_{\mathbf v}\bigr)\Bigr)\cdot\sigv\,\psi,
\label{eq:operator-commutator-action}
\end{align}
where the scalar-vector commutators are understood componentwise. Unlike the purely algebraic paravector commutator, Eq.\ \eqref{eq:operator-commutator-action} generally has both scalar and vector parts. Only when all component operators mutually commute does it reduce to the simpler law
\begin{equation*}
\com{O}{O'}\psi
=2i\bigl(O_{\mathbf v}\times O_{\mathbf v}'\bigr)\cdot\sigv\,\psi.
\end{equation*}

The matching anticommutator is
\begin{align}
\acom{O}{O'}\psi
&=(OO'+O'O)\psi\notag\\
&=\Bigl(\acom{O_s}{O_s'}
+O_{\mathbf v}\cdot O_{\mathbf v}'
+O_{\mathbf v}'\cdot O_{\mathbf v}\Bigr)\psi\notag\\
&\quad+\Bigl(\acom{O_s}{O_{\mathbf v}'}\notag\\
&\qquad\qquad
+\acom{O_{\mathbf v}}{O_s'}\notag\\
&\qquad\qquad
+i\bigl(O_{\mathbf v}\times O_{\mathbf v}'
+O_{\mathbf v}'\times O_{\mathbf v}\bigr)\Bigr)\cdot\sigv\,\psi,
\label{eq:operator-anticommutator-action}
\end{align}
so that
\begin{align*}
OO'
&=\tfrac12\bigl(\acom{O}{O'}+\com{O}{O'}\bigr).
\end{align*}
This is the natural companion to the operator commutator formula and is also the algebraic starting point for covariance and uncertainty identities once a state, expectation functional, and operator domain have been specified.

\noindent\textbf{Carrier closure.}
The same algebra shows which carrier spaces are actually closed under which operators:
\begin{align}
\partial:\C&\to\C\oplus\C^3,\notag\\
\partial:\{0\}\oplus\C^3&\to\C\oplus\C^3,\notag\\
\partial:\C\oplus\C^3&\to\C\oplus\C^3,\notag\\
\partial:(\C\oplus\C^3)\Pi_0
&\to(\C\oplus\C^3)\Pi_0.
\label{eq:carrier-closure}
\end{align}
Indeed,
\begin{align*}
\partial\psi_s
&=(\partial_t\psi_s)+(\gradv\psi_s)\cdot\sigv,\\
\partial(\psi_{\mathbf v}\cdot\sigv)
&=(\gradv\cdot\psi_{\mathbf v})
+\bigl(\partial_t\psi_{\mathbf v}+i\,\gradv\times\psi_{\mathbf v}\bigr)\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
The full complex-paravector space is the smallest algebra containing the scalar
and vector channels, but it is not the smallest left module. The minimal left
ideal \((\C\oplus\C^3)\Pi_0\), selected by a fixed right factor, is a
two-complex-dimensional left module and is
also closed because every differential and potential operator acts on the
left.  The scalar subspace closes only after passing to scalar second-order
combinations such as \(\Box+m^2\). A pure-vector specialization is
algebraically meaningful, but it is not preserved by generic first-order
paravector action and therefore requires Maxwell- or Proca-type constraints if
it is to describe a spin-1 field. A real-paravector specialization is not a
general quantum carrier because it is not closed under multiplication by the
phase \(\exp(i\alpha)\).

With those algebraic preliminaries fixed, the organizing principle is this: one first chooses a carrier field for \(\psi\), then restricts to the operators that preserve that carrier or define its field equation, and then lists the bilinear pairings that extract the physically relevant observables from that carrier. The bilinears need not take values in the same carrier field; in general they land in scalar, vector, or paravector observable channels.

With that understood, the physically consistent sector-by-sector summary is the following.

\noindent\textbf{1. Spin \(0\): complex scalar Klein--Gordon sector}
\begin{align*}
\text{carrier:}\quad
&\psi:\R^{1,3}\to\C,\\
\text{representation:}\quad
&(0,0),\\
\text{field equation:}\quad
&(\Box+m^2)\psi=0,\\
\text{operator class:}\quad
&O_s:\C\to\C,\\
\text{examples:}\quad
&\Box+m^2,\ \partial_t\pm i\Omega,\\
&-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2,\ldots,\\
\text{spin projector:}\quad
&\Pi(\pm)\psi\notin\C.
\end{align*}
The scalar pairings reduce to
\begin{align*}
\eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\phi}\psi)
&=\phi^*\psi,\\
\eqtext{scalar}(\crev{\phi}\psi)
&=\phi\psi,\\
\eqtext{scalar}(\conj{\phi}\psi)
&=\phi^*\psi.
\end{align*}
So the reverse and sigma-conjugate pairings coincide on the scalar sector,
while the metric pairing is different.  The local quantity \(|\psi|^2\) is
positive but is not the time component of the conserved relativistic current.
For \(m>0\), the derivative-built current can be kept in scalar/vector form using the covariant operators already defined:
\begin{align*}
\rho_{\mathrm{KG}}
&:=\frac{1}{2m}\left(\psi^*E\psi+(E\psi)^*\psi\right),\\
\mathbf j_{\mathrm{KG}}
&:=\frac{1}{2m}\left(\psi^*\mathbf P\psi
+(\mathbf P\psi)^*\psi\right),\\
\partial_t\rho_{\mathrm{KG}}+\gradv\cdot\mathbf j_{\mathrm{KG}}&=0.
\end{align*}
For the free field set \(q=0\).  The density is conserved but is not positive
on the full positive- and negative-frequency solution space. A one-particle
interpretation therefore requires a frequency restriction, while quantum
field theory interprets the two branches as particle and antiparticle modes.

\noindent\textbf{2. Spin \(\tfrac12\): Dirac/Pauli sector}
\begin{align*}
\text{algebraic block:}\quad
&\psi:\R^{1,3}\to\C\oplus\C^3,\\
\text{physical field:}\quad
&\bPsi=
\begin{Bmatrix}
\bPsi_1\\[2pt]
\bPsi_2
\end{Bmatrix},
\qquad
\bPsi_n\in(\C\oplus\C^3)\Pi_0,\\
\text{representation:}\quad
&(\tfrac12,0)\oplus(0,\tfrac12),\\
\text{field equations:}\quad
&(\Wmat(i\partial)-m\I)\bPsi=0,\\
&(\Wmat(i\partial-q\Phi^*)-m\I)\bPsi=0,\\
\text{operator class:}\quad
&O=O_s+O_{\mathbf v}\cdot\sigv,\\
&\text{acting on each paravector block},\\
\text{examples:}\quad
&\partial,\ \partial^*,\\
&E\pm\sigv\cdot\mathbf P,\ \Pi(\pm),\\
\text{spin projector:}\quad
&\Pi(\pm)\text{ acts from the left on each block}.
\end{align*}
The basic one-block pairings are
\begin{align*}
\eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\phi}\psi)
&=\phi_s^*\psi_s+\phi_{\mathbf v}^*\cdot\psi_{\mathbf v},\\
\eqtext{scalar}(\crev{\phi}\psi)
&=\phi_s\psi_s-\phi_{\mathbf v}\cdot\psi_{\mathbf v},\\
\eqtext{scalar}(\conj{\phi}\psi)
&=\phi_s^*\psi_s-\phi_{\mathbf v}^*\cdot\psi_{\mathbf v}.
\end{align*}
For the two-block Dirac carrier, the natural extension is
\begin{equation*}
\langle \{\Xi_1;\Xi_2\} \mid \{\Psi_1;\Psi_2\} \rangle
:=2\int_{\R^3}
\sum_{n=1}^{2}\eqtext{scalar}(\rev{\Xi_n}\Psi_n)\,\dd^3r.
\end{equation*}
The reverse pairing is the positive Hermitian-type pairing used for time-slice
expectations. The metric pairing is the algebraic form used in identities such
as \(Z\crev{Z}\), and the companion pairing is indefinite. The minimal-ideal
restriction makes each block one Weyl spinor. Without it, the two matrix
columns give two independent copies of every displayed Dirac and Pauli
equation.

The coupled first-order equation gives the conserved Dirac density and current
directly:
\begin{align*}
\rho_D
&:=2\,\eqtext{scalar}\!\left(
\rev{\bPsi_1}\bPsi_1+\rev{\bPsi_2}\bPsi_2\right),\\
\mathbf j_D
&:=2\,\eqtext{vector}\!\left(
\bPsi_2\rev{\bPsi_2}-\bPsi_1\rev{\bPsi_1}\right),\\
\partial_t\rho_D+\gradv\cdot\mathbf j_D&=0.
\end{align*}
The opposite product order in \(\mathbf j_D\) is essential: it is the order
that transforms as a spatial vector under left spinor transformations.

\noindent\textbf{3. Spin \(1\): vector or field-strength sector}
\begin{align*}
\text{carrier:}\quad
&F:\R^{1,3}\to\{0\}\oplus\C^3,\\
\text{main example:}\quad
&F=(\mathbf E+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv,\\
\text{representation:}\quad
&(1,0)\ \text{or}\ (0,1)\quad\text{for }F,\\
&(1,0)\oplus(0,1)\quad\text{for }F\text{ and its conjugate},\\
&(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\quad\text{for a vector potential }A^\mu,\\
\text{field equation:}\quad
&\partial F=D^*,\\
\text{operator class:}\quad
&\partial,\ \partial^*,\ \Box,\ \text{and Maxwell/Proca constraints},\\
\text{closure facts:}\quad
&\partial,\partial^*:\{0\}\oplus\C^3\to\C\oplus\C^3,\\
&\Box:\{0\}\oplus\C^3\to\{0\}\oplus\C^3,\\
\text{spin projector:}\quad
&\Pi(\pm)F\notin\{0\}\oplus\C^3.
\end{align*}
So the two-projector split \(\Pi(\pm)\) is not the standard spin-\(1\) decomposition. The standard polarization counts are
\begin{align*}
\text{massive spin }1:&\quad m_s=-1,0,1,\\
\text{massless spin }1:&\quad \lambda=\pm1.
\end{align*}
The bilinear quantities already present in the manuscript are
\begin{align*}
\tfrac12F^2
&=\tfrac12(\mathbf E^2-\mathbf B^2)+i\,\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B,\\
\tfrac12F F^{\mathrm R}
&=\tfrac12(\mathbf E^2+\mathbf B^2)+(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
These have direct standard physical interpretations: \(\tfrac12F^2\) packages the Lorentz invariants, while \(\tfrac12F F^{\mathrm R}\) packages the energy density and the Poynting vector. A simple bra-ket built from \(\rev{F}F\) is not the standard Maxwell-field pairing used here; the physically relevant quantities are the field bilinears together with the field equations and their gauge or subsidiary constraints.

The consistent summary is therefore this:
\begin{align*}
\text{spin}
&\neq
\text{component count alone},\\
\text{spin}
&=
(\text{carrier},\ \text{operator law},\ \text{physical bilinears},\ \text{constraints}).
\end{align*}
Within that summary, \((\cdot)^{\mathrm R}\) is the pairing used for quantum expectations, \((\cdot)^{*\mathrm R}\) supplies the determinant pairing used for algebraic factorization, and the pure sigma-conjugate pairing remains an auxiliary companion.

\subsubsection{Spin-0 Wave Mechanics}
The scalar relativistic wave equation can be read directly from the mass-shell identity developed earlier in the kinematic section. Here \(\psi\in\C\) is a complex scalar field, \(m\in\R\), and the free-particle operators are defined using the differential conventions from Section \ref{sec:differential}. Start from
\begin{equation*}
\langle\!\langle mU\rangle\!\rangle^2=m^2
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2=0.
\end{equation*}
For a free particle,
\begin{equation*}
E=i\partial_t,
\qquad
\mathbf P=-i\gradv,
\end{equation*}
and equivalently
\begin{equation*}
E+\mathbf P\cdot\sigv=i\partial^*.
\end{equation*}
Acting on a scalar field \(\psi\), the operator chain is
\begin{align*}
(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2)\psi
&=\bigl(-(i\partial_t)^2-\gradv^2+m^2\bigr)\psi\notag\\
&=\bigl(\partial_t^2-\gradv^2+m^2\bigr)\psi\notag\\
&=(\partial\partial^*+m^2)\psi\notag\\
&=(\Box+m^2)\psi.
\end{align*}
Thus the free spin-0 relativistic wave equation is
\begin{equation*}
(\Box+m^2)\psi=0
\qquad\text{Klein--Gordon equation}.
\end{equation*}
This is the scalar wave equation recorded in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:kg-free}. The dispersion relation \(\omega^2=\mathbf k^2+m^2\) and the later positive/negative-frequency split are consequences of this same operator identity.

\subsubsection{Positive and Negative Frequency Sectors}
The frequency decomposition is one of the central interpretive steps in the
free scalar theory. On a spatial domain where the positive square root is
defined, set
\begin{align*}
\Omega
&=\sqrt{m^2-\gradv^2}\notag\\
&=\langle\!\langle m+\sigv\cdot\gradv\rangle\!\rangle.
\end{align*}
Accordingly,
\begin{align*}
\Box+m^2
&=\partial_t^2+\Omega^2\notag\\
&=\partial_t^2+(m^2-\gradv^2)\notag\\
&=(\partial_t-i\Omega)(\partial_t+i\Omega)\notag\\
&=(\partial_t+i\Omega)(\partial_t-i\Omega).
\end{align*}
The positive- and negative-frequency sectors are then defined by
\begin{align*}
(\partial_t\pm i\Omega)\psi(\pm)&=0,\\
\psi(\pm,t)&=\exp\!\bigl(\mp i\Omega(t-t_0)\bigr)\psi(\pm,t_0),\\
\psi&=\psi(+)+\psi(-).
\end{align*}
Here the parenthesized sign labels frequency and is not the spin-axis label
in \(\Pi(\pm)\psi\).
When \(\Omega^{-1}\) exists, the two parts of a Klein--Gordon solution at any
time can equivalently be extracted as
\begin{equation*}
\psi(\pm)=\frac12\left(\psi\pm i\Omega^{-1}\partial_t\psi\right).
\end{equation*}
For a massless field the spatial zero mode is treated separately.  The split
separates the two frequency branches; it does not turn the negative-frequency
part into a negative probability.  In a relativistic quantum field, the two
branches acquire the particle--antiparticle interpretation \cite{greiner2000}.

\subsubsection{Fourier Meaning of the Frequency Operator}
The nonlocal square root \(\Omega\) is most transparent one spatial Fourier
mode at a time.  With the standard phase convention, write schematically
\begin{align*}
\psi&=\sum_{n} c_{n}f_{n},\\
f_{n}&=\exp\!\bigl(-i\omega_{n}t+i\mathbf k_{n}\cdot\mathbf r\bigr).
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{align*}
\Box\psi
&=\sum_{n}(\mathbf k_{n}^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2})c_{n}f_{n},\\
(\Box+m^{2})\psi
&=\sum_{n}(\mathbf k_{n}^{2}+m^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2})c_{n}f_{n},\\
\Omega\psi
&=\sum_n\sqrt{m^2+\mathbf k_n^2}\,c_nf_n.
\end{align*}
For on-shell Klein--Gordon modes,
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf k_{n}^{2}+m^{2}=\omega_{n}^{2},
\qquad
\omega_{n}=\pm\sqrt{\mathbf k_{n}^{2}+m^{2}},
\end{equation*}
and \(\Omega\) multiplies each spatial mode by its positive energy magnitude.
This defines the only square-root operator needed in the frequency split and
avoids assigning an unnecessary branch to \(\sqrt{\Box}\).

\subsubsection{Klein--Gordon with Potential Fields}
To keep the minimally coupled scalar theory in one self-contained place, start from the charged-particle Lagrangian
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal L=-\frac{m}{\gamma}-q(V-\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf A).
\end{equation*}
Here \(E\) denotes the energy operator, not the electric field \(\mathbf E\). The Hamiltonian and canonical momentum are then
\begin{equation*}
i\partial_t=\mathcal H=E+qV,
\qquad
-i\gradv=\delv\mathcal L=\mathbf P+q\mathbf A,
\end{equation*}
so the covariant energy and momentum operators are
\begin{equation*}
E=i\partial_t-qV,
\qquad
\mathbf P=-i\gradv-q\mathbf A.
\end{equation*}

Acting on a smooth test field \(\psi\), the Leibniz rules needed below are
\begin{align*}
\partial_t(V\psi)&=(\partial_tV)\psi+V\partial_t\psi,\\
\partial_m(A_n\psi)&=(\partial_mA_n)\psi+A_n\partial_m\psi.
\end{align*}
From these one obtains
\begin{align*}
E^2\psi
&=E(i\partial_t\psi-qV\psi)\notag\\
&=\left(-\partial_t^2-iq\,\partial_tV-2iqV\partial_t+q^2V^2\right)\psi,\\
\mathbf P^2\psi
&=\mathbf P\cdot(-i\gradv\psi-q\mathbf A\psi)\notag\\
&=\left(-\gradv^2+iq\,\gradv\cdot\mathbf A+2iq\,\mathbf A\cdot\gradv+q^2\mathbf A^2\right)\psi.
\end{align*}

Recall also
\begin{align*}
\Box&=\partial_t^2-\gradv^2,\\
\Phi&=V+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv,\\
S&=\partial_tV+\gradv\cdot\mathbf A,\\
\langle\!\langle\partial\rangle\!\rangle^2&=\Box,\\
\langle\!\langle\Phi\rangle\!\rangle^2&=V^2-\mathbf A^2.
\end{align*}
Substituting the operator expansions gives the minimally coupled Klein--Gordon equation in explicit form:
\begin{align*}
0&=(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2)\psi\notag\\
&=\Bigl(\Box+iq(\partial_tV+\gradv\cdot\mathbf A)+2iq(V\partial_t+\mathbf A\cdot\gradv)\notag\\
&\qquad\qquad-q^2(V^2-\mathbf A^2)+m^2\Bigr)\psi\notag\\
&=\Bigl(\Box+iqS-q^2\langle\!\langle\Phi\rangle\!\rangle^2+m^2+2iq(V\partial_t+\mathbf A\cdot\gradv)\Bigr)\psi.
\end{align*}

The electromagnetic fields and commutators enter through
\begin{align*}
\mathbf E&=-\partial_t\mathbf A-\gradv V,\\
\mathbf B&=\gradv\times\mathbf A,\\
F&=(\mathbf E+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv,
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
\com{E}{P_m}\psi
&=(i\partial_t-qV)(-i\partial_m\psi-qA_m\psi)\notag\\
&\quad-(-i\partial_m-qA_m)(i\partial_t\psi-qV\psi)\notag\\
&=-iq\bigl((\partial_tA_m)\psi+A_m\partial_t\psi\bigr)\notag\\
&\quad-iq\bigl((\partial_mV)\psi+V\partial_m\psi\bigr)\notag\\
&\quad+iqV\partial_m\psi+iqA_m\partial_t\psi\notag\\
&=-iq(\partial_tA_m+\partial_mV)\psi\notag\\
&=iqE_m\psi,\\
\com{P_m}{P_n}\psi
&=(-i\partial_m-qA_m)\bigl(-i\partial_n\psi-qA_n\psi\bigr)\notag\\
&\quad-(-i\partial_n-qA_n)\bigl(-i\partial_m\psi-qA_m\psi\bigr)\notag\\
&=iq(\partial_mA_n-\partial_nA_m)\psi.
\end{align*}
Hence
\begin{equation*}
\com{E}{\mathbf P}=iq\mathbf E,
\qquad
\mathbf P\times\mathbf P=iq\mathbf B.
\end{equation*}
Substituting those commutators into the ordered products yields
\begin{align*}
(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)(E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)
&=E^2-\mathbf P^2\notag\\
&\qquad+\sigv\cdot\bigl(-\com{E}{\mathbf P}-i\,\mathbf P\times\mathbf P\bigr)\notag\\
&=E^2-\mathbf P^2-iqF,\\
(E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)
&=E^2-\mathbf P^2\notag\\
&\qquad+\sigv\cdot\bigl(\com{E}{\mathbf P}-i\,\mathbf P\times\mathbf P\bigr)\notag\\
&=E^2-\mathbf P^2-iqF^*.
\end{align*}
Since
\begin{equation*}
E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P=i\partial^*-q\Phi,
\qquad
E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P=i\partial-q\Phi^*
=(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)^{*\mathrm R},
\end{equation*}
the two ordered reverse-conjugate products are
\begin{equation*}
(E\pm\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)
(E\pm\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)^{*\mathrm R}
=E^2-\mathbf P^2+\sigv\cdot\bigl(\mp\com{E}{\mathbf P}-i\,\mathbf P\times\mathbf P\bigr).
\end{equation*}
Unlike the determinant form defined for commuting complex
coefficients, these operator products retain a vector part.  Operator ordering
therefore matters, and the two first-order products remain distinct.
Consequently,
\begin{align*}
-(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)^{*\mathrm R}
&=(\partial^*+iq\Phi)(\partial+iq\Phi^*),\\
-(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)^{*\mathrm R}(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)
&=(\partial+iq\Phi^*)(\partial^*+iq\Phi).
\end{align*}
The same factorizations can be rewritten directly in derivative form:
\begin{align*}
(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)(E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)\psi
&=-(\partial^*+iq\Phi)(\partial+iq\Phi^*)\psi\notag\\
&=-\Box\psi-iq\,\partial^*(\Phi^*\psi)\notag\\
&\qquad-iq\,\Phi\,\partial\psi\notag\\
&\qquad+q^2\langle\!\langle\Phi\rangle\!\rangle^2\psi\notag\\
&=-\Box\psi-iqS\psi+q^2\langle\!\langle\Phi\rangle\!\rangle^2\psi\notag\\
&\qquad-2iq(V\partial_t+\mathbf A\cdot\gradv)\psi\notag\\
&\qquad-iqF\psi,\\
(E-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)(E+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)\psi
&=-(\partial+iq\Phi^*)(\partial^*+iq\Phi)\psi\notag\\
&=-\Box\psi-iq\,\partial(\Phi\psi)\notag\\
&\qquad-iq\,\Phi^*\,\partial^*\psi\notag\\
&\qquad+q^2\langle\!\langle\Phi\rangle\!\rangle^2\psi\notag\\
&=-\Box\psi-iqS\psi+q^2\langle\!\langle\Phi\rangle\!\rangle^2\psi\notag\\
&\qquad-2iq(V\partial_t+\mathbf A\cdot\gradv)\psi\notag\\
&\qquad-iqF^*\psi.
\end{align*}
Equivalently,
\begin{align*}
\partial^*(\Phi^*\psi)+\Phi\,\partial\psi
&=S\psi+2(V\partial_t+\mathbf A\cdot\gradv)\psi+F\psi,\\
\partial(\Phi\psi)+\Phi^*\,\partial^*\psi
&=S\psi+2(V\partial_t+\mathbf A\cdot\gradv)\psi+F^*\psi,
\end{align*}
and their difference isolates the electric contribution:
\begin{align*}
\bigl(\partial^*(\Phi^*\psi)+\Phi\,\partial\psi\bigr)
&-\bigl(\partial(\Phi\psi)+\Phi^*\,\partial^*\psi\bigr)\notag\\
&=\bigl((\partial\Phi)^*-\partial\Phi\bigr)\psi\notag\\
&=(F-F^*)\psi\notag\\
&=2(\mathbf E\cdot\sigv)\psi.
\end{align*}

In summary,
\begin{align*}
E&:=i\partial_t-qV, &
\mathbf P&:=-i\gradv-q\mathbf A,\\
\partial&:=\partial_t+\sigv\cdot\gradv, &
\partial^*&:=\partial_t-\sigv\cdot\gradv,\\
\Phi&:=V+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv, &
F&:=(\mathbf E+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv,\\
S&:=\partial_tV+\gradv\cdot\mathbf A, &
\mathbf E&:=-\partial_t\mathbf A-\gradv V,\\
\mathbf B&:=\gradv\times\mathbf A,
\end{align*}
and the scalar Klein--Gordon operator can be recovered from either ordered first-order product:
\begin{align*}
0&=(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2)\psi\notag\\
&=\Bigl(\Box+iqS-q^2\langle\!\langle\Phi\rangle\!\rangle^2+m^2+2iq(V\partial_t+\mathbf A\cdot\gradv)\Bigr)\psi\notag\\
&=(\partial^*+iq\Phi)(\partial+iq\Phi^*)\psi-iqF\psi+m^2\psi\notag\\
&=(\partial+iq\Phi^*)(\partial^*+iq\Phi)\psi-iqF^*\psi+m^2\psi.
\end{align*}
The ordered first-order products themselves obey
\begin{align*}
(E\pm\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)(E\mp\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)
&=E^2-(\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)^2\mp\com{E}{\sigv\cdot\mathbf P}\notag\\
&=E^2-\mathbf P^2-iq(\pm\mathbf E+i\mathbf B)\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
Taking the scalar part therefore recovers
\begin{equation*}
0=\eqtext{scalar}\!\bigl((\partial+iq\Phi^*)(\partial^*+iq\Phi)\bigr)\psi+m^2\psi.
\end{equation*}
Only the scalar part reproduces the Klein--Gordon operator itself; the full ordered products retain the non-scalar field terms \(-iqF\) and \(-iqF^*\) that distinguish the two factorizations.

\subsubsection{Spin-\texorpdfstring{$\frac12$}{1/2} Wave Mechanics}
Before the electromagnetic field is turned on, the same auxiliary
\(2\times2\) map from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:W-map} gives a self-contained
first-order version of the relativistic mass shell. Consider the two-block
field
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi=
\begin{Bmatrix}
\bPsi_1\\[2pt]
\bPsi_2
\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
For one Dirac field both blocks obey \(\bPsi_n=\bPsi_n\Pi_0\).  If they are
left unrestricted, every formula below acts independently on the two matrix
columns and therefore describes two identical Dirac fields.
For any complex paravector or paravector-valued operator \(Z\), define
\begin{align*}
\Wmat(Z)&:=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & Z \\
\crev{Z} & 0
\end{bmatrix},\\
\Wmat(Z)^2&=\operatorname{diag}(Z\crev{Z},\crev{Z}Z).
\end{align*}
The map is complex-linear, so in particular
\begin{align*}
\Wmat(i\partial)
&=i
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \partial \\
\partial^* & 0
\end{bmatrix}
=i\Wmat(\partial),\\
\Wmat(i\partial)^2
&=-\operatorname{diag}(\partial\partial^*,\partial^*\partial)\notag\\
&=-\Box\I.
\end{align*}

Define the free Dirac operator matrix and its mass conjugate by
\begin{equation*}
\Dmat:=\Wmat(i\partial)-m\I,
\qquad
\Dmat':=\Wmat(i\partial)+m\I.
\end{equation*}
Then
\begin{align*}
\Dmat'\Dmat=\Dmat\Dmat'
&=(\Wmat(i\partial)+m\I)(\Wmat(i\partial)-m\I)\notag\\
&=\Wmat(i\partial)^2-m^2\I\notag\\
&=-(\Box+m^2)\I.
\end{align*}
Thus the free Dirac equation
\begin{equation*}
\Dmat\bPsi=0
\end{equation*}
forces each block to satisfy the Klein--Gordon operator.  No determinant of
algebra-valued blocks is needed: Eq.\ \eqref{eq:W-square} supplies the relevant
minimal polynomial directly.

The equivalence with the usual Weyl form is explicit in the Pauli matrix
realization.  For a plane wave
\begin{equation*}
\exp(-iEt+i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r)
\end{equation*}
and one selected matrix column \(\xi_n\) of each block, the two equations are
\begin{equation*}
(E-\mathbf p\cdot\sigv)\xi_2=m\xi_1,
\qquad
(E+\mathbf p\cdot\sigv)\xi_1=m\xi_2.
\end{equation*}
Their product gives \(E^2=\mathbf p^2+m^2\).  These are precisely the two
Weyl equations for one four-component Dirac spinor: the two selected columns
carry the conjugate Weyl transformation laws, and their pair realizes
\((\tfrac12,0)\oplus(0,\tfrac12)\). Retaining both matrix columns gives two
independent copies of this system.

Writing out \(\Dmat\bPsi=0\) gives the two coupled equations
\begin{align*}
i\partial\bPsi_2-m\bPsi_1&=0,\\
i\partial^*\bPsi_1-m\bPsi_2&=0.
\end{align*}
For \(m>0\), solving the first equation for the upper component and substituting into the second yields
\begin{align*}
\bPsi_1&=\frac{i\partial\bPsi_2}{m},\\
0&=i\partial^*\bPsi_1-m\bPsi_2\notag\\
&=-\frac{\partial^*\partial\bPsi_2}{m}-m\bPsi_2\notag\\
&=-\frac{(\Box+m^2)\bPsi_2}{m}.
\end{align*}
Hence the lower component is a Klein--Gordon solution, and the upper component is obtained from it by one first-order derivative.

Conversely, let \(\psi'\) be a scalar Klein--Gordon solution and let
\(a,b\in(\C\oplus\C^3)\Pi_0\) be constant polarization elements.  Apply the
mass-conjugate operator to the ideal-valued seed:
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi
:=\frac1m\Dmat'
\begin{Bmatrix}
\psi'a\\[2pt]
\psi'b
\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
Because the constants occur on the right, derivatives act only on \(\psi'\),
and the two blocks are
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi_1=\psi'a+\frac{i}{m}(\partial\psi')b,
\qquad
\bPsi_2=\frac{i}{m}(\partial^*\psi')a+\psi'b.
\end{equation*}
Using \(\Dmat\Dmat'=-(\Box+m^2)\I\) gives
\begin{equation*}
\Dmat\bPsi
=-\frac1m(\Box+m^2)\psi'
\begin{Bmatrix}a\\ b\end{Bmatrix}
=0.
\end{equation*}
The constants \(a\) and \(b\) carry the spin polarization; a bare scalar
solution alone does not.  For example, \(a=\Pi_0\), \(b=0\) gives
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi=
\begin{Bmatrix}
\psi'\Pi_0\\[2pt]
\dfrac{i}{m}(\partial^*\psi')\Pi_0
\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
This is the first-order lift from the Klein--Gordon mass shell into the chosen
Dirac spinor module.

\subsubsection{Dirac with Potential Field}
Consider again the two-block field
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi=
\begin{Bmatrix}
\bPsi_1\\[2pt]
\bPsi_2
\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
The one-field interpretation continues to impose
\(\bPsi_n=\bPsi_n\Pi_0\); all potential and derivative factors act from the
left and preserve the ideal.
For any complex paravector or paravector-valued operator \(Z\), define
\begin{align*}
\Wmat(Z)&:=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & Z \\
\crev{Z} & 0
\end{bmatrix},\\
\Wmat(Z)^2&=\operatorname{diag}(Z\crev{Z},\crev{Z}Z),\\
\Wmat(Z)^*&=\Wmat(Z^*),\\
\Wmat(Z)^{\mathrm R}&=\Wmat(Z^{\mathrm R}).
\end{align*}
The Dirac operator with potential is
\begin{align*}
\Dmat&:=\Wmat(i\partial-q\Phi^*)-m\I\notag\\
&=i\Wmat(\partial)-q\Wmat(\Phi^*)-m\I.
\end{align*}
Because
\begin{align*}
\Wmat(\partial)^2&=\Box\I,\\
\Wmat(\partial)\Wmat(\Phi^*)
&=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \partial\\
\partial^* & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \Phi^*\\
\Phi & 0
\end{bmatrix}
=\operatorname{diag}(\partial\Phi,\partial^*\Phi^*),\\
\Wmat(\Phi^*)\Wmat(\partial)
&=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \Phi^*\\
\Phi & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \partial\\
\partial^* & 0
\end{bmatrix}
=\operatorname{diag}(\Phi^*\partial^*,\Phi\partial),\\
\Wmat(\Phi^*)^2
&=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \Phi^*\\
\Phi & 0
\end{bmatrix}^{2}
=\operatorname{diag}(\Phi^*\Phi,\Phi\Phi^*)\notag\\
&=\langle\!\langle\Phi\rangle\!\rangle^2\I,
\end{align*}
one obtains
\begin{align*}
\Wmat(i\partial-q\Phi^*)^2
&=-\Wmat(\partial)^2\notag\\
&\qquad-iq\bigl(\Wmat(\partial)\Wmat(\Phi^*)+\Wmat(\Phi^*)\Wmat(\partial)\bigr)\notag\\
&\qquad+q^2\Wmat(\Phi^*)^2\notag\\
&=-\Box\I-iq\,\operatorname{diag}(\partial\Phi+\Phi^*\partial^*,\partial^*\Phi^*+\Phi\partial)\notag\\
&\qquad+q^2\langle\!\langle\Phi\rangle\!\rangle^2\I\notag\\
&=-\operatorname{diag}\!\Big(
(\partial+iq\Phi^*)(\partial^*+iq\Phi),\notag\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad
(\partial^*+iq\Phi)(\partial+iq\Phi^*)
\Big).
\end{align*}
The Dirac equation
\begin{equation*}
\Dmat\bPsi=0
\end{equation*}
therefore becomes the two coupled equations
\begin{align*}
-m\bPsi_1+(i\partial-q\Phi^*)\bPsi_2&=0,\\
(i\partial^*-q\Phi)\bPsi_1-m\bPsi_2&=0.
\end{align*}
Equivalently, their time evolution is
\begin{align*}
i\partial_t\bPsi_1
&=(qV-\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)\bPsi_1+m\bPsi_2,\\
i\partial_t\bPsi_2
&=(qV+\sigv\cdot\mathbf P)\bPsi_2+m\bPsi_1.
\end{align*}
Reverse these equations, multiply on the opposite side, and take scalar
parts.  The real potential terms cancel within each block, the two mass
terms cancel in the sum, and the derivative terms combine into a divergence.
Consequently,
\begin{equation*}
\partial_t\eqtext{scalar}\!\left(
\rev{\bPsi_1}\bPsi_1+\rev{\bPsi_2}\bPsi_2\right)
+\gradv\cdot\eqtext{vector}\!\left(
\bPsi_2\rev{\bPsi_2}-\bPsi_1\rev{\bPsi_1}\right)=0.
\end{equation*}
This verifies the density and current stated above and explains why the vector
part of a single \(\rev{\psi}\psi\) is not the Dirac current.
Solving the first equation for the upper component and substituting into the second gives
\begin{align*}
\bPsi_1&=\frac{(i\partial-q\Phi^*)\bPsi_2}{m},\\
0&=(i\partial^*-q\Phi)\bPsi_1-m\bPsi_2\notag\\
&=-\frac{\bigl((\partial^*+iq\Phi)(\partial+iq\Phi^*)+m^2\bigr)\bPsi_2}{m}.
\end{align*}
Thus each block satisfies a Klein--Gordon-type operator with the appropriate
ordered field coupling.

The mass-conjugate operator is
\begin{equation*}
\Dmat':=\Wmat(i\partial-q\Phi^*)+m\I.
\end{equation*}
Multiplying by \(\Dmat'\) yields
\begin{align*}
0=\Dmat'\Dmat\bPsi
&=\bigl(\Wmat(i\partial-q\Phi^*)^2-m^2\I\bigr)\bPsi\notag\\
&=-\operatorname{diag}\!\Big(
(\partial+iq\Phi^*)(\partial^*+iq\Phi)+m^2,\notag\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad
(\partial^*+iq\Phi)(\partial+iq\Phi^*)+m^2
\Big)\bPsi.
\end{align*}
Equivalently,
\begin{align*}
0&=\bigl((\partial+iq\Phi^*)(\partial^*+iq\Phi)+m^2\bigr)\bPsi_1\notag\\
&=\bigl(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2+iqF^*\bigr)\bPsi_1,\\
0&=\bigl((\partial^*+iq\Phi)(\partial+iq\Phi^*)+m^2\bigr)\bPsi_2\notag\\
&=\bigl(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2+iqF\bigr)\bPsi_2.
\end{align*}
Once the square is taken, the coupled Dirac system separates into two Klein--Gordon-type equations whose field terms differ only by the conjugation pattern fixed by the operator ordering.

\subsubsection{Gauge Transform of the Dirac System}
Let \(\lambda\in\R\) be a real scalar gauge field and define
\begin{equation*}
\Phi_\lambda=\Phi+\partial^*\lambda.
\end{equation*}
Because \(\lambda\) is real,
\begin{equation*}
\partial\lambda=(\Phi_\lambda-\Phi)^*.
\end{equation*}
Transform the two-block field by
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi=\exp(iq\lambda)\bPsi_\lambda.
\end{equation*}
Then the derivative of the phase factor is
\begin{align*}
\partial\bigl(\exp(iq\lambda)\bPsi_\lambda\bigr)
&=\exp(iq\lambda)\bigl(iq\,\partial\lambda+\partial\bigr)\bPsi_\lambda.
\end{align*}
Therefore,
\begin{align*}
0&=(\Wmat(i\partial-q\Phi^*)-m\I)\bPsi\notag\\
&=(\Wmat(i\partial-q\Phi^*)-m\I)\exp(iq\lambda)\bPsi_\lambda\notag\\
&=\exp(iq\lambda)(\Wmat(i\partial-q\,\partial\lambda-q\Phi^*)-m\I)\bPsi_\lambda\notag\\
&=\exp(iq\lambda)(\Wmat(i\partial-q(\Phi_\lambda-\Phi)^*-q\Phi^*)-m\I)\bPsi_\lambda\notag\\
&=\exp(iq\lambda)(\Wmat(i\partial-q\Phi_\lambda^*)-m\I)\bPsi_\lambda.
\end{align*}
Hence
\begin{equation*}
(\Wmat(i\partial-q\Phi_\lambda^*)-m\I)\bPsi_\lambda=0.
\end{equation*}
This is the gauge-covariance statement: the phase change of the Dirac field is exactly compensated by the shift of the potential, so the coupled Dirac equation keeps the same form in the transformed gauge.

\subsubsection{Dirac Equation Components}
For the component form of the coupled Dirac equation, write

\partial&:=\partial_t+\sigv\cdot\gradv,\\
\partial^*&:=\partial_t-\sigv\cdot\gradv,\\
\Phi&:=V+\mathbf A\cdot\sigv\in\R\oplus\R^3,\\
\Phi^*&:=V-\mathbf A\cdot\sigv,\\

\end{align*}
For the field term, use

\subsubsection{Low-Energy Spin-0 Limit}
For the minimally coupled scalar theory, use the covariant operators
\begin{equation*}
E=i\partial_t-qV,
\qquad
\mathbf P=-i\gradv-q\mathbf A.
\end{equation*}
Remove the fast rest-mass oscillation by writing
\begin{equation*}
\psi'=\exp(-imt)\psi.
\end{equation*}
Then
\begin{align*}
E^2\psi'&=\exp(-imt)(E+m)^2\psi,\\
\mathbf P^2\psi'&=\exp(-imt)\mathbf P^2\psi.
\end{align*}
Substituting into the minimally coupled Klein--Gordon equation gives
\begin{align*}
0&=(-E^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2)\psi'\notag\\
&=\exp(-imt)\bigl(-(E+m)^2+\mathbf P^2+m^2\bigr)\psi,
\end{align*}
hence
\begin{equation*}
0=(-E^2-2mE+\mathbf P^2)\psi.
\end{equation*}
For the free theory, or more generally when the operators in the iteration
commute, this gives
\begin{align*}
E&=\frac{\mathbf P^2-E^2}{2m},\\
&=\frac{\mathbf P^2}{2m}-\frac{\mathbf P^4}{8m^3}+\cdots.
\end{align*}
For noncommuting or time-dependent potentials the higher-order terms also
contain commutators; the leading low-energy relation remains
\begin{equation*}
E\psi\approx \frac{\mathbf P^2}{2m}\psi.
\end{equation*}
Solving for \(i\partial_t\psi\) yields the Schr\"odinger equation
\begin{equation*}
i\partial_t\psi
\approx\left(\frac{(-i\gradv-q\mathbf A)^2}{2m}+qV\right)\psi.
\end{equation*}
If \(\mathbf A=0\), this reduces to
\begin{equation*}
i\partial_t\psi
\approx\left(-\frac{\gradv^2}{2m}+qV\right)\psi.
\end{equation*}
The scalar low-energy limit is therefore already encoded in the relativistic Klein--Gordon theory.

\subsubsection{Low-Energy Spin-\texorpdfstring{$\frac12$}{1/2} and Pauli Limit}
Start from the minimal-left-ideal two-block field
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi'=
\begin{Bmatrix}
\bPsi_1'\\[2pt]
\bPsi_2'
\end{Bmatrix}
\end{equation*}
and the potential-coupled Dirac equation
\begin{equation*}
\bigl(\Wmat(i\partial-q\Phi^*)-m\I\bigr)\bPsi'=0.
\end{equation*}
Its two block equations are
\begin{align*}
(i\partial-q\Phi^*)\bPsi_2'-m\bPsi_1'&=0,\\
(i\partial^*-q\Phi)\bPsi_1'-m\bPsi_2'&=0.
\end{align*}
Introduce the covariant operators
\begin{equation*}
E:=i\partial_t-qV,
\qquad
\mathbf P:=-i\gradv-q\mathbf A.
\end{equation*}
The system is then
\begin{align*}
(E-\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)\bPsi_2'-m\bPsi_1'&=0,\\
(E+\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)\bPsi_1'-m\bPsi_2'&=0.
\end{align*}
Peel off the fast rest-mass oscillation with
\begin{equation*}
\bPsi_n'=\exp(-imt)\bPsi_n.
\end{equation*}
Since
\begin{equation*}
E\bPsi_n'=\exp(-imt)(E+m)\bPsi_n,
\end{equation*}
the reduced system becomes
\begin{align*}
(E+m-\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)\bPsi_2-m\bPsi_1&=0,\\
(E+m+\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)\bPsi_1-m\bPsi_2&=0.
\end{align*}
In the Weyl basis neither block is small at rest.  The energy-basis combinations
are instead
\begin{equation*}
\phi:=\frac{\bPsi_1+\bPsi_2}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
\chi:=\frac{\bPsi_2-\bPsi_1}{\sqrt2}.
\end{equation*}
Adding and subtracting the reduced equations gives the exact system
\begin{equation*}
E\phi=(\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)\chi,
\qquad
(E+2m)\chi=(\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)\phi.
\end{equation*}
Thus \(\phi\) is the large positive-energy field and \(\chi\) is suppressed
by one power of \(1/m\).  Formally,
\begin{align*}
\chi&=(E+2m)^{-1}(\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)\phi,\\
E\phi
&=(\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)(E+2m)^{-1}
  (\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)\phi\notag\\
&=\frac{(\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)^2}{2m}\phi+O(m^{-2}),
\end{align*}
where the expansion assumes energies small compared with \(2m\) and smooth
external fields on the Compton scale.

The magnetic term follows directly from the covariant-momentum commutator:
\begin{align*}
\com{P_m}{P_n}&=iq(\partial_mA_n-\partial_nA_m),\\
\mathbf P\times\mathbf P&=iq\,\mathbf B,\\
(\mathbf P\cdot\sigv)^2
&=\mathbf P^2+i(\mathbf P\times\mathbf P)\cdot\sigv\notag\\
&=\mathbf P^2-q\,\mathbf B\cdot\sigv.
\end{align*}
Consequently,
\begin{equation*}
E\phi
=\frac{\mathbf P^2-q\,\mathbf B\cdot\sigv}{2m}\phi
+O(m^{-2}),
\end{equation*}
and the Pauli equation is
\begin{equation*}
i\partial_t\phi
=\left(\frac{(-i\gradv-q\mathbf A)^2}{2m}+qV\right)\phi
-\frac{q}{2m}(\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)\phi+O(m^{-2}).
\end{equation*}
Electric-field, spin-orbit, and Darwin terms arise at the next order through
the noncommutation of \(E\) and \(\mathbf P\); they must not be obtained by
discarding the electric term in the second-order equation for a single Weyl
block.  The leading spin coupling is therefore a controlled consequence of
the Dirac system \cite{pauli1927,sakurai2017}.

\subsubsection{Spin Up and Spin Down}
Where \(\mathbf B\neq\mathbf0\), let its direction define the spin axis,
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf u:=\frac{\mathbf B}{\|\mathbf B\|},
\end{equation*}
and use the projector pair from Eq.\ \eqref{eq:projectors},
\begin{equation*}
\Pi(\pm)=\tfrac12(1\pm \mathbf u\cdot\sigv),
\qquad
(\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)\Pi(\pm)=\pm\Pi(\pm).
\end{equation*}
Decompose the Pauli state by
\begin{align*}
\phi(\pm)&:=\Pi(\pm)\phi,\\
(\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)\phi(\pm)&=\pm\phi(\pm),\\
\phi&=\phi(+)+\phi(-),
\end{align*}
so \(\phi(+)\) and \(\phi(-)\) are the spin-up and spin-down parts along
the field axis.  These left projectors preserve the fixed minimal-ideal
condition \(\phi=\phi\Pi_0\).

At leading order, write the Pauli equation as
\begin{align*}
\mathbf P&:=-i\gradv-q\mathbf A,\\
i\partial_t\phi
&=\left(\frac{\mathbf P^2}{2m}+qV\right)\phi
-\frac{q}{2m}\,\|\mathbf B\|(\mathbf u\cdot\sigv)\phi.
\end{align*}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation*}
i\partial_t\phi=H\phi,
\end{equation*}
with
\begin{equation*}
H:=\frac{\mathbf P^2}{2m}+qV-\frac{q}{2m}\,\|\mathbf B\|\,\mathbf u\cdot\sigv.
\end{equation*}
Applying \(\Pi(\pm)\) to the time evolution gives
\begin{align*}
i\partial_t\phi(\pm)
&=H\phi(\pm)
+\bigl(i\partial_t\Pi(\pm)+\com{\Pi(\pm)}{H}\bigr)\phi\notag\\
&=H\phi(\pm)
+\left(i\partial_t\Pi(\pm)
+\frac{\com{\Pi(\pm)}{\mathbf P^2}}{2m}\right)\phi.
\end{align*}
Here \(qV\) and the local field-axis term commute pointwise with
\(\Pi(\pm)\); spatial variation enters through the displayed kinetic
commutator.  This term, together with \(\partial_t\Pi(\pm)\), couples the two
local spin sectors.
If \(\mathbf B\) is uniform and static, then \(\Pi(\pm)\) is constant and
the coupling term vanishes, so the two spin sectors decouple. In that case
\begin{equation*}
(-\mathbf B\cdot\sigv)\phi(\pm)=\mp \|\mathbf B\|\,\phi(\pm),
\end{equation*}
and the Zeeman effect appears as
\begin{equation*}
i\partial_t\phi(\pm)
=\left(\frac{\mathbf P^2\mp q\|\mathbf B\|}{2m}+qV\right)\phi(\pm).
\end{equation*}
The field direction therefore selects a preferred axis, and the two projector parts evolve with opposite Zeeman shifts.

\subsubsection{Free Energy and Helicity Projector Checks}
For the free momentum-mode Hamiltonian in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:dirac-hamiltonian},
direct block multiplication gives
\begin{align*}
H_D^2
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
(\sigv\cdot\mathbf p)^2+m^2
&-m\sigv\cdot\mathbf p+m\sigv\cdot\mathbf p\\
-m\sigv\cdot\mathbf p+m\sigv\cdot\mathbf p
&(\sigv\cdot\mathbf p)^2+m^2
\end{pmatrix}\notag\\
&=(m^2+\mathbf p^2)\I
=E_{\mathbf p}^2\I.
\end{align*}
It follows immediately that
\begin{align*}
\Lmat(\epsilon)^2
&=\frac14\left(
\I+2\epsilon\frac{H_D}{E_{\mathbf p}}
+\frac{H_D^2}{E_{\mathbf p}^2}\right)
=\Lmat(\epsilon),\\
H_D\Lmat(\epsilon)&=\epsilon E_{\mathbf p}\Lmat(\epsilon).
\end{align*}
The complementary relations are
\begin{equation*}
\Lmat(+)\Lmat(-)=0,
\qquad
\Lmat(+)+\Lmat(-)=\I.
\end{equation*}

For \(\mathbf p\neq\mathbf0\), the helicity axis obeys
\begin{equation*}
(\sigv\cdot\mathbf p)\Pi(s)
=s\|\mathbf p\|\Pi(s),
\qquad
\com{\sigv\cdot\mathbf p}{\Pi(s)}=0.
\end{equation*}
The mass entries commute with every \(\Pi(s)\), so
\begin{equation*}
\com{H_D}{\Pi_h(s)}=0,
\qquad
\com{\Lmat(\epsilon)}{\Pi_h(s)}=0.
\end{equation*}
Thus the commuting product in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:energy-spin-projectors} is a
projector onto a simultaneous energy--helicity sector.

Finally, all entries of \(H_D\), \(\Lmat(\epsilon)\), and \(\Pi_h(s)\) act
from the left.  Hence, whenever a block satisfies the fixed-ideal condition
\(\bPsi_n=\bPsi_n\Pi_0\), every projected block satisfies it as well, since
\begin{equation*}
(O\bPsi_n)\Pi_0=O(\bPsi_n\Pi_0)=O\bPsi_n
\end{equation*}
for each such left-acting block entry \(O\).  The energy and helicity
decomposition therefore introduces no second spinor column and preserves the
one-Dirac-field carrier selected in Eq.\ \eqref{eq:minimal-ideal}.
