\begin{abstract}
This document develops a three-generator sigma algebra and its complex
paravectors, then applies them to relativity, electromagnetism, and quantum
mechanics.  Natural units $\hbar=c=1$, rationalized electromagnetic units,
and signature $(+,-,-,-)$ are used.
\end{abstract}

\section{The algebra at a glance}
\label{sec:algebra}

The generators $\sigma_n$, $n=1,2,3$, obey ordered multiplication rules.
Their products generally do not commute.  The panel gives their defining
relations and the general complex element developed below.

\begin{center}
\setlength{\fboxsep}{1.2em}
\fcolorbox{MidnightBlue}{MidnightBlue!4}{%
\begin{minipage}{0.88\textwidth}
\begin{align*}
 \sigma_n^2&=1,
 &\sigma_n\sigma_m&=-\sigma_m\sigma_n\quad(n\ne m),\\
 \ii&=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3,
 &\ii^2&=-1,\\
 \bsig&=\{\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3\},
 &\ii\bsig&=\{\sigma_2\sigma_3,\sigma_3\sigma_1,\sigma_1\sigma_2\},\\
 Z&=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig,
 &a,b&\in\R,\quad \bA,\bB\in\R^3.
\end{align*}
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

Ordinary three-vector operations use
\begin{align*}
 \bA\cdot\bB&=A_1B_1+A_2B_2+A_3B_3,\\
 \bA\times\bB&=\{A_2B_3-A_3B_2,
 A_3B_1-A_1B_3,A_1B_2-A_2B_1\},\\
 \bA^2&:=\bA\cdot\bA.
\end{align*}
Thus the square of a vector always denotes its self dot product.
The elementary Cartesian vectors used below are the real vectors
\[
 \bm e_1=\{1,0,0\},\qquad
 \bm e_2=\{0,1,0\},\qquad
 \bm e_3=\{0,0,1\}.
\]
Thus $\bm e_k\cdot\bsig=\sigma_k$; the scalar basis element is $1$.
Unindexed $e$ denotes only Euler's number.

The set $\{1,\bsig,\ii\bsig,\ii\}$ is a complete basis, displayed by type as
\[
  \underbrace{1}_{\text{scalar}},\qquad
  \underbrace{\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3}_{\text{vectors}},\qquad
  \underbrace{\ii\sigma_1,\ii\sigma_2,\ii\sigma_3}_{\text{bivectors}},\qquad
  \underbrace{\ii}_{\text{pseudoscalar}}.
\]
Anticommutation gives
$\ii^2=-\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2\sigma_3^2=-1$.  The pseudoscalar is central:
moving $\sigma_n$ through the other two generators produces two sign
changes, so
\begin{equation*}
 \ii\sigma_n=\sigma_n\ii.
\end{equation*}
Thus its order of multiplication does not matter.

For algebra elements $X,Y$, the commutator is
\begin{gather*}
 [X,Y]:=XY-YX\\
 [Y,X]=-[X,Y].
\end{gather*}
Thus $[X,Y]=0$ exactly when $X$ and $Y$ commute.
For $n,m,k\in\{1,2,3\}$, define the Kronecker delta by
\begin{equation*}
 \delta_{nm}:=
 \begin{cases}
  1,&n=m,\\
  0,&n\ne m,
 \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
and the Levi--Civita symbol by
\begin{equation*}
 \epsilon_{nmk}:=
 \begin{cases}
  +1,&(n,m,k)\text{ is an even permutation of }(1,2,3),\\
  -1,&(n,m,k)\text{ is an odd permutation of }(1,2,3),\\
  0,&\text{two or more indices are equal}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
Equation~\eqref{eq:sigma-product} gives the ordered multiplication rule
for $n,m\in\{1,2,3\}$:
\begin{align}
  \sigma_n\sigma_m=\delta_{nm}
  +\sum_{k=1}^{3}\epsilon_{nmk}\,\ii\sigma_k
 \label{eq:sigma-product}\\
  [\sigma_n,\sigma_m]
  =2\sum_{k=1}^{3}\epsilon_{nmk}\,\ii\sigma_k. \notag
\end{align}
\section{Hermitian and sigma-conjugate operators}
\label{sec:conjugate-operators}

Define the two operations by
\begin{align*}
 (\,\cdot\,)^{\mathsf H}
 &:=\text{reverse the order of every product of sigma generators},\\
 (\,\cdot\,)^*
 &:=\text{replace every $\sigma_n$ by $-\sigma_n$ without changing product order}.
\end{align*}
Thus, for two generators,
\begin{align*}
 (\sigma_m\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}&=\sigma_n\sigma_m,\\
 (\sigma_m\sigma_n)^*&=(-\sigma_m)(-\sigma_n).
\end{align*}
Both operations act term by term on sums.  Their actions on the four basis
types are
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c c c c c}
  \toprule
  type & scalar & vector & bivector & pseudoscalar\\
  representative & $1$ & $\sigma_n$ & $\ii\sigma_n$ & $\ii$\\
  \midrule
  $(\cdot)^*$ & $+$ & $-$ & $+$ & $-$\\
  $(\cdot)^{\mathsf H}$ & $+$ & $+$ & $-$ & $-$\\
  $(\cdot)^{*\mathsf H}$ & $+$ & $-$ & $-$ & $+$\\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For example, on a bivector the signs follow directly:
\begin{align*}
 (\ii\sigma_n)^*&=\ii^*\sigma_n^*=\ii\sigma_n,\\
 (\ii\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}
 &=\sigma_n^{\mathsf H}\ii^{\mathsf H}=-\ii\sigma_n.
\end{align*}
For arbitrary algebra elements $Z,Z'$, the definitions give the product
laws \eqref{eq:hermitian-product} and \eqref{eq:sigma-conjugate-product}:
\begin{align}
 (ZZ')^{\mathsf H}&=Z'^{\mathsf H}Z^{\mathsf H},
 \label{eq:hermitian-product}\\
 (ZZ')^*&=Z^*Z'^*.
 \label{eq:sigma-conjugate-product}
\end{align}
The operations commute with each other: $Z^{*\mathsf H}=Z^{\mathsf H*}$.
Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix} reproduces this as Hermitian conjugation
in the Pauli-matrix representation.

\section{Paravectors}
\label{sec:paravectors}

\subsection{Core operations}
\label{sec:algebra-operations}

Let $a,b\in\R$ and $\bA,\bB\in\R^3$.  The real paravectors
$X=a+\bA\cdot\bsig$ and $Y=b+\bB\cdot\bsig$ form the general complex
paravector in \eqref{eq:paravector}:
\begin{equation}
 Z=X+\ii Y=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\ii\bB)\cdot\bsig
   =S+\bV\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:paravector}
\end{equation}
Here $S=a+b\ii\in\C$ and $\bV=\bA+\ii\bB\in\C^3$ are its
complex scalar and vector coefficients.  Equations~\eqref{eq:scalar-extraction}
and \eqref{eq:vector-extraction} define their extraction functions:
\begin{align}
 \scpart Z&:=S,
 \label{eq:scalar-extraction}\\
 \vecpart Z&:=\bV.
 \label{eq:vector-extraction}
\end{align}
This notation applies throughout the algebra unless a real specialization
is stated.  On the coefficients, both conjugations give
$S^*=S^{\mathsf H}=a-b\ii$ and
$\bV^*=\bV^{\mathsf H}=\bA-\ii\bB$; on the paravector,
\eqref{eq:algebra-star} and \eqref{eq:algebra-H} differ in the vector sign:
\begin{align}
 Z^*&=S^*-\bV^*\cdot\bsig
 =(a-b\ii)+(-\bA+\ii\bB)\cdot\bsig,
 \label{eq:algebra-star}\\
 Z^{\mathsf H}&=S^*+\bV^*\cdot\bsig
 =(a-b\ii)+(\bA-\ii\bB)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:algebra-H}
\end{align}
Their composition defines the adjugate in \eqref{eq:adjugate}:
\begin{equation}
 \adj Z:=Z^{*\mathsf H}=S-\bV\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:adjugate}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:trace-scalar} identifies trace with twice the scalar part:
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{tr}(Z):=Z+\adj Z=2\scpart Z=2S.
 \label{eq:trace-scalar}
\end{equation}
The determinant in \eqref{eq:det} is a complex scalar:
\begin{equation}
 \det(Z):=\adj Z\,Z=Z\adj Z=S^2-\bV^2.
 \label{eq:det}
\end{equation}
For complex $Z'$, \eqref{eq:adjugate-product} reverses product order.
This gives determinant multiplicativity in
\eqref{eq:det-product}:
\begin{align}
 \adj{ZZ'}&=\adj{Z'}\adj Z,
 \label{eq:adjugate-product}\\
 \det(ZZ')&=\adj{Z'}(\adj Z\,Z)Z'
           =\det(Z)\det(Z').
 \label{eq:det-product}
\end{align}
Since the determinants are scalars, the determinant of any product is
unchanged by reordering its factors.  For a complex scalar $c$,
\eqref{eq:det-scalar-scaling} gives the scaling law:
\begin{align}
 \adj{cZ}&=c\adj Z, \notag\\
 \det(cZ)&=c^2\det(Z).
 \label{eq:det-scalar-scaling}
\end{align}
When $\det(Z)\ne0$, equation~\eqref{eq:inverse} gives the inverse:
\begin{equation}
 Z^{-1}=\frac{\adj Z}{\det(Z)}
       =\frac{S-\bV\cdot\bsig}{S^2-\bV^2}.
 \label{eq:inverse}
\end{equation}
An invertible paravector $U$ is called a \emph{unit}, as in
\eqref{eq:unit-paravector}:
\begin{equation}
 UU^{-1}=U^{-1}U=1.
 \label{eq:unit-paravector}
\end{equation}
It is \emph{unitary} when it satisfies \eqref{eq:unitary-transform}:
\begin{equation}
 U^{\mathsf H}U=1.
 \label{eq:unitary-transform}
\end{equation}
Then $U^{\mathsf H}=U^{-1}$ and $UU^{\mathsf H}=1$.
Invertibility, determinant one, and Hermitian unitarity are distinct
conditions; their physical roles are compared in
Section~\ref{sec:preserved-quantities}.

For $Z'=S'+\bV'\cdot\bsig$, with complex coefficients, division is
order-sensitive.  An unambiguous quotient exists when $Z$ is invertible
and the factors commute, as specified in \eqref{eq:commuting-quotient}:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{Z'}{Z}:=Z'Z^{-1}=Z^{-1}Z'
 \qquad\text{if }\bV\times\bV'=0.
 \label{eq:commuting-quotient}
\end{equation}
Differentiating $Z^{-1}Z=1$ gives the inverse rule
\eqref{eq:inverse-differential}:
\begin{equation}
 \dd(Z^{-1})=-Z^{-1}(\dd Z)Z^{-1}.
 \label{eq:inverse-differential}
\end{equation}
Scalar part obeys the cyclic laws \eqref{eq:scalar-cyclic} and
\eqref{eq:scalar-cyclic-three} for complex $Z,Z',Z''$:
\begin{align}
 \scpart{ZZ'}&=\scpart{Z'Z},
 \label{eq:scalar-cyclic}\\
 \scpart{ZZ'Z''}&=\scpart{Z'Z''Z}=\scpart{Z''ZZ'}.
 \label{eq:scalar-cyclic-three}
\end{align}
Multiplying these identities by $2$ gives trace cyclicity.  Their similarity
invariants are derived in Section~\ref{sec:preserved-quantities}.

The real and imaginary extractions \eqref{eq:algebra-re} and
\eqref{eq:algebra-im} use the Hermitian operation:
\begin{align}
 \repart Z&:=\tfrac12(Z+Z^{\mathsf H})=X,
 \label{eq:algebra-re}\\
 \impart Z&:=\frac{Z-Z^{\mathsf H}}{2\ii}=Y.
 \label{eq:algebra-im}
\end{align}
Adjugation evaluates the extractions explicitly in
\eqref{eq:scalar-from-adjugate} and \eqref{eq:vector-from-adjugate}:
\begin{align}
 \scpart Z&=\tfrac12(Z+\adj Z)=S,
 \label{eq:scalar-from-adjugate}\\
 (\vecpart Z)\cdot\bsig&=\tfrac12(Z-\adj Z)=\bV\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:vector-from-adjugate}
\end{align}
The functions $\operatorname{re}$ and $\operatorname{im}$ also act on
complex coefficients.  Composing extractions recovers all four real parts:
\begin{align*}
 a&=\repart S=\scpart{\repart Z},\\
 b&=\impart S=\scpart{\impart Z},\\
 \bA&=\repart{\bV}=\vecpart{\repart Z},\\
 \bB&=\impart{\bV}=\vecpart{\impart Z}.
\end{align*}
Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix} reproduces these intrinsic operations with Pauli
matrices.

\subsection{Products and quadratic forms}

Here $\bA,\bB\in\R^3$ are real coefficient vectors.
The sigma rule~\eqref{eq:sigma-product} gives the dot--cross identity
\eqref{eq:vector-product}:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)(\bB\cdot\bsig)
 &=\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sum_{m=1}^{3}
   A_nB_m\sigma_n\sigma_m\\
 &=\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sum_{m=1}^{3}A_nB_m\delta_{nm}
   +\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sum_{m=1}^{3}\sum_{k=1}^{3}
   A_nB_m\epsilon_{nmk}\ii\sigma_k\\
 &=\bA\cdot\bB+\ii(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:vector-product}
\end{equation}
Adding the product with $\bA$ and $\bB$ interchanged gives
\begin{equation*}
 2(\bA\cdot\bB)
 =(\bA\cdot\bsig)(\bB\cdot\bsig)
 +(\bB\cdot\bsig)(\bA\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation*}
Consequently, for every nonnegative integer $n$,
\begin{align*}
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)^2&=\bA^2,\\
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)^{2n}&=(\bA^2)^n,\\
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)^{2n+1}&=(\bA^2)^n\bA\cdot\bsig.
\end{align*}
Also,
\begin{equation*}
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)\bsig=\bA-\ii\bA\times\bsig.
\end{equation*}
Subtracting the two orders gives \eqref{eq:vector-commutator}:
\begin{equation}
 [\bA\cdot\bsig,\bB\cdot\bsig]
 =2\ii(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:vector-commutator}
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{align*}
 [\bA\cdot\bsig,\bsig]&=-2\ii\bA\times\bsig,\\
 [\bA\cdot\ii\bsig,\bsig]&=2\bA\times\bsig,\\
 [\bA\cdot\bsig,\bsig]\cdot\bB
 &=2\ii(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align*}

\subsection{Quadratic identity and eigenvalues}

Recall $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, with $S\in\C$ and $\bV\in\C^3$.
Since $Z-S=\bV\cdot\bsig$ and
$(\bV\cdot\bsig)^2=\bV^2$,
\begin{align*}
 (Z-S)^2&=\bV^2,\\
 Z^2-2SZ+(S^2-\bV^2)&=0.
\end{align*}
Using $\operatorname{tr}(Z)=2S$ and
$\det(Z)=S^2-\bV^2$ gives the trace--determinant identity \eqref{eq:minimal}:
\begin{equation}
 Z^2-\operatorname{tr}(Z)Z+\det(Z)=0.
  \label{eq:minimal}
\end{equation}
Solving \eqref{eq:minimal} for a scalar eigenvalue gives
\eqref{eq:eigenvalues}:
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_\pm
 =\frac{\operatorname{tr}(Z)
 \pm\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(Z)^2-4\det(Z)}}{2}
 =S\pm\sqrt{\bV^2}.
 \label{eq:eigenvalues}
\end{equation}
Because $\bV$ is generally complex, $\bV^2=\bV\cdot\bV$ and its square
root may also be complex.
The quadratic identity therefore factors as
\begin{equation*}
 (Z-\lambda_-)(Z-\lambda_+)=0.
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Real products}

For real $X=a+\bA\cdot\bsig$ and $Y=b+\bB\cdot\bsig$, with
$a,b\in\R$ and $\bA,\bB\in\R^3$, equation~\eqref{eq:vector-product} gives the real product
\eqref{eq:real-paravector-product} and its quadratic cases:
\begin{align}
 XY&=(ab+\bA\cdot\bB)
 +(a\bB+b\bA+\ii\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig,
 \label{eq:real-paravector-product}\\
 [X,Y]&=2\ii(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig, \notag\\
 X^2&=(a^2+\bA^2)+2a\bA\cdot\bsig. \notag
\end{align}
Their scalar and vector parts are read directly from these equations.
The real quadratic identity is \eqref{eq:minimal} with $S=a$.
If $\bA\times\bB=0$, then $XY=YX$; when $\det(X)\ne0$, also
$X^{-1}Y=YX^{-1}$.

\subsection{Complex products}

Let $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$ and $Z'=S'+\bV'\cdot\bsig$, where
$S,S'\in\C$ and $\bV,\bV'\in\C^3$.  Complex bilinearity extends
\eqref{eq:vector-product} to the general product \eqref{eq:para-product}:
\begin{equation}
 ZZ'=(SS'+\bV\cdot\bV')+
 \bigl(S\bV'+S'\bV+\ii\bV\times\bV'\bigr)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:para-product}
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{align*}
 \scpart{ZZ'}&=SS'+\bV\cdot\bV',\\
 \vecpart{ZZ'}&=S\bV'+S'\bV+\ii\bV\times\bV'.
\end{align*}
Equivalently, write $Z=X+\ii Y$ and $Z'=X'+\ii Y'$ with all four
factors real.  Their products need not be real, but distributivity gives
\begin{equation*}
 ZZ'=(XX'-YY')+\ii(XY'+YX').
\end{equation*}

Write $Z=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\ii\bB)\cdot\bsig$ with real
$a,b,\bA,\bB$, and use primes for the second paravector's real components.  The four
real components of $ZZ'$ are
\begin{align*}
 \text{real scalar: }&aa'-bb'+\bA\cdot\bA'-\bB\cdot\bB',\\
 \text{imaginary scalar: }&ab'+ba'+\bA\cdot\bB'+\bB\cdot\bA',\\
 \text{real vector: }&a\bA'-b\bB'+a'\bA-b'\bB
                    -\bA\times\bB'-\bB\times\bA',\\
 \text{imaginary vector: }&a\bB'+b\bA'+a'\bB+b'\bA
                    +\bA\times\bA'-\bB\times\bB'.
\end{align*}
Specializing \eqref{eq:para-product} gives the quadratic products:
\begin{align}
 Z^2&=(a^2-b^2+\bA^2-\bB^2)
 +2\ii(ab+\bA\cdot\bB) \notag\\
 &\quad+2(a\bA-b\bB)\cdot\bsig
 +2\ii(a\bB+b\bA)\cdot\bsig, \notag\\
 ZZ^*&=(a^2+b^2-\bA^2-\bB^2)
 -2(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig
 +2\ii(a\bB-b\bA)\cdot\bsig,
 \label{eq:star-product-left}\\
 Z^*Z&=(a^2+b^2-\bA^2-\bB^2)
 +2(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig
 +2\ii(a\bB-b\bA)\cdot\bsig,
 \label{eq:star-product-right}\\
 ZZ^{\mathsf H}&=a^2+b^2+\bA^2+\bB^2
 +2(a\bA+b\bB+\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig,
 \label{eq:hermitian-product-left}\\
 Z^{\mathsf H}Z&=a^2+b^2+\bA^2+\bB^2
 +2(a\bA+b\bB-\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig,
 \label{eq:hermitian-product-right}\\
 ZZ^{*\mathsf H}&=\det(Z)
 =a^2-b^2-\bA^2+\bB^2
 +2\ii(ab-\bA\cdot\bB). \notag
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:star-product-left} and \eqref{eq:star-product-right}
show $ZZ^*=Z^*Z$ exactly when $\bA\times\bB=0$.  Equations
\eqref{eq:hermitian-product-left} and \eqref{eq:hermitian-product-right}
give the same condition for $ZZ^{\mathsf H}=Z^{\mathsf H}Z$.

\subsubsection{Norm and determinant}
For the complex coefficients $S=a+b\ii$ and $\bV=\bA+\ii\bB$, define
\begin{align*}
 \lVert S\rVert^2&:=SS^*=a^2+b^2,\\
 \lVert\bV\rVert^2&:=\bV\cdot\bV^*=\bA^2+\bB^2.
\end{align*}
The positive paravector norm is defined by \eqref{eq:coefficient-norm}:
\begin{equation}
 \lVert Z\rVert^2:=\lVert S\rVert^2+\lVert\bV\rVert^2.
 \label{eq:coefficient-norm}
\end{equation}
The quadratic products and cyclicity give the Hermitian form
\eqref{eq:hermitian-norm}:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \lVert Z\rVert^2&=\scpart{Z^{\mathsf H}Z}
 =\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(Z^{\mathsf H}Z)
 =\scpart{ZZ^{\mathsf H}}\\
 &=\scpart{Z^*Z^{*\mathsf H}}
 =\scpart{Z^{*\mathsf H}Z^*}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:hermitian-norm}
\end{equation}
For complex coefficients, $S^2$ and $\bV^2$ generally differ from these
positive norms.  Also $\det(Z)$ may be complex.  For a complex scalar
$c=r e^{\ii\theta}$, with real $r\ge0$ and $\theta$, scalar scaling gives
\begin{align}
 \det(cZ)&=r^2e^{2\ii\theta}\det(Z), \notag\\
 \lVert cZ\rVert^2&=\lVert c\rVert^2\lVert Z\rVert^2
 =r^2\lVert Z\rVert^2.
 \label{eq:norm-scalar-scaling}
\end{align}
The determinant retains the scalar phase by \eqref{eq:det-scalar-scaling};
the norm loses it by \eqref{eq:norm-scalar-scaling}.

\subsection{Projection operators}
\label{sec:algebra-projectors}

For a real unit vector $\bu$, equation~\eqref{eq:projectors} defines
the two orthogonal projectors:
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_\pm:=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig).
 \label{eq:projectors}
\end{equation}
Since $(\bu\cdot\bsig)^2=1$,
\begin{align*}
 \Pi_\pm^2&=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_+\Pi_-&=0,\\
 \Pi_++\Pi_-&=1,\\
 \Pi_\pm^{\mathsf H}&=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_\pm^*&=\Pi_\mp,\\
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)\Pi_\pm&=\pm \Pi_\pm.
\end{align*}
Thus $\bu\cdot\bsig=\Pi_+-\Pi_-$.  For a second real unit vector $\bu'$,
\begin{align*}
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)(\bu'\cdot\bsig)
 &=\bu\cdot\bu'+\ii(\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 [\bu\cdot\bsig,\bu'\cdot\bsig]
 &=2\ii(\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)(\bu'\cdot\bsig)(\bu\cdot\bsig)
 &=\left(2(\bu\cdot\bu')\bu-\bu'\right)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align*}
Define $\bu'_{\parallel}=(\bu'\cdot\bu)\bu$ and
$\bu'_{\perp}=\bu'-\bu'_{\parallel}$.  Then
\begin{align*}
 \Pi_\pm(\bu'\cdot\bsig)\Pi_\pm
 &=\pm(\bu\cdot\bu')\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_\pm(\bu'\cdot\bsig)\Pi_\mp
 &=\tfrac12(\bu'_{\perp}\pm\ii(\bu\times\bu'))\cdot\bsig\,
 \Pi_\mp.
\end{align*}
For $\Pi_\pm'=(1\pm\bu'\cdot\bsig)/2$, all four products are
\begin{align*}
 \Pi_\pm\Pi_\pm'&=\tfrac14(1+\bu\cdot\bu')
 +\tfrac14(\pm\bu\pm\bu'+\ii\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 \Pi_\pm\Pi_\mp'&=\tfrac14(1-\bu\cdot\bu')
 +\tfrac14(\pm\bu\mp\bu'-\ii\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig.
\end{align*}

For arbitrary real vector increments $\dd\bu$ and $\dd\bu'$, the same
dot--cross decomposition gives the geometric determinant identity
\begin{equation}
 \Det{(\dd\bu\cdot\bsig)(\dd\bu'\cdot\bsig)}
 =(\dd\bu\cdot\dd\bu')^2
 +\frac14\left\lVert
 [\dd\bu\cdot\bsig,\dd\bu'\cdot\bsig]\right\rVert^2.
 \label{eq:lagrange-determinant}
\end{equation}
Dropping the nonnegative dot-product square in
\eqref{eq:lagrange-determinant} gives \eqref{eq:lagrange-inequality}:
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt{\Det{(\dd\bu\cdot\bsig)(\dd\bu'\cdot\bsig)}}
 \geq\frac12\left\lVert
 [\dd\bu\cdot\bsig,\dd\bu'\cdot\bsig]\right\rVert.
 \label{eq:lagrange-inequality}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:lagrange-determinant} is Lagrange's dot--cross identity;
\eqref{eq:lagrange-inequality} is a geometric bound, not a quantum uncertainty law.

\subsubsection{Ladder operators}
\label{sec:ladder-operators}

Along the third axis, equation~\eqref{eq:ladder-operators} defines complex
ladder paravectors; the real projectors specialize \eqref{eq:projectors}:
\begin{align}
 Z_\pm&=\tfrac12(\sigma_1\pm\ii\sigma_2)
 \label{eq:ladder-operators}\\
 \Pi_\pm&=\tfrac12(1\pm\sigma_3). \notag
\end{align}
Then
\begin{align*}
 [\sigma_3,Z_\pm]&=\pm2Z_\pm,\\
 Z_\pm^2&=0,\\
 Z_\pm Z_\mp&=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \sigma_1&=Z_++Z_-,\\
 \sigma_2&=(Z_+-Z_-)/\ii,\\
 \sigma_3&=\Pi_+-\Pi_-.
\end{align*}
The two signs give the maps between projected sectors:
\begin{align*}
 Z_\pm\Pi_\pm&=0,& Z_\pm\Pi_\mp&=Z_\pm,\\
 \Pi_\pm Z_\pm&=Z_\pm,& \Pi_\mp Z_\pm&=0.
\end{align*}

\subsection{Functions of paravectors}
\label{sec:spectral}

\subsubsection{Spectral rule}
Let $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, with $S\in\C$, $\bV\in\C^3$, and
$\bV^2\ne0$.  Define the complex scalars $w,\lambda_\pm$, complex vector $\bu$, and
paravector projectors $\Pi_\pm$ by
\begin{align*}
 w&:=+\sqrt{\bV^2},& \lambda_\pm&:=S\pm w,\\
 \bu&:=\bV/w,& \Pi_\pm&:=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig).
\end{align*}
The root is the principal scalar root.  The normalization is bilinear:
$\bu^2=1$, while the Hermitian norm $\lVert\bu\rVert^2$ need not be $1$.
The identity $(\bu\cdot\bsig)^2=1$ gives
\begin{align*}
 \Pi_\pm^2&=\tfrac14(1\pm2\bu\cdot\bsig+1)=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_+\Pi_-&=\tfrac14(1-(\bu\cdot\bsig)^2)=0,\\
 \Pi_++\Pi_-&=1,\\
 Z&=S(\Pi_++\Pi_-)+w(\Pi_+-\Pi_-)
   =\lambda_+\Pi_++\lambda_-\Pi_-.
\end{align*}
These projectors need not be Hermitian.  Orthogonality gives, for every
nonnegative integer $n$,
\[
 Z^n=\lambda_+^n\Pi_++\lambda_-^n\Pi_-
 =\tfrac12(\lambda_+^n+\lambda_-^n)
 +\tfrac12(\lambda_+^n-\lambda_-^n)\bu\cdot\bsig.
\]
For a convergent scalar power series $f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_nz^n$,
with $c_n\in\C$, inserting these powers into $f(Z)=\sum_n c_nZ^n$
gives the spectral rule~\eqref{eq:complex-function-projectors}:
\begin{equation}
 f(Z)=f(\lambda_+)\Pi_++f(\lambda_-)\Pi_-.
 \label{eq:complex-function-projectors}
\end{equation}
The same rule defines chosen analytic branches at the two eigenvalues.
Its proof uses only complementary idempotents and therefore applies to
any decomposition with these projector identities.

Adjugation exchanges the eigenvalues, so
\begin{align*}
 \adj Z&=\lambda_-\Pi_++\lambda_+\Pi_-,\\
 \det(Z)&=\lambda_+\lambda_-,\\
 Z^{-1}&=\frac{\adj Z}{\det(Z)}
       =\lambda_+^{-1}\Pi_++\lambda_-^{-1}\Pi_-.
\end{align*}
The inverse requires both eigenvalues to be nonzero.  Independently choosing
the signs of their principal square roots gives
\eqref{eq:paravector-square-root}:
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt Z=\pm\sqrt{\lambda_+}\Pi_+\pm\sqrt{\lambda_-}\Pi_-.
 \label{eq:paravector-square-root}
\end{equation}
There are four distinct roots when both eigenvalues are nonzero, and two
when one vanishes.  Changing $w$ to $-w$ exchanges the eigenvalue--projector
pairs and leaves the function unchanged when its branch choices are
exchanged with them.

\subsubsection{Real paravectors and direct square roots}
For real $X=a+\bA\cdot\bsig$, with $a\in\R$, $\bA\in\R^3$, and
$\bA\ne0$, the spectral quantities above reduce to
\[
 w=\lVert\bA\rVert,\qquad
 \bu=\bA/\lVert\bA\rVert,\qquad
 \lambda_\pm=a\pm\lVert\bA\rVert.
\]
The projectors are now Hermitian.  In particular, for a real unit $\bu$,
\begin{align*}
 f(\bu\cdot\bsig)
 &=f(1)\Pi_++f(-1)\Pi_-\\
 &=\tfrac12(f(1)+f(-1))
  +\tfrac12(f(1)-f(-1))\bu\cdot\bsig,\\
 \sqrt{\bu\cdot\bsig}&=\pm\Pi_+\pm\ii\Pi_-.
\end{align*}
The two root signs are independent.  For a fixed analytic branch,
$f(X)\to f(a)$ as $\bA\to0$; independent root choices require the
scalar case below.

A direct component derivation also determines all \emph{real} roots.
Let $Y=b+\bB\cdot\bsig$ be real, with $b\in\R$, $\bB\in\R^3$,
and seek real $X=a+\bA\cdot\bsig$ such that $X^2=Y$.  Equating parts gives
\begin{align*}
 a^2+\bA^2&=b,\\
 2a\bA&=\bB.
\end{align*}
If $\bB\ne0$, then $a\ne0$ and $\bA=\bB/(2a)$.  Substitution gives
\begin{align}
 a^4-ba^2+\tfrac14\bB^2&=0, \notag\\
 a^2&=\tfrac12\left(b\pm\sqrt{b^2-\bB^2}\right).
 \label{eq:real-square-root-scalar}
\end{align}
For each nonnegative $a^2$ in \eqref{eq:real-square-root-scalar}, take both
scalar roots $a$ and set
$\bA=\bB/(2a)$; then $\bA^2=b-a^2$.  There are four real roots when
$b>\lVert\bB\rVert$, two when $b=\lVert\bB\rVert>0$, and none when
$b<\lVert\bB\rVert$.

If $\bB=0$, the equations instead give the scalar roots
$X=\pm\sqrt b$ and the pure-vector roots $X=\bA\cdot\bsig$ with
$\bA^2=b$ for $b>0$.  For $b=0$ only $X=0$ is real; for $b<0$ there
is no real root.

\subsubsection{Coalescing eigenvalues}
For complex $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$ with $\bV^2=0$, define
$N=\bV\cdot\bsig$, so $N^2=0$.  A scalar function analytic at $S$
has the terminating Taylor expansion \eqref{eq:coalescing-function}:
\begin{equation}
 f(Z)=f(S)+f'(S)N.
 \label{eq:coalescing-function}
\end{equation}
For $S\ne0$, the chosen square root and logarithm are
\eqref{eq:coalescing-square-root} and \eqref{eq:coalescing-logarithm}:
\begin{align}
 \sqrt Z&=r+\frac{N}{2r},
 \label{eq:coalescing-square-root}\\
 \log Z&=\log S+\frac{N}{S}.
 \label{eq:coalescing-logarithm}
\end{align}
Here $r\in\C$ is a chosen scalar root of $S$ ($r^2=S$); use a consistent
logarithm branch.  A nonzero $N$ with $S=0$ has no square
root: such a root would have only zero eigenvalues, and its quadratic
identity would force its square to vanish.  It has no logarithm because
it is not invertible.  If $N=0$, a fixed analytic
branch gives $f(S)$; non-scalar square roots of a scalar must instead be
found from the component equations $a^2+\bA^2=S$, $2a\bA=0$, now allowing
$a$ and $\bA$ to be complex.  Independent branch choices need not remain
finite as distinct eigenvalues coalesce.

\subsubsection{Exponential: projection and series derivations}
For complex $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$ with $\bV^2\ne0$, recall
$w=+\sqrt{\bV^2}$, $\bu=\bV/w$, and $\lambda_\pm=S\pm w$.
The spectral rule~\eqref{eq:complex-function-projectors} gives
\begin{align*}
 \exp(Z)
 &=\tfrac12e^{S+w}(1+\bu\cdot\bsig)
  +\tfrac12e^{S-w}(1-\bu\cdot\bsig)\\
 &=e^S\left(\frac{e^w+e^{-w}}2
      +\frac{e^w-e^{-w}}2\bu\cdot\bsig\right).
\end{align*}
Independently, $S$ commutes with the vector part and
$(w\bu\cdot\bsig)^{2n}=w^{2n}$,
$(w\bu\cdot\bsig)^{2n+1}=w^{2n+1}\bu\cdot\bsig$.  Separating the
defining series therefore gives
\begin{align*}
 \exp(Z)&=e^S\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
      \frac{(w\bu\cdot\bsig)^n}{n!}\\
 &=e^S\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{2n}}{(2n)!}
       +\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}\bu\cdot\bsig\right).
\end{align*}
Both derivations give \eqref{eq:paravector-exponential}:
\begin{equation}
 \exp(Z)=e^S(\cosh w+\sinh w\,\bu\cdot\bsig).
 \label{eq:paravector-exponential}
\end{equation}
This is independent of the choice $w$ or $-w$: $\cosh$ is even, while
both $\sinh w$ and $\bu$ change sign.  When $\bV^2=0$,
\eqref{eq:coalescing-function} instead gives
\[
 \exp(S+\bV\cdot\bsig)=e^S(1+\bV\cdot\bsig).
\]
The defining series also gives
\begin{align*}
 \exp(Z^*)&=\exp(Z)^*,\\
 \exp(Z^{\mathsf H})&=\exp(Z)^{\mathsf H}.
\end{align*}

For the real components $a,b,\bA,\bB$ of
$Z=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\ii\bB)\cdot\bsig$, write
\[
 w=+\sqrt{\bA^2-\bB^2+2\ii\bA\cdot\bB}.
\]
Substituting $\bu=(\bA+\ii\bB)/w$ into
\eqref{eq:paravector-exponential} gives the component form
\eqref{eq:exponential-components}:
\begin{equation}
 \exp(Z)=e^a(\cos b+\ii\sin b)
 \left(\cosh w+\frac{\sinh w}{w}(\bA+\ii\bB)\cdot\bsig\right).
 \label{eq:exponential-components}
\end{equation}
The real and imaginary specializations of \eqref{eq:exponential-components} are
\begin{align}
 \exp(a+\bA\cdot\bsig)
 &=e^a\left(\cosh\lVert\bA\rVert
  +\frac{\sinh\lVert\bA\rVert}{\lVert\bA\rVert}\bA\cdot\bsig\right),
 \label{eq:real-exponential}\\
 \exp\!\left(\ii(b+\bB\cdot\bsig)\right)
 &=(\cos b+\ii\sin b)\left(\cos\lVert\bB\rVert
  +\ii\frac{\sin\lVert\bB\rVert}{\lVert\bB\rVert}\bB\cdot\bsig\right).
 \label{eq:imaginary-exponential}
\end{align}
Setting $a=0$ in \eqref{eq:real-exponential} or $b=0$ in
\eqref{eq:imaginary-exponential} gives the pure-vector exponentials.
Every quotient uses its analytic limit at a zero argument.

\subsubsection{Rotation and boost factors}
\label{sec:algebra-rotation-boost}
Let $\theta\in\R$ and let $\bu\in\R^3$ be a real unit vector,
$\bu^2=1$.  The two special exponentials just derived define the complex
rotation factor $R$ and the real boost factor $L$:
\begin{equation}
 R(\theta,\bu):=\exp\!\left(\ii\frac\theta2\bu\cdot\bsig\right)
 =\cos\frac\theta2+\ii\sin\frac\theta2\bu\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:rotation-exponential}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 L(\theta,\bu):=\exp\!\left(\frac\theta2\bu\cdot\bsig\right)
 =\cosh\frac\theta2+\sinh\frac\theta2\bu\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:boost-exponential}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:rotation-exponential} and \eqref{eq:boost-exponential},
the parameter is a rotation angle for $R$ and a rapidity for $L$; both are
real dimensionless numbers.  Here these names specify algebraic objects.
Their actions on a general paravector are derived in
Section~\ref{sec:preserved-quantities}; their physical applications follow in
Section~\ref{sec:transformations}.

Because $(\bu\cdot\bsig)^2=1$, reversing the parameter gives the
inverses in \eqref{eq:rotation-inverse} and \eqref{eq:boost-inverse}:
\begin{align}
 R^{-1}&=R(-\theta,\bu)
 =\cos\frac\theta2-\ii\sin\frac\theta2\bu\cdot\bsig,
 \label{eq:rotation-inverse}\\
 L^{-1}&=L(-\theta,\bu)
 =\cosh\frac\theta2-\sinh\frac\theta2\bu\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:boost-inverse}
\end{align}
Sigma conjugation changes both $\ii$ and $\bsig$ in sign; Hermitian
conjugation changes $\ii$ but fixes a real vector.  Hence
\eqref{eq:rotation-star} and \eqref{eq:boost-star} give sigma conjugates;
\eqref{eq:rotation-H} and \eqref{eq:boost-H} give Hermitian conjugates:
\begin{align}
 R^*&=R, \label{eq:rotation-star}\\
 R^{\mathsf H}&=R^{-1}, \label{eq:rotation-H}\\
 L^*&=L^{-1}, \label{eq:boost-star}\\
 L^{\mathsf H}&=L. \label{eq:boost-H}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:rotation-H} makes $R$ unitary; \eqref{eq:boost-H}
makes $L$ Hermitian, so $L^{\mathsf H}L=L^2$.  Their determinants and
positive norms follow from the circular and hyperbolic identities:
\begin{align}
 \det(R)&=\cos^2(\theta/2)+\sin^2(\theta/2)=1,
 \label{eq:rotation-determinant}\\
 \det(L)&=\cosh^2(\theta/2)-\sinh^2(\theta/2)=1,
 \label{eq:boost-determinant}\\
 \lVert R\rVert^2&=1, \label{eq:rotation-norm}\\
 \lVert L\rVert^2&=\cosh\theta. \label{eq:boost-norm}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:rotation-determinant} and \eqref{eq:boost-determinant}
make both adjugates equal their inverses.  Equations~\eqref{eq:rotation-norm}
and \eqref{eq:boost-norm} distinguish their positive norms; only $R$ is
unitary for nonzero $\theta$.  The half-angle gives the sign and period in
\eqref{eq:rotation-two-pi} and \eqref{eq:rotation-four-pi}:
\begin{align}
 R(\theta+2\pi,\bu)&=-R(\theta,\bu),
 \label{eq:rotation-two-pi}\\
 R(\theta+4\pi,\bu)&=R(\theta,\bu).
 \label{eq:rotation-four-pi}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Gamma form.}
Recall $L=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ from
\eqref{eq:boost-exponential}.  Equations~\eqref{eq:boost-beta} and
\eqref{eq:boost-gamma} define its real velocity parameter and gamma factor:
\begin{align}
 \beta&:=\tanh\theta,
 \label{eq:boost-beta}\\
 \gamma&:=\cosh\theta=\frac1{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}.
 \label{eq:boost-gamma}
\end{align}
Here $-1<\beta<1$, $\gamma\ge1$, and $\gamma\beta=\sinh\theta$.
The half-angle identities give
\begin{align*}
 \cosh\frac\theta2&=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{2}},\\
 \sinh\frac\theta2&=\frac{\gamma\beta}{\sqrt{2(\gamma+1)}}.
\end{align*}
Hence the boost factor itself is
\begin{equation}
 L=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{2}}
 \left(1+\frac{\gamma\beta}{\gamma+1}\bu\cdot\bsig\right).
 \label{eq:boost-gamma-form}
\end{equation}
Squaring doubles the exponent, so the full-rapidity paravector is
\begin{equation}
 L^2=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig)
 =\gamma(1+\beta\bu\cdot\bsig).
 \label{eq:boost-square}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:boost-square} has prefactor $\gamma$; $L$ has the half-angle
prefactor in \eqref{eq:boost-gamma-form}.  Equivalently, for any real vector
$\bA\ne0$, put $\bv=\tanh\lVert\bA\rVert\,\bA/\lVert\bA\rVert$ and
$\gamma=\cosh\lVert\bA\rVert$.  Then
\[
 \exp(\bA\cdot\bsig)=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig).
\]
The zero-vector case follows by continuity.  This last real paravector has
determinant one and becomes proper velocity in
Section~\ref{sec:kinematics}.

\subsubsection{Cayley transform}
Let $Y=b+\bB\cdot\bsig$ be real, with $b\in\R$, $\bB\in\R^3$, and
$0<\lVert\bB\rVert<b$.  Set $\bu=\bB/\lVert\bB\rVert$ and recall
$\Pi_\pm=(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig)/2$.  The real conjugate
$Y^*=b-\bB\cdot\bsig=\adj Y$ commutes with $Y$, so
\[
 \frac{Y}{Y^*}=\frac{Y^2}{\det(Y)}
 =\frac{b^2+\bB^2+2b\bB\cdot\bsig}{b^2-\bB^2}.
\]
Define the real parameter $\theta=2\tanh^{-1}(\lVert\bB\rVert/b)$.
The double-angle identities give
\begin{align*}
 \cosh\theta&=\frac{b^2+\bB^2}{b^2-\bB^2},\\
 \sinh\theta&=\frac{2b\lVert\bB\rVert}{b^2-\bB^2}.
\end{align*}
Thus the quotient is the full-rapidity exponential
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \frac{b+\bB\cdot\bsig}{b-\bB\cdot\bsig}
 &=\cosh\theta+\sinh\theta\,\bu\cdot\bsig\\
 &=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig)
 =e^\theta\Pi_++e^{-\theta}\Pi_-.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:cayley-exponential}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cayley-exponential} is $L(\theta,\bu)^2$ from
\eqref{eq:boost-square}.  Its real generator
vector is $\bA=\theta\bu$, while the Cayley vector obeys
\begin{equation}
 \bB=b\tanh\!\left(\frac{\lVert\bA\rVert}{2}\right)
       \frac{\bA}{\lVert\bA\rVert}.
 \label{eq:cayley-generator-map}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cayley-generator-map} gives
$\lVert\bB\rVert/b=\tanh(\theta/2)$, the half-rapidity
parameter.  The corresponding real gamma factor and vector are
\begin{align*}
 \gamma&=\frac{b^2+\bB^2}{b^2-\bB^2},\\
 \bv&=\frac{2b\bB}{b^2+\bB^2}.
\end{align*}
They give the equivalent forms
\[
 \exp(\bA\cdot\bsig)=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig)
 =\frac{b+\bB\cdot\bsig}{b-\bB\cdot\bsig}=\frac{Y}{Y^*}.
\]
Choosing $b=1$ gives the normalized Cayley form; $\bB=0$ follows by
continuity.

\subsubsection{Composing the factors}
Recall $L(\theta,\bu)=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ and
$R(\theta,\bu)=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ from
\eqref{eq:boost-exponential} and \eqref{eq:rotation-exponential}.
For a fixed real unit direction $\bu$ and real parameters $\theta,\theta'$, the
generators commute.  Equation~\eqref{eq:boost-composition} therefore adds
their parameters:
\begin{equation}
 L(\theta,\bu)L(\theta',\bu)=L(\theta+\theta',\bu).
 \label{eq:boost-composition}
\end{equation}
\begin{align*}
 R(\theta,\bu)R(\theta',\bu)&=R(\theta+\theta',\bu),\\
 L(\theta,\bu)R(\theta',\bu)&=R(\theta',\bu)L(\theta,\bu).
\end{align*}
For the aligned real and imaginary generator, put
$w=(\theta+\ii\theta')/2\in\C$.  Its exponential is
\[
 \exp(w\bu\cdot\bsig)
 =L(\theta,\bu)R(\theta',\bu)
 =\cosh w+\sinh w\,\bu\cdot\bsig.
\]
Expanding the complex scalar coefficients gives
\begin{align*}
 \cosh w&=\cosh\frac\theta2\cos\frac{\theta'}2
 +\ii\sinh\frac\theta2\sin\frac{\theta'}2,\\
 \sinh w&=\sinh\frac\theta2\cos\frac{\theta'}2
 +\ii\cosh\frac\theta2\sin\frac{\theta'}2.
\end{align*}
This aligned factorization requires parallel real and imaginary generator
vectors.  For different real unit directions $\bu,\bv$, the vector product
instead gives
\begin{equation}
 \left[\tfrac12\bu\cdot\bsig,\tfrac12\bv\cdot\bsig\right]
 =\tfrac\ii2(\bu\times\bv)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:boost-generator-commutator}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:boost-generator-commutator} is a rotation generator:
boosts about different axes do not commute.

\subsubsection{Logarithm}
For complex $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$ with $\bV^2\ne0$, recall
$w=+\sqrt{\bV^2}$, $\bu=\bV/w$, $\lambda_\pm=S\pm w$, and
$\Pi_\pm=(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig)/2$.  Assume both eigenvalues are nonzero.
For a nonzero complex scalar $\lambda$, the scalar branches are
\[
 \log\lambda=\log\lVert\lambda\rVert+\ii(\arg\lambda+2n\pi),
 \qquad n\in\mathbb Z.
\]
Choose one branch for each eigenvalue.  The spectral rule gives
\eqref{eq:paravector-logarithm}:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \log Z&=\log(\lambda_+)\Pi_++\log(\lambda_-)\Pi_-\\
 &=\tfrac12\bigl(\log\lambda_++\log\lambda_-\bigr)
 +\tfrac12\bigl(\log\lambda_+-\log\lambda_-\bigr)\bu\cdot\bsig.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:paravector-logarithm}
\end{equation}
Exchanging $w$ and $-w$ exchanges both eigenvalue--projector pairs and
preserves the same chosen logarithm.  Independent scalar branches give
the different logarithms.

For a real unit vector $\bu$, \eqref{eq:paravector-logarithm} uses
eigenvalues $1$ and $-1$, giving \eqref{eq:unit-vector-logarithm} for integers $n,m$:
\begin{equation}
 \log(\bu\cdot\bsig)
 =2n\pi\ii\Pi_++(2m+1)\pi\ii\Pi_-.
 \label{eq:unit-vector-logarithm}
\end{equation}
For real $Y=b+\bB\cdot\bsig$, with $b\in\R$, $\bB\in\R^3$, and
$b>\lVert\bB\rVert$, both eigenvalues are positive.  Combining their real
logarithms yields \eqref{eq:positive-real-logarithm}:
\begin{equation}
 \log Y=\tfrac12\log(b^2-\bB^2)
 +\tanh^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\lVert\bB\rVert}{b}\right)
       \frac{\bB}{\lVert\bB\rVert}\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:positive-real-logarithm}
\end{equation}
The vector quotient uses its limit at $\bB=0$; the general coalescing case
was treated above.

\section{Transformations and invariants}
\label{sec:preserved-quantities}

Here $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$ is a complex paravector: $S\in\C$ and
$\bV\in\C^3$.  Transformation factors are constant complex paravectors.
Distinguish the scalar part $\scpart Z=S=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}Z$
from \eqref{eq:trace-scalar}, the complex determinant $S^2-\bV^2$ from
\eqref{eq:det}, and the positive norm $\scpart{Z^{\mathsf H}Z}$ from
\eqref{eq:hermitian-norm}.  Scalar part and trace carry exactly the same
information; we use $\operatorname{sc}$ below.

\subsection{Similarity, determinant, and norm}

For an invertible complex paravector $T$, similarity means $Z'=T^{-1}ZT$.
Scalar-part cyclicity proves \eqref{eq:scalar-similarity}:
\begin{equation}
 \scpart{Z'}=\scpart{T^{-1}ZT}
 =\scpart{ZTT^{-1}}=\scpart Z.
 \label{eq:scalar-similarity}
\end{equation}
Thus trace is also preserved.  Multiplicativity proves the determinant
invariance \eqref{eq:det-similarity}:
\begin{equation}
 \det(Z')=\det(T^{-1})\det(Z)\det(T)=\det(Z).
 \label{eq:det-similarity}
\end{equation}
The opposite similarity $Z'=TZT^{-1}$ has the same invariants.
For independent complex factors $T,T'$, the law $Z'=T'ZT$ instead gives
\begin{equation}
 \det(Z')=\det(T')\det(T)\det(Z).
 \label{eq:two-factor-determinant}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:two-factor-determinant} preserves the determinant for
every $Z$ exactly when $\det(T')\det(T)=1$.  This alone does not preserve
scalar part, norm, or the real paravector subspace.

Recall that a unitary $U$ satisfies $U^{\mathsf H}=U^{-1}$ by
\eqref{eq:unitary-transform}.  For $Z'=U^{\mathsf H}ZU$, cancellation and
scalar-part cyclicity prove \eqref{eq:unitary-norm-invariance}:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \lVert Z'\rVert^2
 &=\scpart{U^{\mathsf H}Z^{\mathsf H}UU^{\mathsf H}ZU}\\
 &=\scpart{Z^{\mathsf H}ZUU^{\mathsf H}}=\lVert Z\rVert^2.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:unitary-norm-invariance}
\end{equation}
The norm is also preserved by $Z'=UZ$, $Z'=ZU$, and
$Z'=UZU^{\mathsf H}$.  The two similarities additionally preserve scalar
part and determinant; a one-sided unitary product need not.

\subsubsection{Unit determinant is not Hermitian unitarity}

The word \emph{unit} in \eqref{eq:unit-paravector} means invertible;
\emph{unitary} means \eqref{eq:unitary-transform}.
A useful third condition is determinant one.  To see their difference,
let $\dd X=\dd t+\dd\br\cdot\bsig$ be a real paravector increment, with
$\dd t>\lVert\dd\br\rVert$, and define the positive real scalar
$\dd s=\sqrt{\dd t^2-\dd\br^2}$.  The real normalized paravector is
$U=\dd X/\dd s$.  Since $\dd X^*\dd X=\dd s^2$,
\begin{equation}
 U^*U=\frac{\dd X^*\dd X}{\dd s^2}=1.
 \label{eq:proper-velocity-star-normalization}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:proper-velocity-star-normalization} gives $\det(U)=1$
and $U^{-1}=U^*$.  However, the two conjugates are
\begin{align*}
 U^{\mathsf H}&=U=\frac{\dd t+\dd\br\cdot\bsig}{\dd s},\\
 U^*&=\frac{\dd t-\dd\br\cdot\bsig}{\dd s}.
\end{align*}
Hermitian conjugation leaves every real paravector fixed; sigma conjugation
reverses its vector part.  Therefore
\begin{align}
 U^{\mathsf H}U&=U^2
 =\frac{\dd t^2+\dd\br^2+2\dd t\,\dd\br\cdot\bsig}{\dd s^2},
 \label{eq:proper-velocity-hermitian-square}\\
 \lVert U\rVert^2&=\frac{\dd t^2+\dd\br^2}{\dd t^2-\dd\br^2}.
 \label{eq:proper-velocity-positive-norm}
\end{align}
Both expressions in \eqref{eq:proper-velocity-hermitian-square} and
\eqref{eq:proper-velocity-positive-norm} equal $1$ only when $\dd\br=0$.  In
Section~\ref{sec:kinematics}, this $U$ is proper velocity: its normalization
is $U^*U=1$, while $U^{\mathsf H}=U^*$ holds only in its rest frame.

\subsection{Real paravectors and the Lorentz metric}

Let $X=a+\bA\cdot\bsig$ be real, with $a\in\R$ and
$\bA\in\R^3$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:det} gives
$\det(X)=a^2-\bA^2$, with Lorentz signature;
\eqref{eq:coefficient-norm} gives $\lVert X\rVert^2=a^2+\bA^2$,
with Euclidean signature.  A transformation of real spacetime paravectors must
preserve both reality and the determinant.  For a complex $T$ with
$\det(T)=1$, these requirements hold for the Hermitian congruence
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{X'=T^{-1}X(T^{-1})^{\mathsf H}.}
 \label{eq:general-lorentz-congruence}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:general-lorentz-congruence} preserves reality and
determinant because
\begin{align*}
 X'^{\mathsf H}&=T^{-1}X^{\mathsf H}(T^{-1})^{\mathsf H}=X',\\
 \det(X')&=\det(T^{-1})\det(X)\det((T^{-1})^{\mathsf H})
 =\det(X).
\end{align*}
These congruences give the proper, future-preserving Lorentz transformations.
In the matrix representation, the eigenvalues of $X$ are
$a\pm\lVert\bA\rVert$; the future cone consists of positive semidefinite
Hermitian matrices.  Congruence preserves this positivity.  The factors
$T$ and $-T$ give the same transformation, producing the double cover
$\mathrm{SL}(2,\C)$.\cite{baylis2004}

For real $\theta$ and a real unit $\bu$, the algebraic definitions
$R=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ and
$L=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ reduce this congruence to
$X'=R^{\mathsf H}XR$ and $X'=L^{-1}XL^{-1}$, respectively.
Their components are derived in the next two subsections.
For $X=1$, equation~\eqref{eq:boost-time-axis} makes the invariant
distinction explicit:
\begin{align}
 X'&=L^{-2}=\cosh\theta-\sinh\theta\bu\cdot\bsig,
 \label{eq:boost-time-axis}\\
 \det(X')&=1, \notag\\
 \scpart{X'}&=\cosh\theta, \notag\\
 \lVert X'\rVert^2&=\cosh(2\theta). \notag
\end{align}
Thus the determinant supplies the spacetime metric, while the object being
transformed determines whether to use congruence or similarity.

\subsection{Rotation action and Rodrigues' formula}
\label{sec:algebra-rotation-action}

Recall the unitary factor
$R=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ from
\eqref{eq:rotation-exponential}, with real angle $\theta$ and real unit
axis $\bu$.  For the complex paravector $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, define
\begin{equation}
 Z'=R^{\mathsf H}ZR=R^{-1}ZR.
 \label{eq:rotation-action}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:rotation-action} keeps $S'=S$.  The vector product
\eqref{eq:vector-product} gives
\begin{align*}
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)(\bV\cdot\bsig)(\bu\cdot\bsig)
 &=2(\bu\cdot\bV)\bu\cdot\bsig-\bV\cdot\bsig,\\
 [\bV\cdot\bsig,\bu\cdot\bsig]
 &=-2\ii(\bu\times\bV)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align*}
Inserting the sine and cosine forms of $R$ and $R^{-1}$ therefore yields
\begin{align*}
 \bV'\cdot\bsig
 &={}\cos^2\frac\theta2\,\bV\cdot\bsig
 +2\sin\frac\theta2\cos\frac\theta2
       (\bu\times\bV)\cdot\bsig\\
 &\quad+\sin^2\frac\theta2
 \left(2(\bu\cdot\bV)\bu\cdot\bsig-\bV\cdot\bsig\right).
\end{align*}
Collecting terms gives Rodrigues' formula:
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\bV'=\bV\cos\theta+(\bu\times\bV)\sin\theta
 +(\bu\cdot\bV)(1-\cos\theta)\bu.}
 \label{eq:rodrigues}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:rodrigues} is a right-handed rotation through
$+\theta$ about $\bu$.
It preserves $\operatorname{sc}$, determinant, norm, and the real
paravector subspace.  For complex $\bV$, it rotates its real and imaginary
parts by the same real rotation.  The sign change in \eqref{eq:rotation-two-pi} affects both factors
in \eqref{eq:rotation-action}, so $Z'$ has period $2\pi$.

\subsection{Boost action and its sign convention}
\label{sec:algebra-boost-action}

Recall $L=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ from
\eqref{eq:boost-exponential}; $\theta\in\R$, $\bu\in\R^3$, $\bu^2=1$,
$\beta=\tanh\theta$, and $\gamma=\cosh\theta$.
For a complex $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, choose the congruence
\begin{equation}
 Z'=L^{-1}ZL^{-1}.
 \label{eq:boost-action}
\end{equation}
The factors in \eqref{eq:boost-action} have the same sign because
$L^{\mathsf H}=L$ by \eqref{eq:boost-H}.
Write the complex vector as the sum of its parallel and perpendicular parts:
\begin{align*}
 \bV_{\parallel}&:=(\bu\cdot\bV)\bu,\\
 \bV_{\perp}&:=\bV-\bV_{\parallel}.
\end{align*}
The parallel term commutes with $\bu\cdot\bsig$; the perpendicular term
anticommutes with it.  Consequently,
\begin{align*}
 L^{-1}(S+\bV_{\parallel}\cdot\bsig)L^{-1}
 &=(S+\bV_{\parallel}\cdot\bsig)L^{-2},\\
 L^{-1}(\bV_{\perp}\cdot\bsig)L^{-1}
 &=\bV_{\perp}\cdot\bsig.
\end{align*}
Using $L^{-2}=\gamma(1-\beta\bu\cdot\bsig)$ from
\eqref{eq:boost-square}, multiplication gives
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{S'=\gamma(S-\beta\bu\cdot\bV).}
 \label{eq:boost-scalar-component}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\bV'=\bV_{\perp}+\gamma(\bV_{\parallel}-\beta S\bu).}
 \label{eq:boost-vector-component}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:boost-scalar-component} and
\eqref{eq:boost-vector-component} preserve $S^2-\bV^2$ and reality; they generally
change $S$ and $\lVert Z\rVert^2$.

The inverse signs are a convention: equation~\eqref{eq:boost-inverse}
gives $L^{-1}=\exp(-\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)=L(-\theta,\bu)$.
Replacing $\theta$ by $-\theta$ changes \eqref{eq:boost-action} to
$Z'=LZL$ and changes $\beta$ to $-\beta$ in the component equations.
The two conventions describe opposite boosts.  We keep the positive
exponential definition of $L$ throughout; in the spacetime application,
\eqref{eq:boost-action} gives components in the frame moving with velocity
$+\beta\bu$.

A boost similarity is a different action.  For $Z'=L^{-1}ZL$, the same
commutation rules instead give $S'=S$ and
\begin{equation}
 \bV'=\bV_{\parallel}
 +\gamma\bigl(\bV_{\perp}-\ii\beta\bu\times\bV\bigr).
 \label{eq:boost-similarity-vector}
\end{equation}
The similarity \eqref{eq:boost-similarity-vector} preserves scalar part and
determinant, but a real vector generally
acquires an imaginary component.  It will act on the complex electromagnetic
field, whose real and imaginary vectors represent two physical fields.

\section{Differentiated fields}
\label{sec:differentiated-fields}

\subsection{Paravector differential operators}

Let $t\in\R$ and $\br=\{r_1,r_2,r_3\}\in\R^3$ be real coordinates.
Define their scalar and vector derivatives by
\[
 \partial_t:=\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\qquad
 \pdr:=\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial r_1},
              \frac{\partial}{\partial r_2},
              \frac{\partial}{\partial r_3}\right\}.
\]
Equations~\eqref{eq:paravector-derivative} and
\eqref{eq:conjugate-paravector-derivative} define the partial paravector
and its sigma conjugate:
\begin{equation}
 \partial:=\partial_t+\bsig\cdot\pdr
 =\partial_t+\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sigma_n\frac{\partial}{\partial r_n},
 \label{eq:paravector-derivative}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \partial^*=\adj{\partial}=\partial_t-\bsig\cdot\pdr.
 \label{eq:conjugate-paravector-derivative}
\end{equation}
The sigma generators are constant.  Derivatives act from the left on
everything to their right unless parentheses specify their scope.
For fields with commuting second derivatives, anticommutation cancels
all mixed spatial terms:
\begin{align*}
 (\bsig\cdot\pdr)^2
 &=\sum_{n=1}^{3}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r_n^2}
 +\sum_{n<m}(\sigma_n\sigma_m+\sigma_m\sigma_n)
           \frac{\partial^2}{\partial r_n\partial r_m}\\
 &=\pdr\cdot\pdr.
\end{align*}
The mixed time--space terms likewise cancel.  Both orders therefore give
the scalar d'Alembertian in \eqref{eq:dalembertian}:
\begin{equation}
 \partial\partial^*=\partial^*\partial
 =\partial_t^2-\pdr\cdot\pdr=:\Box.
 \label{eq:dalembertian}
\end{equation}
These are operator identities: $\partial\partial^*Z$ means
$\partial(\partial^*Z)$.

\subsection{Components and the product rule}

Let the complex paravector field be
\[
 Z(t,\br)=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\ii\bB)\cdot\bsig,
\]
with real scalar functions $a,b:\R^4\to\R$ and real vector functions
$\bA,\bB:\R^4\to\R^3$.  Ordered vector multiplication gives its four
real components in \eqref{eq:differentiated-components}:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \partial Z={}&(\partial_ta+\pdr\cdot\bA)
 +\ii(\partial_tb+\pdr\cdot\bB)\\
 &+(\pdr a+\partial_t\bA-\pdr\times\bB)\cdot\bsig\\
 &+\ii(\pdr b+\pdr\times\bA+\partial_t\bB)\cdot\bsig.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:differentiated-components}
\end{equation}
Multiplication by $\ii$ exchanges real and imaginary parts, with the
former imaginary parts acquiring a minus sign.

For a second complex paravector field $Z'(t,\br)$, the prime labels the
field rather than a derivative.  Apply the scalar product rule before
reordering any factors:
\begin{align*}
 \partial(ZZ')
 ={}&(\partial_tZ)Z'+Z(\partial_tZ')\\
 &+\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sigma_n
   \left(\frac{\partial Z}{\partial r_n}Z'
          +Z\frac{\partial Z'}{\partial r_n}\right)\\
 ={}&(\partial Z)Z'+Z(\partial Z')
    +\sum_{n=1}^{3}[\sigma_n,Z]\frac{\partial Z'}{\partial r_n}.
\end{align*}
Define the last sum as
$[\bsig,Z]\cdot\pdr Z':=\sum_n[\sigma_n,Z]\,\partial Z'/\partial r_n$.
Changing the spatial sign gives the paired rules
\eqref{eq:paravector-product-rule} and \eqref{eq:conjugate-product-rule}:
\begin{align}
 \partial(ZZ')&=(\partial Z)Z'+Z(\partial Z')
                +[\bsig,Z]\cdot\pdr Z',
 \label{eq:paravector-product-rule}\\
 \partial^*(ZZ')&=(\partial^*Z)Z'+Z(\partial^*Z')
                -[\bsig,Z]\cdot\pdr Z'.
 \label{eq:conjugate-product-rule}
\end{align}
The contraction retains the displayed multiplied order.

For a noncommuting check, take $Z=\sigma_2$ and $Z'=r_1$.  Since
$[\sigma_1,\sigma_2]=2\ii\sigma_3$, the two right-hand sides give
\begin{align*}
 \partial(\sigma_2r_1)
 &=\sigma_2\sigma_1+[\sigma_1,\sigma_2]
  =-\ii\sigma_3+2\ii\sigma_3=\ii\sigma_3,\\
 \partial^*(\sigma_2r_1)
 &=-\sigma_2\sigma_1-[\sigma_1,\sigma_2]
  =\ii\sigma_3-2\ii\sigma_3=-\ii\sigma_3.
\end{align*}
These agree with direct differentiation, $\pm\sigma_1\sigma_2$.
The ordinary Leibniz rule holds when the left factor commutes with every
$\sigma_n$, in particular for a complex scalar.  Otherwise the commutator
term must be retained.

\section{Space--time kinematics}
\label{sec:kinematics}

Let $X=t+\br\cdot\bsig$ be a real event, with time $t\in\R$ and
position $\br\in\R^3$.  A massive particle follows a future-directed
time-like path $\br(t)$; $s$ denotes its proper time.

\subsection{Intervals, velocity, and momentum}

The differential event and its invariant interval are
\[
 \dd X=\dd t+\dd\br\cdot\bsig.
\]
\begin{equation}
 \dd s^2=\det(\dd X)=\dd X\,\dd X^*
 =\dd t^2-\dd\br^2.
 \label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
By \eqref{eq:general-lorentz-congruence}, a constant complex factor $T$
with $\det T=1$ gives
\[
 \dd X'=T^{-1}\dd X(T^{-1})^{\mathsf H},
 \qquad \det(\dd X')=\dd s^2.
\]
Positive, zero, and negative values of \eqref{eq:interval} mean time-like, null, and space-like
separation.  Massive particles follow time-like paths; light follows null
paths; causal signals cannot connect space-like separated events.
For two real intervals,
\begin{align*}
 [\dd X,\dd X']&=2\ii(\dd\br\times\dd\br')\cdot\bsig,\\
 (\dd X)^{-1}&=\frac{\dd X^*}{\dd s^2}\qquad(\dd s^2\ne0).
\end{align*}
A null interval has no inverse.

Along the massive path, define the real coordinate velocity
$\bv=\dd\br/\dd t$ and choose $\dd s>0$.  Then
$\dd s^2=(1-\bv^2)\dd t^2$ gives
\begin{align}
 \gamma&:=\frac1{\sqrt{1-\bv^2}}=\frac{\dd t}{\dd s},
 \label{eq:lorentz-factor}\\
 \frac{\dd}{\dd s}&=\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}.
 \label{eq:proper-time-derivative}
\end{align}
Applying \eqref{eq:proper-time-derivative} with \eqref{eq:lorentz-factor}
gives the real proper velocity
\begin{equation}
 U:=\frac{\dd X}{\dd s}=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig).
 \label{eq:proper-velocity}
\end{equation}
For \eqref{eq:proper-velocity}, the normalization
\eqref{eq:proper-velocity-star-normalization} gives
$U^*U=1$ and $U^{-1}=U^*=\gamma(1-\bv\cdot\bsig)$.
Since $U^{\mathsf H}=U$, this is unit Minkowski determinant, not
Hermitian unitarity except at rest.
For a real unit direction $\bu$, write $\bv=\beta\bu$ and
$\theta=\tanh^{-1}\beta$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:boost-square} gives $U=L^2$ for
$L=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$.  Between real times $t_0$ and $t$,
the elapsed proper time is
\begin{equation}
 s(t)-s(t_0)=\int_{t_0}^{t}\frac{\dd t'}{\gamma(t')}.
 \label{eq:elapsed-proper-time}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:elapsed-proper-time} compares clock readings along
the path.  For constant real mass $m>0$, define the real four-momentum
\begin{equation}
 P:=mU=E+\bp\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:four-momentum}
\end{equation}
The real coefficients of \eqref{eq:four-momentum} are energy and momentum:
\begin{align*}
 E&=\scpart P=m\gamma,\\
 \bp&=\vecpart P=m\gamma\bv,\\
 P^{-1}&=\frac{E-\bp\cdot\bsig}{m^2}.
\end{align*}
The determinant gives the mass shell
\begin{equation}
 E^2-\bp^2=\det P=m^2.
 \label{eq:mass-shell}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:mass-shell} follows from $\det U=1$.
A massless path has no proper-time velocity.  Define its momentum directly
as a future null paravector $P=E+\bp\cdot\bsig$, with $E>0$ and
$E^2=\bp^2$; it has no inverse.

\subsection{Proper acceleration}
\label{sec:proper-acceleration}

Recall the real velocity $\bv(t)$, proper velocity $U$, and Lorentz factor
$\gamma$ above.  Define the real coordinate acceleration
$\ba=\dd\bv/\dd t$.  Differentiating gives
\begin{align*}
 \frac{\dd\gamma}{\dd t}
 &=-\tfrac12(1-\bv^2)^{-3/2}(-2\bv\cdot\ba)
 =\gamma^3\bv\cdot\ba,\\
 \frac{\dd^2}{\dd s^2}
 &=\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\left(\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\right)
 =\gamma^2\frac{\dd^2}{\dd t^2}
  +\gamma^4(\bv\cdot\ba)\frac{\dd}{\dd t}.
\end{align*}
Applying this to $X$ yields the real proper-acceleration paravector:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd U}{\dd s}
 =\gamma^4(\bv\cdot\ba)
 +\left(\gamma^2\ba+\gamma^4(\bv\cdot\ba)\bv\right)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:proper-acceleration-components}
\end{equation}
Differentiating $U^*U=1$ gives
\[
 \left(\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\right)^*U
 +U^*\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}=0.
\]
The two terms have equal real scalar parts, so
\begin{equation}
 \scpart{U^*\dd U/\dd s}=0.
 \label{eq:proper-acceleration-orthogonal}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:proper-acceleration-orthogonal} states Minkowski
orthogonality of four-velocity and four-acceleration.  Multiplication on the right by $U^*$ also gives
\begin{align*}
 \adj{\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}}
 &=-U^*\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}U^*.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation}
 U^*\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}
 =\gamma^3(\ba-\ii\bv\times\ba)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:velocity-acceleration-product}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:velocity-acceleration-product}, the real vector records acceleration and the imaginary vector records
turning of the velocity.  Since $\ba\cdot(\bv\times\ba)=0$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \det\left(\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\right)
 &=-\left(U^*\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\right)^2\\
 &=-\gamma^6\left(\ba^2-(\bv\times\ba)^2\right)\\
 &=-\gamma^4\left(\ba^2+\gamma^2(\bv\cdot\ba)^2\right).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:proper-acceleration-norm}
\end{equation}
The last equality in \eqref{eq:proper-acceleration-norm} uses
$(\bv\times\ba)^2=\bv^2\ba^2-(\bv\cdot\ba)^2$ and
$1-\bv^2=\gamma^{-2}$.  Nonzero proper acceleration is therefore
space-like; its invariant squared magnitude is the negative determinant.
For $\bv\ne0$, define
$\ba_{\parallel}=(\bv\cdot\ba)\bv/\bv^2$ and
$\ba_{\perp}=\ba-\ba_{\parallel}$.  Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
 -\det\left(\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\right)
 =\gamma^4\ba_{\perp}^2+\gamma^6\ba_{\parallel}^2.
 \label{eq:proper-acceleration-split}
\end{equation}
At rest \eqref{eq:proper-acceleration-split} reduces to $\ba^2$.

\subsection{Free-particle action}

Recall real $m>0$, $\bv=\dd\br/\dd t$, and $\dd s=\dd t/\gamma$.
Here $L(t,\br,\bv)$ is a real scalar Lagrangian, not the boost factor.
The free action is the physical choice
\begin{equation}
 \int L\dd t=-m\int\dd s.
 \label{eq:free-particle-action}
\end{equation}
Using $\dd s=\dd t/\gamma$, equation~\eqref{eq:free-particle-action}
gives the Lagrangian
\begin{equation}
 L=-m\sqrt{1-\bv^2}=-m/\gamma.
 \label{eq:free-particle-lagrangian}
\end{equation}
For endpoint-fixed real path variations $\delta\br(t)$, varying
\eqref{eq:free-particle-lagrangian} and integrating by parts gives
\[
 \delta\int L\dd t
 =\int\left(\pdr L-\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\partial_{\bv}L\right)
 \cdot\delta\br\dd t.
\]
Stationarity for every variation yields the Euler--Lagrange equation.
With real Hamiltonian $H:=\bv\cdot\partial_{\bv}L-L$, the chain rule gives
\begin{align}
 \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\partial_{\bv}L&=\pdr L, \label{eq:euler-lagrange}\\
 \frac{\dd H}{\dd t}
 &=\bv\cdot\left(\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\partial_{\bv}L-\pdr L\right)
   -\partial_tL=-\partial_tL. \label{eq:hamiltonian-energy-balance}
\end{align}
By \eqref{eq:euler-lagrange} and \eqref{eq:hamiltonian-energy-balance},
spatial and temporal translation invariance conserve canonical
momentum and energy.  For the free action,
\begin{align*}
 \partial_{\bv}L&=m\gamma\bv=\bp,\\
 H&=m\gamma\bv^2+m/\gamma=m\gamma=E.
\end{align*}
Writing $v=\lVert\bv\rVert\ll1$, the binomial series gives
\begin{align*}
 \sqrt{1-v^2}&=1-\tfrac12v^2+O(v^4),\\
 \gamma&=1+\tfrac12v^2+O(v^4),\\
 L&=-m+\tfrac12mv^2+O(mv^4),\\
 H&=m+\tfrac12mv^2+O(mv^4).
\end{align*}
The exact kinetic energy above rest is
\begin{equation}
 K:=H-m=m(\gamma-1).
 \label{eq:kinetic-energy}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:kinetic-energy} and both expansions recover $mv^2/2$
at low speed after removal of the constant rest term.

\section{Electromagnetism}
\label{sec:electromagnetism}

All coefficient fields in this section are real functions of real
$(t,\br)$.  Define
\begin{align}
 F&=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig
 &&\text{electric and magnetic fields}, \label{eq:em-field-definition}\\
 \Phi&=V+\bA\cdot\bsig
 &&\text{scalar and vector potentials}, \notag\\
 D&=\rho+\bJ\cdot\bsig
 &&\text{charge and current densities}. \notag
\end{align}
Thus $\Phi,D$ are real paravectors, while \eqref{eq:em-field-definition}
is a complex pure vector.
Recall $\partial=\partial_t+\bsig\cdot\pdr$ and
$\partial^*\partial=\Box=\partial_t^2-\pdr^2$.

The dot/cross product~\eqref{eq:vector-product} combines metric and
orientation.  With a spatial derivative it gives divergence and curl,
while the six real components of $F$ represent an antisymmetric spacetime
field tensor.  This algebra of physical space is isomorphic to the even
subalgebra of spacetime algebra; its real paravectors represent spacetime
vectors relative to an observer.\cite{baylis2004}
The product explains the compact form; Maxwell dynamics remains physical
input, derived from an action in Section~\ref{sec:maxwell-action}.

\subsection{Maxwell's equations and wave propagation}

Maxwell's four equations are the single field law
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\partial F=D^*=\rho-\bJ\cdot\bsig.}
 \label{eq:maxwell}
\end{equation}
The product rule expands the left side of \eqref{eq:maxwell} into
\begin{align*}
 \partial F={}&\underbrace{\pdr\cdot\bE}_{\text{real scalar}}
 +\underbrace{\ii\pdr\cdot\bBf}_{\text{imaginary scalar}}\\
 &+\underbrace{(\partial_t\bE-\pdr\times\bBf)\cdot\bsig}
 _{\text{real vector}}\\
 &+\underbrace{\ii(\partial_t\bBf+\pdr\times\bE)\cdot\bsig}
 _{\text{imaginary vector}}.
\end{align*}
Equating the four parts gives
\begin{align}
 \pdr\cdot\bE&=\rho, \label{eq:gauss-electric}\\
 \pdr\cdot\bBf&=0, \label{eq:gauss-magnetic}\\
 \pdr\times\bBf-\partial_t\bE&=\bJ, \label{eq:ampere-maxwell}\\
 \pdr\times\bE+\partial_t\bBf&=0. \label{eq:faraday}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:gauss-electric}--\eqref{eq:faraday} are Gauss'
electric and magnetic laws, Amp\`ere--Maxwell's law, and Faraday's law.
Applying $\partial^*$ to \eqref{eq:maxwell} gives
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \Box F&=\partial^*D^*=(\partial D)^*\\
 &=(\partial_t\rho+\pdr\cdot\bJ)
 +(-\partial_t\bJ-\pdr\rho+\ii\pdr\times\bJ)\cdot\bsig.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:maxwell-wave}
\end{equation}
The scalar part of \eqref{eq:maxwell-wave} vanishes because $F$ is scalar-free.  Thus
\begin{align}
 \partial_t\rho+\pdr\cdot\bJ&=0, \label{eq:charge-continuity}\\
 \Box\bE&=-\partial_t\bJ-\pdr\rho, \label{eq:electric-wave}\\
 \Box\bBf&=\pdr\times\bJ. \label{eq:magnetic-wave}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:charge-continuity} is charge conservation;
\eqref{eq:electric-wave} and \eqref{eq:magnetic-wave} are the sourced field waves.
In vacuum, $D=0$ and both fields obey the free wave equation.

\subsection{Potentials and gauge freedom}

Recall the real potential $\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$.  Its derivative is
\begin{align*}
 \partial\Phi={}&(\partial_tV+\pdr\cdot\bA)
 +(\partial_t\bA+\pdr V)\cdot\bsig
 +\ii(\pdr\times\bA)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align*}
Define the real scalar $S:=\partial_tV+\pdr\cdot\bA$.  Identifying the
remaining components with $F^*$ gives
\begin{align}
 \partial\Phi&=S+F^*, \label{eq:potential-field}\\
 \bE&=-\partial_t\bA-\pdr V, \label{eq:electric-potential}\\
 \bBf&=\pdr\times\bA. \label{eq:magnetic-potential}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:potential-field} separates the gauge scalar from the field;
\eqref{eq:electric-potential} and \eqref{eq:magnetic-potential} satisfy
\eqref{eq:gauss-magnetic} and \eqref{eq:faraday} identically.
Since $F=\partial^*\Phi^*-S$,
\begin{align*}
 \partial F&=\Box\Phi^*-\partial S\\
 &=(\Box V-\partial_tS)-(\Box\bA+\pdr S)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align*}
Comparison with \eqref{eq:maxwell} yields
\begin{align}
 \rho&=\Box V-\partial_tS, \label{eq:potential-charge}\\
 \bJ&=\Box\bA+\pdr S. \label{eq:potential-current}
\end{align}
These source equations~\eqref{eq:potential-charge}--\eqref{eq:potential-current}
hold before gauge fixing.
For a real scalar gauge function $\lambda(t,\br)$, let
\begin{equation}
 \Phi'=\Phi+\partial^*\lambda.
 \label{eq:gauge-transformation}
\end{equation}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:gauge-transformation} gives
$\partial\Phi'=\partial\Phi+\Box\lambda$.  Only the scalar changes: $S'=S+\Box\lambda$, while $F'=F$.
In the Lorenz gauge $S=0$, the source equations reduce to
$\Box V=\rho$ and $\Box\bA=\bJ$.

\subsection{The electromagnetic field action}
\label{sec:maxwell-action}

For real scalar potential $V(t,\br)$ and vector potential
$\bA(t,\br)$, write $\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$.  With prescribed $D$,
Maxwell dynamics follows from the real Lagrangian density
\begin{equation}
 L=\tfrac12\repart{F^2}-\scpart{\Phi^*D}
  =\tfrac12(\bE^2-\bBf^2)-\rho V+\bJ\cdot\bA.
 \label{eq:maxwell-lagrangian}
\end{equation}
In varying the action of \eqref{eq:maxwell-lagrangian}, boundary-vanishing variations satisfy
\begin{align*}
 \delta\bE&=-\pdr\delta V-\partial_t\delta\bA,\\
 \delta\bBf&=\pdr\times\delta\bA.
\end{align*}
Integration by parts gives
\begin{equation}
 \delta\int L\dd t\dd^3\br
 =\int\left[(\pdr\cdot\bE-\rho)\delta V
 +(\partial_t\bE-\pdr\times\bBf+\bJ)\cdot\delta\bA\right]\dd t\dd^3\br.
 \label{eq:maxwell-action-variation}
\end{equation}
Independent variations in \eqref{eq:maxwell-action-variation} give
\eqref{eq:gauss-electric} and \eqref{eq:ampere-maxwell}; the potential
identities supply \eqref{eq:gauss-magnetic} and \eqref{eq:faraday}.
Together they recover \eqref{eq:maxwell}.  The symbol $\delta$ denotes variation.

\subsection{Field invariants}

Since $\scpart F=0$, its adjugate is $-F$.  Squaring gives
\begin{equation}
 F^2=-\det F=\bE^2-\bBf^2+2\ii\bE\cdot\bBf.
 \label{eq:field-invariants}
\end{equation}
For an invertible complex paravector $T$, the field similarity
$F'=T^{-1}FT$ preserves this scalar by \eqref{eq:det-similarity}.
Physical rotations and boosts use
$R=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ and
$L=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$, respectively, with real unit $\bu$
and real angle or rapidity $\theta$; their field laws are derived in
Section~\ref{sec:transformations}.
The real and imaginary parts of \eqref{eq:field-invariants} are
therefore observer-independent.
For a nonzero field, they give the classification
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Condition & Consequence\\
\midrule
$\bE\cdot\bBf=0$, $\bE^2>\bBf^2$ & Some frame has $\bBf'=0$.\\
$\bE\cdot\bBf=0$, $\bE^2<\bBf^2$ & Some frame has $\bE'=0$.\\
$\bE\cdot\bBf\ne0$ & Neither field can be eliminated.\\
$\bE\cdot\bBf=0$, $\bE^2=\bBf^2$ & Null field: equal, perpendicular fields.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Lorentz force from the action}

Let $q\in\R$ be charge and $m>0$ constant mass.  Recall the real
worldline quantities $U=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig)$ and
$P=mU=E+\bp\cdot\bsig$, with $E=m\gamma$ and $\bp=m\gamma\bv$.
The real particle Lagrangian $L$ is fixed by the action
\begin{equation}
 \int L\dd t=-m\int\dd s-q\int\scpart{\Phi^*U}\dd s.
 \label{eq:charged-particle-action}
\end{equation}
Since $\scpart{\Phi^*U}=\gamma(V-\bv\cdot\bA)$,
\eqref{eq:charged-particle-action} gives
\begin{equation}
 L=-m/\gamma-qV+q\bv\cdot\bA.
 \label{eq:charged-particle-lagrangian}
\end{equation}
The Euler--Lagrange terms of \eqref{eq:charged-particle-lagrangian} are
\begin{align*}
 \partial_{\bv}L&=\bp+q\bA,\\
 \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\partial_{\bv}L
 &=\frac{\dd\bp}{\dd t}+q\partial_t\bA+q(\bv\cdot\pdr)\bA,\\
 \pdr L&=-q\pdr V+q\pdr(\bv\cdot\bA).
\end{align*}
Here spatial differentiation holds the independent velocity argument fixed.
Using
$\pdr(\bv\cdot\bA)-(\bv\cdot\pdr)\bA
=\bv\times(\pdr\times\bA)$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd\bp}{\dd t}=q(\bE+\bv\times\bBf).
 \label{eq:lorentz-momentum}
\end{equation}
Here \eqref{eq:electric-potential} and \eqref{eq:magnetic-potential}
turn the potential derivatives into the fields of \eqref{eq:lorentz-momentum}.
The Hamiltonian and its balance law are
\begin{equation}
 H=\bv\cdot(\bp+q\bA)-L=E+qV.
 \label{eq:charged-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
Using \eqref{eq:hamiltonian-energy-balance} in \eqref{eq:charged-hamiltonian},
\begin{align*}
 \frac{\dd E}{\dd t}+q\partial_tV+q\bv\cdot\pdr V
 &=-\partial_tL=q\partial_tV-q\bv\cdot\partial_t\bA.
\end{align*}
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd E}{\dd t}=q\bv\cdot\bE.
 \label{eq:lorentz-energy}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:lorentz-energy} is the mechanical work rate.  Thus $\bp+q\bA$ and $H$ are canonical momentum and energy; $\bp,E$
are mechanical momentum and energy, with $E$ including rest energy.
Canonical energy is conserved for static potentials in the chosen gauge.
A time-dependent gauge transformation can change $H$ while leaving $E$
and the force unchanged.

Now expand the complex product of the field and proper velocity:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \frac{FU}{\gamma}={}&\bv\cdot\bE
   +(\bE+\bv\times\bBf)\cdot\bsig\\
 &+\ii\left(\bv\cdot\bBf
   +(\bBf-\bv\times\bE)\cdot\bsig\right).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:force-product}
\end{equation}
The real scalar and vector parts of \eqref{eq:force-product} reproduce
\eqref{eq:lorentz-energy} and \eqref{eq:lorentz-momentum}.
Since $P$ is real and $\dd/\dd s=\gamma\dd/\dd t$, they combine into
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\frac{\dd P}{\dd s}
 =q\repart{FU}=\frac q2(FU+UF^{\mathsf H}).}
 \label{eq:lorentz}
\end{equation}
Hermitian extraction in \eqref{eq:lorentz} enforces real mechanical
momentum and reproduces the action-derived force.  The imaginary part is the magnetic-dual force in
Section~\ref{sec:magnetic-dual-force}; it need not vanish for an electric charge.

\subsection{Acceleration and the mass shell}
\label{sec:lorentz-proper-acceleration}

Recall $P=mU$, $U^*U=1$, and real coordinate acceleration
$\ba=\dd\bv/\dd t$.  Scalar-part cyclicity proves tangency to the mass shell:
\begin{align*}
 \scpart{U^*\dd P/\dd s}
 &=\frac q2\scpart{U^*FU+F^{\mathsf H}}\\
 &=\frac q2\left(\scpart F+\scpart{F^{\mathsf H}}\right)=0.
\end{align*}
This agrees with $\scpart{U^*\dd U/\dd s}=0$ in
Section~\ref{sec:proper-acceleration}.
Combining \eqref{eq:proper-acceleration-components} with the force components
in \eqref{eq:force-product} gives
\begin{align*}
 m\gamma^3\bv\cdot\ba&=q\bv\cdot\bE,\\
 m\gamma\left(\ba+\gamma^2(\bv\cdot\ba)\bv\right)
 &=q(\bE+\bv\times\bBf).
\end{align*}
Eliminating $\bv\cdot\ba$ yields the coordinate acceleration
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\ba=\frac{q}{m\gamma}
 \left(\bE+\bv\times\bBf-(\bv\cdot\bE)\bv\right).}
 \label{eq:lorentz-coordinate-acceleration}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:lorentz-coordinate-acceleration} shows how the
magnetic term changes momentum without doing work.
Taking the determinant of the real force gives
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 -\det\left(\frac{\dd P}{\dd s}\right)
 &=-m^2\det\left(\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\right)\\
 &=q^2\gamma^2\left((\bE+\bv\times\bBf)^2-(\bv\cdot\bE)^2\right).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:proper-force-magnitude}
\end{equation}
Dividing \eqref{eq:proper-force-magnitude} by $m^2$ gives the invariant squared proper acceleration.
The factor $\gamma^2((\bE+\bv\times\bBf)^2-(\bv\cdot\bE)^2)$
is the squared electric field in the particle's instantaneous rest frame.

\subsection{Energy and momentum flux}

Recall $F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$.  Its Hermitian product is
\begin{equation}
 \tfrac12FF^{\mathsf H}
 =\tfrac12(\bE^2+\bBf^2)+(\bE\times\bBf)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:field-energy-flux}
\end{equation}
Define the real energy density $u=(\bE^2+\bBf^2)/2$ and real Poynting
vector $\bS=\bE\times\bBf$.  The coefficients of \eqref{eq:field-energy-flux} are frame-dependent,
whereas $F^2$ contains the Lorentz invariants.
The full stress--energy tensor is represented by the real linear map
\begin{equation}
 T(N):=\tfrac12FNF^{\mathsf H},
 \label{eq:electromagnetic-stress-map}
\end{equation}
with real paravector input $N$.  The time-direction value of \eqref{eq:electromagnetic-stress-map} is
$T(1)=u+\bS\cdot\bsig$.  It is future causal because $u\geq0$ and
\begin{equation}
 \det(T(1))=u^2-\bS^2=\tfrac14\lVert\det F\rVert^2\geq0.
 \label{eq:stress-causal-bound}
\end{equation}
Equality in \eqref{eq:stress-causal-bound} holds for null fields.  Reversing the order gives
$F^{\mathsf H}F=(FF^{\mathsf H})^*$: the same energy density and opposite
flux.  The transformation of the full map is derived in
Section~\ref{sec:transformations}.

Using Maxwell's vector equations,
\begin{align*}
 \partial_tu
 &=\bE\cdot(\pdr\times\bBf-\bJ)-\bBf\cdot(\pdr\times\bE)\\
 &=-\pdr\cdot\bS-\bJ\cdot\bE,
\end{align*}
where
$\pdr\cdot(\bE\times\bBf)
=\bBf\cdot(\pdr\times\bE)-\bE\cdot(\pdr\times\bBf)$.
Thus Poynting's energy-balance law is
\begin{equation}
 \partial_tu+\pdr\cdot\bS=-\bJ\cdot\bE.
 \label{eq:poynting}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the left side of \eqref{eq:poynting} is
$\tfrac12\partial_t\scpart{FF^{\mathsf H}}
+\tfrac12\pdr\cdot\vecpart{FF^{\mathsf H}}$.
The right side is the field's loss of energy to matter per unit volume.

\section{Relativistic wave equations}
\label{sec:waves}

Throughout this section, $X=t+\br\cdot\bsig$ has real coordinates
$(t,\br)\in\R^4$, $m\geq0$ is mass, $q\in\R$ is charge, and
$\hbar=c=1$.  Wave amplitudes are complex-valued functions of these real
coordinates; their scalar, ideal, or two-entry type is specified below.
Spin is fixed by the rotation law, not by the word ``paravector.''
We first identify the carriers and their probabilities, then derive the
free and coupled equations before their low-energy limits and applications.

\subsection{Spin and probability}
\label{sec:spin-and-probability}

\subsubsection{Pairing and observables}
\label{sec:paravector-inner-products}

Let $\psi,\psi'$ be complex paravector amplitudes and $Z$ a paravector
acting from the left.  Define
\begin{align}
 \langle\psi'\mid\psi\rangle
 &:=\scpart{\psi'^{\mathsf H}\psi},
 \label{eq:paravector-inner-product}\\
 \langle\psi'\mid Z\mid\psi\rangle
 &:=\scpart{\psi'^{\mathsf H}Z\psi}.
 \label{eq:paravector-matrix-element}
\end{align}
The pairing \eqref{eq:paravector-inner-product} defines the real norm density
\begin{equation}
 \rho:=\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}=\lVert\psi\rVert^2\geq0.
 \label{eq:probability-density}
\end{equation}
For a finite amplitude, \eqref{eq:probability-density} is its squared norm; for an
ideal wavefunction it is the local density.  Spatial normalization
means $\int\rho\,\dd^3\br=1$, not $\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi=1$.
The spatial norm is conserved by Pauli and, after summing both entries,
Dirac; local densities may vary.  The Klein--Gordon scalar uses a
different conserved pairing.

For $\rho>0$ and real $Z^{\mathsf H}=Z$, normalize the matrix element
\eqref{eq:paravector-matrix-element} to define the mean
\begin{equation}
 \langle Z\rangle_\psi
 :=\frac{\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}Z\psi}}{\rho}.
 \label{eq:paravector-expectation}
\end{equation}
For a spatial wavefunction in \eqref{eq:paravector-expectation}, integrate
numerator and denominator.  A
Hermitian differential operator can replace $Z$ when its derivatives act
to the right and boundary conditions justify integration by parts.
The Born rule supplies the probability
$\lVert\Pi\psi\rVert^2/\rho$ for an orthogonal projector $\Pi$; the
algebra evaluates this physical postulate.

If $\psi'=T\psi\ne0$ for a complex paravector $T$, then
\begin{equation}
 \langle Z\rangle_{\psi'}
 =\frac{\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}T^{\mathsf H}ZT\psi}}
 {\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}T^{\mathsf H}T\psi}}.
 \label{eq:bilinear-congruence}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:bilinear-congruence} shows that $T^{\mathsf H}T=1$
preserves the finite-amplitude norm.  A Lorentz
boost does not preserve this norm of spinor components: the invariant
Dirac normalization is current flux through a spacelike hypersurface,
including its transformed measure.

\subsubsection{How the spin number is defined}
\label{sec:paravector-spin-content}

Recall the real unit axis $\bu$, real angle $\theta$, and unitary rotor
$R=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ from
\eqref{eq:rotation-exponential}.  All intrinsic transformations below
compare field values at corresponding rotated points.  Rotating their
coordinate dependence additionally produces orbital angular momentum.

Define the intrinsic spin operator $S(\bu)$ by writing an infinitesimal
rotation as $1-\ii\theta S(\bu)+O(\theta^2)$ on the amplitude.  Here
$S(\bu)$ is a linear operator, whose action depends on the carrier.
With the real Cartesian elementary vectors $\bm e_1,\bm e_2,\bm e_3$,
write $S_k=S(\bm e_k)$ and $S^2=S_1^2+S_2^2+S_3^2$.
The rotation algebra gives $[S_1,S_2]=\ii S_3$ and cyclic permutations.
For spin number $s=0,1/2,1,\ldots$, an irreducible carrier has
\begin{equation}
 S^2=s(s+1).
 \label{eq:spin-casimir}
\end{equation}
Together with the rotation commutators, \eqref{eq:spin-casimir} gives
projections $-s,-s+1,\ldots,s$ along any axis, in units of $\hbar$.

\Needspace{18\baselineskip}
\subsubsection{Spin zero: a scalar amplitude}
\label{sec:spin-zero}

Let $\psi(\br,t)\in\C$ be a scalar field.  Its intrinsic rotation law is
\begin{equation}
 \psi'=\psi.
 \label{eq:spin-zero-law}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:spin-zero-law} gives $S(\bu)\psi=0$ for every axis
and $S^2\psi=0$, hence $s=0$ by \eqref{eq:spin-casimir}.
There is one complex amplitude and only the zero intrinsic projection;
spatial dependence can still carry orbital angular momentum.

A scalar KG field uses this law and stays scalar under boosts.
The KG equation alone does not fix spin: free Dirac components satisfy
the same equation but have the spinor rotation law.

\subsubsection{Spin one-half: a one-sided ideal amplitude}
\label{sec:spin-half}

For a complex ideal amplitude $\psi\Pi_3=\psi$, with fixed
$\Pi_3=(1+\sigma_3)/2$, the rotation acts from one side:
\begin{equation}
 \psi'=R^{\mathsf H}\psi.
 \label{eq:spin-half-law}
\end{equation}
Here $R=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$.
Expanding \eqref{eq:spin-half-law} identifies the generator; its square gives
\begin{align}
 S(\bu)\psi&=\tfrac12(\bu\cdot\bsig)\psi,
 \label{eq:spin-half-generator}\\
 S^2\psi&=\tfrac14(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2+\sigma_3^2)\psi
          =\tfrac34\psi.
 \label{eq:spin-half-square}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:spin-casimir} and \eqref{eq:spin-half-square} give
$s=1/2$.  Since $(\bu\cdot\bsig)^2=1$, the generator
\eqref{eq:spin-half-generator} has projections $\pm1/2$.  A $2\pi$ rotation gives
$R=-1$, reversing the amplitude's sign; a $4\pi$ rotation restores it.
The sign cancels in probabilities but relative phases remain observable
in interference.\cite{feynmanspin}

\subsubsection{Spin one: a two-sided vector amplitude}
\label{sec:spin-one}

Let $\bV(\br,t)\in\C^3$ be a complex vector field.  With
$R=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ its intrinsic law is
\begin{equation}
 \bV'\cdot\bsig=R^{\mathsf H}(\bV\cdot\bsig)R.
 \label{eq:spin-one-law}
\end{equation}
Expanding both factors in \eqref{eq:spin-one-law} gives
$\bV'=\bV+\theta\bu\times\bV+O(\theta^2)$, hence
\begin{align}
 S(\bu)\bV&=\ii\bu\times\bV,
 \label{eq:spin-one-generator}\\
 S^2\bV&=-\sum_{k=1}^3\bm e_k\times(\bm e_k\times\bV)=2\bV.
 \label{eq:spin-one-square}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:spin-casimir} and \eqref{eq:spin-one-square} give
$s=1$.  The third-axis eigenvectors of \eqref{eq:spin-one-generator} satisfy
\begin{align*}
 S_3\bm e_3&=0,\\
 S_3(\bm e_1\pm\ii\bm e_2)&=\pm(\bm e_1\pm\ii\bm e_2).
\end{align*}
The projections are $0,+1,-1$, and a $2\pi$ rotation restores the
amplitude.  A complex scalar plus this vector has separate spin-zero
and spin-one rotation parts.  A position or momentum paravector is a
four-vector observable, not a particle's spin amplitude.

The electromagnetic field $F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$ has this
spin-one rotation law, with real fields $\bE,\bBf$.  Maxwell's equations
constrain source-free radiation to be transverse.  The physical photon
has two helicities, $+1$ and $-1$, rather than three independent
polarizations.  A massive spin-one particle has three polarizations once
its field equation and constraint are imposed.  A classical field $F$
is not itself a normalized particle amplitude.

The scalar, spinor, electromagnetic field, and four-vector also have
distinct boost laws, derived in Section~\ref{sec:transformations}.

\subsubsection{Spinor components and probabilities}
\label{sec:physical-spinors}

Fix the real paravector $\Pi_3=(1+\sigma_3)/2$ on the right.  For
any complex paravector $Z$, set
\begin{equation}
 \psi=Z\Pi_3.
 \label{eq:ideal-amplitude}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ideal-amplitude} imposes $\psi\Pi_3=\psi$, preserved
by every left action since
$(Z'\psi)\Pi_3=Z'\psi$.  This minimal left ideal has two independent
complex components.  The fixed right projection selects one Pauli column;
a measured spin outcome is a separate left projection.  $Z$ is not unique.

Choose the following two constant, normalized ideal amplitudes:
\begin{equation}
 \{\psi_+,\psi_-\}:=\sqrt2\{\Pi_3,\sigma_1\Pi_3\}.
 \label{eq:ideal-spin-basis}
\end{equation}
The basis \eqref{eq:ideal-spin-basis} obeys $\sigma_3\psi_\pm=\pm\psi_\pm$ and
$\langle\psi_a\mid\psi_b\rangle=\delta_{ab}$ for $a,b\in\{+,-\}$.
The scalar coefficients $c_+,c_-\in\C$ give the full expansion
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \psi&=c_+\psi_++c_-\psi_-\\
 &=\frac{c_+(1+\sigma_3)+c_-(\sigma_1-\ii\sigma_2)}{\sqrt2}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:ideal-components}
\end{equation}
Thus \eqref{eq:probability-density} becomes
$\rho=\lVert c_+\rVert^2+\lVert c_-\rVert^2$.
For a wavefunction, $c_\pm=c_\pm(\br,t)$ are complex scalar functions.
Equation~\eqref{eq:ideal-components} encodes four paravector coefficients
using two independent complex components: the Pauli column entries in Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-spinors}.
Since $\sigma_1$ exchanges the basis, neither one-dimensional subspace
is invariant under all rotations.

For a real unit measurement axis $\bu$, the real paravector projectors are
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig).
 \label{eq:spin-projectors}
\end{equation}
The identities $\Pi_\pm^2=\Pi_\pm$, $\Pi_+\Pi_-=0$, and
$\Pi_++\Pi_-=1$ for \eqref{eq:spin-projectors} imply
\begin{align*}
 \psi&=\Pi_+\psi+\Pi_-\psi,\\
 \langle\Pi_+\psi\mid\Pi_-\psi\rangle&=0,\\
 S(\bu)(\Pi_\pm\psi)&=\pm\tfrac12\Pi_\pm\psi,\\
 \lVert\Pi_\pm\psi\rVert^2&=\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm\psi}.
\end{align*}
After a nonzero-probability outcome, the normalized amplitude is
$\Pi_\pm\psi/\lVert\Pi_\pm\psi\rVert$.
For the third axis, its probability is $\lVert c_\pm\rVert^2/\rho$.

\subsubsection{The real state paravector and probabilities}

For $\rho>0$, define the real paravector
\begin{equation}
 B:=\frac{\psi\psi^{\mathsf H}}{\rho}=1+\bm{s}\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:spin-state}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:spin-state}, $\bm{s}=\vecpart B\in\R^3$ is the dimensionless
Bloch polarization;
$\bm{s}/2$ is the mean spin.  Since $\det\Pi_3=0$,
\begin{equation}
 \det B=\frac{\lVert\det\psi\rVert^2}{\rho^2}=1-\bm{s}^2=0.
 \label{eq:spin-state-purity}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:spin-state-purity} gives $\bm{s}^2=1$ for a nonzero
ideal amplitude.  A statistical mixture $B=\sum_k w_k B_k$ of these
real pure-state paravectors, with real weights $w_k\geq0$ and
$\sum_k w_k=1$, has $\bm{s}^2\leq1$.  Conversely, every such real $B$ can be factored as
$\psi\psi^{\mathsf H}/\rho$ using an unrestricted complex paravector.
Its matrix image divided by two is the usual density matrix.
The resulting ball of polarizations is state geometry: $\det B=0$
means a pure spin state, not a massless particle.

Cyclicity of $\scpart{\cdot}$ gives
\begin{align*}
 \lVert\Pi_\pm\psi\rVert^2
 &=\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm\psi}
  =\rho\,\scpart{\Pi_\pm B},\\
 \scpart{\Pi_\pm B}&=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bm{s}).
\end{align*}
Hence the Born probabilities are
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{p_\pm(\bu)=\frac{\lVert\Pi_\pm\psi\rVert^2}{\rho}
                    =\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bm{s}).}
 \label{eq:spin-probability-intrinsic}
\end{equation}
For a wavefunction, \eqref{eq:spin-probability-intrinsic} is conditional
on position.  A detector collecting the whole packet measures
\begin{equation}
 p_\pm(\bu)=
 \frac{\displaystyle\int\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm\psi}\,\dd^3\br}
 {\displaystyle\int\rho\,\dd^3\br}.
 \label{eq:spin-probability-wavepacket}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:spin-probability-wavepacket} weights each local spin
fraction by its density.  A real observable $Z'=a+\bb\cdot\bsig$, with real scalar $a$
and real vector $\bb$, has possible values $a\pm\lVert\bb\rVert$ and mean
\begin{equation}
 \langle Z'\rangle_\psi=\scpart{Z'B}=a+\bb\cdot\bm{s}.
 \label{eq:state-expectation}
\end{equation}
For \eqref{eq:state-expectation}, use $\bu=\bb/\lVert\bb\rVert$ when
$\bb\ne0$ to obtain its outcome probabilities from
\eqref{eq:spin-probability-intrinsic}.

The amplitude law \eqref{eq:spin-half-law}, with
$R=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$, gives
\begin{equation}
 B'=R^{\mathsf H}BR.
 \label{eq:spin-state-rotation}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:spin-state-rotation} rotates the polarization as a
vector while the amplitude has spin one-half.  An unrestricted paravector
under the one-sided law contains two spin-one-half copies; the ideal
selects one.  Dirac couples two ideals with the same rotation law and
opposite boost laws.

\subsubsection{Spin uncertainty}
For real unit axes $\bu,\bu'$, set $A=\bu\cdot\bsig/2$ and
$A'=\bu'\cdot\bsig/2$.  Define the real variance by
$\operatorname{var}A=\langle A^2\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2$.
The product rule gives
\begin{align*}
 [A,A']&=\frac{\ii}{2}(\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 \operatorname{var}A&=\frac{1-(\bu\cdot\bm{s})^2}{4}.
\end{align*}
For normalized $\psi$, positivity of
$\lVert(A-\langle A\rangle)\psi+\ii\lambda(A'-\langle A'\rangle)\psi\rVert^2$
for every real auxiliary scalar $\lambda$ makes its quadratic discriminant
nonpositive.  Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\operatorname{var}A\;\operatorname{var}A'
 \geq\tfrac14\lVert\langle[A,A']\rangle\rVert^2
 =\frac{[(\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bm{s}]^2}{16}.}
 \label{eq:spin-uncertainty}
\end{equation}
The Robertson bound \eqref{eq:spin-uncertainty} also holds for mixtures by the same
positive-pairing argument.

\subsection{Energy and momentum as differential operators}
\label{sec:quantum-energy-momentum}

Recall $X=t+\br\cdot\bsig$, with real coordinates $(t,\br)\in\R^4$.
For a plane wave let $P=E+\bp\cdot\bsig$ be a constant real
four-momentum: $E\in\R$ is its energy and $\bp\in\R^3$ its momentum.
The real phase and complex scalar wave are
\begin{align}
 f(X)&:=\scpart{\adj P\,X}=Et-\bp\cdot\br,
 \label{eq:plane-wave-phase}\\
 w(X)&:=\exp(-\ii f(X)).
 \label{eq:plane-wave}
\end{align}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:plane-wave}, with phase
\eqref{eq:plane-wave-phase}, gives the translation eigenvalues:
\begin{align*}
 \ii\partial_t w&=Ew,\\
 -\ii\pdr w&=\bp w,\\
 \ii\partial w&=\adj P\,w.
\end{align*}
Their scalar and vector parts combine into one paravector equation,
\begin{equation}
 \ii\partial^*w=Pw.
 \label{eq:momentum-operator-eigenvalues}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:momentum-operator-eigenvalues} identifies
$\scpart{\ii\partial^*}=\ii\partial_t$ and
$\vecpart{\ii\partial^*}=-\ii\pdr$ as the energy and momentum operators.
Extraction here precedes their action on a wave.  On the spatially
integrated pairing, $-\ii\pdr$ is Hermitian when boundary terms vanish.
An energy spectrum is real when the wave equation has a Hermitian
Hamiltonian $H$ in $\ii\partial_t\bPsi=H\bPsi$.

In a field, recall the real potential paravector
$\Phi(t,\br)=V(t,\br)+\bA(t,\br)\cdot\bsig$:
$V$ is a real scalar potential and $\bA$ a real vector potential.
The real charge is $q$.  The particle action gives canonical energy
$E+qV$ and momentum $\bp+q\bA$; identifying them with translation
operators defines the gauge-covariant mechanical operators
\begin{align}
 E&:=\ii\partial_t-qV,
 \label{eq:mechanical-energy-operator}\\
 \bp&:=-\ii\pdr-q\bA.
 \label{eq:mechanical-momentum-operator}
\end{align}
In \eqref{eq:mechanical-energy-operator}, $E$ includes rest energy;
$\ii\partial_t$ is canonical energy.  The operator
\eqref{eq:mechanical-momentum-operator} is mechanical momentum.
Below, $E,\bp$ denote these scalar and vector differential operators
unless a real mode eigenvalue is specified.  They act to the right and form
\begin{align}
 E-\bp\cdot\bsig&=\ii\partial-q\Phi^*,
 \label{eq:covariant-momentum-adjugate}\\
 E+\bp\cdot\bsig&=\ii\partial^*-q\Phi.
 \label{eq:covariant-momentum-paravectors}
\end{align}
The components of \eqref{eq:covariant-momentum-adjugate} and
\eqref{eq:covariant-momentum-paravectors} need not commute; their ordered
product cannot be replaced by an ordinary paravector determinant.  For free
operators they commute, and $\Box=\partial_t^2-\pdr^2$ gives
\begin{equation}
 (\ii\partial)(\ii\partial^*)=-\Box.
 \label{eq:free-momentum-product}
\end{equation}
Reversing the order in \eqref{eq:free-momentum-product} gives the same result.

\subsubsection{Field strength from noncommuting derivatives}
Recall the real electric and magnetic fields from
\eqref{eq:electric-potential} and \eqref{eq:magnetic-potential}, and
$S=\partial_tV+\pdr\cdot\bA$, the real scalar in
\eqref{eq:potential-field}.  The real potential is $\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$;
$F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$ is a complex vector paravector.

Apply the operators to a differentiable complex amplitude.
The product rule cancels all derivatives of the amplitude in the
commutators; for Cartesian indices $k,l\in\{1,2,3\}$,
\begin{align*}
 [E,p_k]&=-\ii q(\partial_tA_k+\partial_kV)=\ii qE_k,\\
 [p_k,p_l]&=\ii q(\partial_kA_l-\partial_lA_k),\\
 \bp\times\bp&=\ii q\bBf.
\end{align*}
Sigma multiplication and the energy commutator consequently give
\begin{equation}
 (\bp\cdot\bsig)^2=\bp^2-q\bBf\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:kinetic-sigma-square}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 [E,\bp\cdot\bsig]=\ii q\bE\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:kinetic-electric-commutator}
\end{equation}
Here $E_k$ is an electric-field component.  Using
\eqref{eq:kinetic-sigma-square} and \eqref{eq:kinetic-electric-commutator}
in the ordered products gives
\begin{align}
 (E+\bp\cdot\bsig)(E-\bp\cdot\bsig)
 &=E^2-(\bp\cdot\bsig)^2-[E,\bp\cdot\bsig]\notag\\
 &=E^2-\bp^2-\ii qF,
 \label{eq:covariant-product-plus}\\[0.35em]
 (E-\bp\cdot\bsig)(E+\bp\cdot\bsig)
 &=E^2-(\bp\cdot\bsig)^2+[E,\bp\cdot\bsig]\notag\\
 &=E^2-\bp^2-\ii qF^*.
 \label{eq:covariant-product-minus}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:covariant-product-plus} and
\eqref{eq:covariant-product-minus} share scalar part $E^2-\bp^2$;
their vector remainders are
$-\ii qF$ and $-\ii qF^*$.  This separation will distinguish the scalar
KG equation from the squared spinor equation.


\subsection{Klein--Gordon equation}

Let $\psi:\R^4\to\C$ be a scalar field and $m>0$ its real mass.
For a free real four-momentum, $\det P=m^2$ means
$E^2-\bp^2=m^2$.  Replacing its coefficients by the translation
operators in \eqref{eq:momentum-operator-eigenvalues} gives
\[
 \bigl((\ii\partial)(\ii\partial^*)-m^2\bigr)\psi=0.
\]
Using \eqref{eq:free-momentum-product}, this is the Klein--Gordon equation:
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{(\Box+m^2)\psi=0.}
 \label{eq:kg}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Free solutions and initial data}
To solve \eqref{eq:kg}, define the positive spatial operator
$H=\sqrt{m^2-\pdr^2}$ by its Fourier symbol.  Here $\bk\in\R^3$ is the wavevector and $E(\bk)>0$ the
mode energy, not a differential operator:
\begin{align*}
 E(\bk)&:=\sqrt{m^2+\bk^2},\\
 H\exp(\ii\bk\cdot\br)&=E(\bk)\exp(\ii\bk\cdot\br).
\end{align*}
\begin{equation}
 \Box+m^2=(\partial_t-\ii H)(\partial_t+\ii H).
 \label{eq:kg-frequency-factorization}
\end{equation}
By \eqref{eq:kg-frequency-factorization}, each Fourier coefficient
satisfies the oscillator equation
$\partial_t^2\psi+E(\bk)^2\psi=0$.  Its two solutions give
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\psi(t,\br)=\int\frac{\dd^3\bk}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}
 \left(a(\bk)e^{-\ii E(\bk)t}+b(\bk)e^{\ii E(\bk)t}\right)
 e^{\ii\bk\cdot\br}.}
 \label{eq:kg-general-solution}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:kg-general-solution}, the complex scalar functions
$a,b:\R^3\to\C$ are fixed by the initial
field and its first time derivative.  To display this directly, write
$\psi=\psi_++\psi_-$, where the complex scalar fields $\psi_\pm$ obey
\begin{align*}
 (\partial_t\pm\ii H)\psi_\pm&=0,\\
 \psi_\pm(0,\br)&=\tfrac12\left(\psi(0,\br)
             \pm\ii H^{-1}\partial_t\psi(0,\br)\right),\\
 \psi_\pm(t,\br)&=e^{\mp\ii Ht}\psi_\pm(0,\br).
\end{align*}
Equivalently, arbitrary initial data evolve by
\begin{equation}
 \psi(t,\br)=\cos(Ht)\psi(0,\br)
       +H^{-1}\sin(Ht)\partial_t\psi(0,\br).
 \label{eq:kg-initial-value-solution}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:kg-initial-value-solution} reconstructs the field from
its value and time derivative.  The square root acts by Fourier multiplication.
The original $\sqrt\Box$ factorization follows likewise, using real frequency $\omega$:
\[
 \Box e^{\ii(\omega t+\bk\cdot\br)}
   =(\bk^2-\omega^2)e^{\ii(\omega t+\bk\cdot\br)}.
\]
\begin{equation}
 \Box+m^2=(m-\ii\sqrt\Box)(m+\ii\sqrt\Box).
 \label{eq:kg-root-factorization}
\end{equation}
Choose one branch of $\sqrt{\bk^2-\omega^2}$ consistently in
\eqref{eq:kg-root-factorization}.
This operator is distinct from the positive $H$; the two on-shell
frequencies are $\omega=\pm E(\bk)$~\cite{tong-qft}.

\subsubsection{Potential and conserved current}
Recall the real potentials $V,\bA$, charge $q$, and kinetic operators
$E=\ii\partial_t-qV$, $\bp=-\ii\pdr-q\bA$.
The scalar equation in a field is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{(-E^2+\bp^2+m^2)\psi=0.}
 \label{eq:kg-field}
\end{equation}
Indeed, the scalar part of either ordered product
$(E-\bp\cdot\bsig)(E+\bp\cdot\bsig)$ is $E^2-\bp^2$.
The vector remainder acts on spinor amplitudes in the Dirac factorization.

The product rule separately gives
\begin{align}
 E^2&=-\partial_t^2-\ii q(\partial_tV)
       -2\ii qV\partial_t+q^2V^2,
 \label{eq:energy-operator-square}\\
 \bp^2&=-\pdr^2+\ii q(\pdr\cdot\bA)
       +2\ii q\bA\cdot\pdr+q^2\bA^2.
 \label{eq:momentum-operator-square}
\end{align}
Substituting \eqref{eq:energy-operator-square} and
\eqref{eq:momentum-operator-square} into \eqref{eq:kg-field} gives
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\left(\Box+\ii qS-q^2\det\Phi+m^2
 +2\ii q(V\partial_t+\bA\cdot\pdr)\right)\psi=0.}
 \label{eq:kg-potential-expanded}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:kg-potential-expanded} retains every derivative of
the potential.  Its real current is $J=\rho+\bJ\cdot\bsig$, where
$\rho:\R^4\to\R$ is its signed density and
$\bJ:\R^4\to\R^3$ its spatial flux:
\begin{equation}
 J:=\frac1m\repart{\psi^{\mathsf H}
       (E+\bp\cdot\bsig)\psi}.
 \label{eq:kg-current}
\end{equation}
Extracting \eqref{eq:kg-current} gives
\begin{align}
 \rho&=\frac{\ii}{2m}\left(\psi^{\mathsf H}\partial_t\psi
       -(\partial_t\psi)^{\mathsf H}\psi\right)
       -\frac{qV}{m}\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi,
 \label{eq:kg-density}\\
 \bJ&=-\frac{\ii}{2m}\left(\psi^{\mathsf H}\pdr\psi
       -(\pdr\psi)^{\mathsf H}\psi\right)
       -\frac{q\bA}{m}\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi.
 \label{eq:kg-flux}
\end{align}
For the continuity check of \eqref{eq:kg-density} and \eqref{eq:kg-flux}, abbreviate the scalar wave operator as
$Q=-E^2+\bp^2+m^2$.  The product rule cancels the products of first
derivatives, leaving
\begin{align*}
 \partial_t\rho+\pdr\cdot\bJ
 &=\frac{\ii}{2m}\left(\psi^{\mathsf H}Q\psi
                      -(Q\psi)^{\mathsf H}\psi\right).
\end{align*}
\begin{equation}
 \scpart{\partial J}=\partial_t\rho+\pdr\cdot\bJ=0.
 \label{eq:kg-continuity}
\end{equation}
To interpret the conserved current \eqref{eq:kg-continuity}, take free
waves $\psi=a\exp(\mp\ii\scpart{\adj P\,X})$,
where $a\in\C$ is constant and $P$ has positive energy,
\begin{equation}
 J=\pm\frac{\lVert a\rVert^2}{m}P.
 \label{eq:kg-plane-current}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:kg-plane-current} identifies $q\rho$ as charge density;
$\rho$ is not a positive probability
density for arbitrary KG fields.  For the positive-frequency sector
($b=0$ in \eqref{eq:kg-general-solution}), Parseval's identity gives
\begin{equation}
 \int\rho\,\dd^3\br
   =\int\frac{E(\bk)}m\lVert a(\bk)\rVert^2\dd^3\bk>0
 \label{eq:kg-frequency-norm}
\end{equation}
for a nonzero field.  The pairing \eqref{eq:kg-frequency-norm} is positive
even though the
local density of a superposition need not be.  A general time-dependent
potential need not preserve this sector.  The full particle--antiparticle
interpretation requires the quantized scalar field; the positive Pauli
and Dirac density cannot simply be assigned to a KG amplitude.

\subsubsection{Schr\"odinger limit}

Recall $m>0$, real $V,\bA,q$, and the kinetic operators $E,\bp$ above.
Let $\psi'$ be the relativistic complex scalar field.  Its envelope
$\psi:\R^4\to\C$ removes the rest-energy phase:
\begin{equation}
 \psi'(t,\br)=e^{-\ii mt}\psi(t,\br).
 \label{eq:kg-rest-phase}
\end{equation}
Under \eqref{eq:kg-rest-phase}, $E$ acting on $\psi$ measures energy above rest:
$E\psi'=e^{-\ii mt}(E+m)\psi$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:kg-field} becomes
\begin{align*}
 (-E^2-2mE+\bp^2)\psi&=0,\\
 E\psi&=\frac{\bp^2-E^2}{2m}\psi.
\end{align*}
On the free positive-frequency branch the commuting operators give
\begin{equation}
 E\psi=\left(\sqrt{m^2+\bp^2}-m\right)\psi.
 \label{eq:kg-positive-residual-energy}
\end{equation}
The small-momentum expansion of \eqref{eq:kg-positive-residual-energy} is
\begin{equation}
 E\psi=\left(\frac{\bp^2}{2m}-\frac{\bp^4}{8m^3}+\cdots\right)\psi.
 \label{eq:kg-low-energy-expansion}
\end{equation}
More generally, when residual energies and momenta are small compared
with $m$, dropping $E^2\psi$ at leading order gives Schr\"odinger's equation:
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\ii\partial_t\psi=\left(
 \frac{(-\ii\pdr-q\bA)^2}{2m}+qV\right)\psi.}
 \label{eq:schrodinger}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:schrodinger} is the leading scalar limit.  The higher
terms of \eqref{eq:kg-low-energy-expansion} assume commuting operators
and cannot be imported unchanged into arbitrary time-dependent fields.

\subsection{Dirac equation}
\label{sec:dirac-development}

\subsubsection{Block form and free factorization}

Recall $m>0$, the real space--time coordinates $(t,\br)$, and the fixed
idempotent $\Pi_3=(1+\sigma_3)/2$.  A Dirac field is the two-entry block
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi(t,\br)=\begin{Bmatrix}\Psi_1(t,\br)\\\Psi_2(t,\br)\end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:dirac-block}
\end{equation}
Its entries satisfy $\Psi_k\Pi_3=\Psi_k$ for $k=1,2$.
Each complex ideal entry in \eqref{eq:dirac-block} has two independent
complex components, so the block has four.
On blocks, $^{\mathsf H}$ transposes entries and applies the existing
Hermitian operation to each; $^*$ acts entrywise without transposing.
For two such fields, $\bPsi$ and $\bPsi'$, the local pairing is
\begin{equation}
 \langle\bPsi'\mid\bPsi\rangle
 :=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\scpart{\Psi_k'^{\mathsf H}\Psi_k}.
 \label{eq:dirac-inner-product}
\end{equation}
The pairing \eqref{eq:dirac-inner-product} gives the positive real density
\begin{equation}
 \lVert\bPsi\rVert^2
 =\scpart{\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}\Psi_1}
 +\scpart{\Psi_2^{\mathsf H}\Psi_2}.
 \label{eq:dirac-norm}
\end{equation}

For an algebraic complex paravector $Z$, define the block map
\begin{equation}
 \Wop(Z):=\begin{Bmatrix}0&Z\\\adj Z&0\end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:dirac-block-map}
\end{equation}
Here $\bI$ is the two-entry block identity.  Multiplying
\eqref{eq:dirac-block-map} gives
\begin{equation}
 \Wop(Z)^2=\begin{Bmatrix}Z\adj Z&0\\0&\adj Z\,Z\end{Bmatrix}
            =\det(Z)\bI.
 \label{eq:dirac-block-square}
\end{equation}
Entrywise conjugation gives $\Wop(Z)^*=\Wop(Z^*)$; the Hermitian operation also transposes:
\begin{equation}
 \Wop(Z)^{\mathsf H}=\Wop(Z^*).
 \label{eq:dirac-block-adjoint}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:dirac-block-square} and \eqref{eq:dirac-block-adjoint}
are algebraic identities.  Differential Hermiticity additionally
requires integration by parts; $\Wop(Z)$ is not generally Hermitian.
For the free field define the operator blocks
\begin{align*}
 \bDm&:=\Wop(\ii\partial)-m\bI,\\
 \bDm'&:=\Wop(\ii\partial)+m\bI.
\end{align*}
The Dirac equation is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\bDm\bPsi=0.}
 \label{eq:dirac}
\end{equation}
Since $\Wop(\ii\partial)^2=-\Box\bI$, its mass conjugate gives
\begin{equation}
 \bDm'\bDm=\Wop(\ii\partial)^2-m^2\bI
            =-(\Box+m^2)\bI.
 \label{eq:dirac-free-factorization}
\end{equation}
Applying \eqref{eq:dirac-free-factorization} to \eqref{eq:dirac} gives
$(\Box+m^2)\bPsi=0$: every Dirac component satisfies KG.  The original first-order
constraint is the coupled pair
\begin{align}
 \ii\partial\Psi_2&=m\Psi_1,\label{eq:dirac-free-first}\\
 \ii\partial^*\Psi_1&=m\Psi_2.\label{eq:dirac-free-second}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Reconstructing free solutions from Klein--Gordon}
Let $\psi':\R^4\to\C$ solve KG.  For constant complex scalars $a,b$,
the original two-constant construction is
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi=\frac1m
 \begin{Bmatrix}
  ma+\ii b\partial\\
  \ii a\partial^*+mb
 \end{Bmatrix}\psi'\Pi_3.
 \label{eq:kg-dirac-general-lift}
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:kg-dirac-general-lift} into
\eqref{eq:dirac-free-first} and \eqref{eq:dirac-free-second} gives
\begin{align*}
 \ii\partial\Psi_2-m\Psi_1
    &=-\frac{a}{m}(\Box+m^2)\psi'\Pi_3=0,\\
 \ii\partial^*\Psi_1-m\Psi_2
    &=-\frac{b}{m}(\Box+m^2)\psi'\Pi_3=0.
\end{align*}
Taking $a=1,b=0$ gives the scalar-seeded solution.  One scalar seed with
two constants does not span every Dirac field.  For the complete converse,
let $\psi:\R^4\to\C+\C^3\cdot\bsig$ be an ideal-valued field
($\psi\Pi_3=\psi$) whose two complex components each solve KG.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\bPsi=\begin{Bmatrix}\psi\\\ii\partial^*\psi/m\end{Bmatrix}.}
 \label{eq:kg-dirac-complete-lift}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:kg-dirac-complete-lift}, the second Dirac equation holds by
construction and the first reduces to KG.  Conversely, every massive
free solution has this form with
$\psi=\Psi_1$.  Applying \eqref{eq:kg-general-solution} to its two
coefficients therefore supplies arbitrary initial data.

For completeness, on a Fourier mode the ordinary $4\times4$ matrix of
$\bDm$ has characteristic polynomial
\begin{equation}
 \det\bigl(\operatorname{mat}(\bDm)-sI_4\bigr)
       =\bigl((s+m)^2+\Box\bigr)^2.
 \label{eq:dirac-characteristic}
\end{equation}
Here $s\in\C$ is the spectral parameter, $I_4$ the four-dimensional
identity, and $\Box$ denotes its scalar Fourier eigenvalue.  Equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-characteristic} has two
roots $s=-m\pm\ii\sqrt\Box$ each have multiplicity two; treating the
paravector block as an ordinary scalar $2\times2$ matrix loses this factor.

\subsubsection{Potential and paravector components}
Recall the real $\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$, real charge $q$, and
$E=\ii\partial_t-qV$, $\bp=-\ii\pdr-q\bA$.  Minimal coupling gives
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\bigl(\Wop(\ii\partial-q\Phi^*)-m\bI\bigr)\bPsi=0.}
 \label{eq:dirac-field}
\end{equation}
The entries of \eqref{eq:dirac-field} are
\begin{align}
 (E-\bp\cdot\bsig)\Psi_2&=m\Psi_1,\label{eq:dirac-entry-one}\\
 (E+\bp\cdot\bsig)\Psi_1&=m\Psi_2.\label{eq:dirac-entry-two}
\end{align}
For a real scalar gauge function $\lambda:\R^4\to\R$, the product
rule preserves \eqref{eq:dirac-entry-one} and \eqref{eq:dirac-entry-two}
under $\Phi'=\Phi+\partial^*\lambda$ and
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi'=e^{-\ii q\lambda}\bPsi.
 \label{eq:dirac-gauge-amplitude}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-gauge-amplitude} changes the local phase only.
To expose each coefficient, write
$\Psi_k=a_k+\bb_k\cdot\bsig$ for $k=1,2$, with complex scalar
functions $a_k:\R^4\to\C$ and complex vector functions
$\bb_k:\R^4\to\C^3$.  Sigma multiplication gives
\begin{align}
 Ea_2-\bp\cdot\bb_2&=ma_1,\label{eq:dirac-scalar-one}\\
 E\bb_2-\bp a_2-\ii\bp\times\bb_2&=m\bb_1,\label{eq:dirac-vector-one}\\
 Ea_1+\bp\cdot\bb_1&=ma_2,\label{eq:dirac-scalar-two}\\
 E\bb_1+\bp a_1+\ii\bp\times\bb_1&=m\bb_2.\label{eq:dirac-vector-two}
\end{align}
The scalar equations \eqref{eq:dirac-scalar-one}, \eqref{eq:dirac-scalar-two}
and vector equations \eqref{eq:dirac-vector-one}, \eqref{eq:dirac-vector-two}
act on the coefficient to their right.
The ideal condition imposes $b_{k3}=a_k$ and $b_{k2}=-\ii b_{k1}$;
the independent coefficients are $a_1,b_{11},a_2,b_{21}$.
Their four complex equations are exactly the conventional Dirac system;
Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-spinors} supplies the column representation.

\subsubsection{Hamiltonian, density, and flux}
Isolating the time derivative gives
$\ii\partial_t\bPsi=H\bPsi$, where the Hamiltonian block is
\begin{equation}
 H:=qV\bI+\begin{Bmatrix}
  -\bp\cdot\bsig&m\\m&\bp\cdot\bsig
 \end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:dirac-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
Equivalently $H=qV\bI+\Wop(1)(m\bI+\Wop(\bp\cdot\bsig))$.
Real potentials and vanishing boundary terms make
\eqref{eq:dirac-hamiltonian} Hermitian in the
integrated pairing, so it conserves the norm \eqref{eq:dirac-norm}.

Define the real probability-current paravector
$J=\rho+\bJ\cdot\bsig$ by
\begin{equation}
 J:=\adj{\Psi_1\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}}+\Psi_2\Psi_2^{\mathsf H}.
 \label{eq:dirac-current}
\end{equation}
Extracting \eqref{eq:dirac-current} gives its nonnegative density
$\rho:\R^4\to\R$ and spatial probability flux $\bJ:\R^4\to\R^3$:
\begin{align*}
 \rho&=\scpart{\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}\Psi_1}
       +\scpart{\Psi_2^{\mathsf H}\Psi_2},\\
 \bJ&=-\vecpart{\Psi_1\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}}
       +\vecpart{\Psi_2\Psi_2^{\mathsf H}}.
\end{align*}
The component equations and their Hermitian forms give
\begin{align*}
 \partial_t\lVert\Psi_1\rVert^2
 -\pdr\cdot\vecpart{\Psi_1\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}}
 &=2m\impart{\scpart{\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}\Psi_2}},\\
 \partial_t\lVert\Psi_2\rVert^2
 +\pdr\cdot\vecpart{\Psi_2\Psi_2^{\mathsf H}}
 &=-2m\impart{\scpart{\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}\Psi_2}}.
\end{align*}
The mass transfers density between entries; summing cancels the transfer:
\begin{equation}
 \scpart{\partial J}=\partial_t\rho+\pdr\cdot\bJ=0.
 \label{eq:dirac-continuity}
\end{equation}
The conserved current \eqref{eq:dirac-continuity} is a sum of two
future-null terms by the ideal condition.  Thus $J$ is real
and future causal, as the determinant confirms:
\begin{equation}
 \det J=\rho^2-\bJ^2
       =4\left\lVert\scpart{\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}\Psi_2}\right\rVert^2
       \geq0.
 \label{eq:dirac-current-causal}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-current-causal} bounds the probability-flow
velocity $\bJ/\rho$ by one wherever $\rho>0$.  Normalize by $\int\rho\,\dd^3\br=1$.
More generally, for a spacelike hypersurface let $N$ be its future-pointing
real unit normal ($\det N=1$) and $\dd A$ its real invariant volume element.
The Lorentz-invariant normalization is
\begin{equation}
 \int\scpart{\adj N\,J}\,\dd A=1.
 \label{eq:dirac-hypersurface-norm}
\end{equation}
On a constant-time surface $N=1$, so
\eqref{eq:dirac-hypersurface-norm} recovers the spatial integral.

\subsubsection{Free Dirac modes and spin probabilities}
\label{sec:dirac-spin-energy}

Let $\bp\in\R^3$ be fixed momentum and $m>0$ the mass.  Define the
positive real energy $E=\sqrt{\bp^2+m^2}$.
Set $V=\bA=0$ in the Hamiltonian \eqref{eq:dirac-hamiltonian}
and replace the momentum operator by this mode's real $\bp$.
Squaring cancels the off-diagonal entries:
$H^2=(\bp^2+m^2)\bI=E^2\bI$, with $\bI$ the block identity.
Its orthogonal energy projectors are therefore
\begin{equation}
 \bQm_\pm:=\tfrac12(\bI\pm H/E).
 \label{eq:dirac-energy-projectors}
\end{equation}
The projectors \eqref{eq:dirac-energy-projectors} obey $\bQm_\pm^2=\bQm_\pm=\bQm_\pm^{\mathsf H}$,
$\bQm_+\bQm_-=0$, and $\bQm_++\bQm_-=\bI$.  For a nonzero finite amplitude,
\begin{equation}
 p_\pm=\frac{\lVert\bQm_\pm\bPsi\rVert^2}{\lVert\bPsi\rVert^2}.
 \label{eq:dirac-energy-probability}
\end{equation}
For a packet in \eqref{eq:dirac-energy-probability}, let $\bPsi(\bp,t)$
denote its spatial Fourier transform:
apply $\bQm_\pm(\bp)$ at each real momentum and integrate numerator
and denominator over $\dd^3\bp$.  In position space
$E=\sqrt{-\pdr^2+m^2}$ makes the projectors nonlocal.

\paragraph{Constructing the normalized modes.}
At rest, $H=m\Wop(1)$, and the energy sign $a=\pm1$ requires
$\Psi_2=a\Psi_1$.  With the constant third-axis basis $\psi_\pm$ of
\eqref{eq:ideal-spin-basis}, the four orthonormal rest amplitudes are
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi_{ab}(0)=\frac1{\sqrt2}\{\psi_b,a\psi_b\}^{\mathsf T}.
 \label{eq:dirac-rest-modes}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:dirac-rest-modes}, $a,b\in\{+1,-1\}$: $a$ labels energy
sign and $b/2$ spin along the chosen axis.
Neither original entry by itself labels energy or spin.

For arbitrary momentum define the real paravector
$A=E+m+\bp\cdot\bsig$.  Its adjugate is
$\adj A=E+m-\bp\cdot\bsig$, and direct multiplication gives
\begin{align*}
 A\adj A&=(E+m)^2-\bp^2=2m(E+m),\\
 A^2+(\adj A)^2&=2[(E+m)^2+\bp^2]=4E(E+m).
\end{align*}
The second identity and $A\adj A=\adj A A$ prove that the block map
\begin{equation}
 \bT:=\frac1{2\sqrt{E(E+m)}}
 \begin{Bmatrix}\adj A&A\\A&-\adj A\end{Bmatrix}
 \label{eq:free-fw-map}
\end{equation}
is Hermitian and unitary: in \eqref{eq:free-fw-map}, $\bT^{\mathsf H}=\bT$ and $\bT^2=\bI$.
The eigenvalue equations follow component by component:
\begin{align*}
 -(\bp\cdot\bsig)\adj A+mA&=E\adj A,\\
 m\adj A+(\bp\cdot\bsig)A&=EA,\\
 -(\bp\cdot\bsig)A-m\adj A&=-EA,\\
 mA-(\bp\cdot\bsig)\adj A&=E\adj A.
\end{align*}
Thus the first column has energy $+E$ and the second has energy $-E$:
\begin{equation}
 \bT^{\mathsf H}H\bT=\begin{Bmatrix}E&0\\0&-E\end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:free-fw-diagonalization}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:free-fw-diagonalization} is the free
Foldy--Wouthuysen change of representation in the chiral
basis.  It preserves positive norms and is distinct from a Lorentz
boost.\cite{foldy-wouthuysen}

Now choose any real unit rest-spin axis $\bu$.  In the following formulas,
$\psi_b$ ($b=\pm1$) denotes a constant normalized ideal amplitude with
$(\bu\cdot\bsig)\psi_b=b\psi_b$.
Project the fixed $\sqrt2\Pi_3$ using $\Pi_b=(1+b\bu\cdot\bsig)/2$
and normalize.  Its squared norm is $(1+b u_3)/2$; when zero, project
$\sqrt2\sigma_1\Pi_3$ instead.
The resulting pair is orthonormal and recovers the third-axis basis at
$\bu=\bm e_3$.

The four normalized momentum modes are
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi_{+b}(\bp)
 =\frac{\{\adj A\,\psi_b,A\psi_b\}^{\mathsf T}}{2\sqrt{E(E+m)}}.
 \label{eq:dirac-positive-mode}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi_{-b}(\bp)
 =\frac{\{A\psi_b,-\adj A\,\psi_b\}^{\mathsf T}}{2\sqrt{E(E+m)}}.
 \label{eq:dirac-negative-mode}
\end{equation}
Unitarity of $\bT$ proves normalization, orthogonality, and completeness
of \eqref{eq:dirac-positive-mode} and \eqref{eq:dirac-negative-mode}.
Multiplying by $e^{-\ii aEt+\ii\bp\cdot\br}$ gives the space--time mode,
with the same spatial momentum for both energy signs.  Hence every free
packet is
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi(\br,t)=\sum_{a,b=\pm1}\int\frac{\dd^3\bp}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}
 c_{ab}(\bp)\bPsi_{ab}(\bp)e^{-\ii aEt+\ii\bp\cdot\br}.
 \label{eq:dirac-free-packet}
\end{equation}
Here $c_{ab}:\R^3\to\C$ are scalar Fourier amplitudes.  Spatial unit
normalization is exactly
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{a,b}\int\lVert c_{ab}(\bp)\rVert^2\dd^3\bp=1.
 \label{eq:dirac-packet-normalization}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-packet-normalization} normalizes the packet
\eqref{eq:dirac-free-packet}; each individual plane wave instead has unit
finite spinor norm and an
infinite spatial integral.  The sum/difference basis in
Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-dirac-components} gives the familiar large and
small components proportional to $\psi_b$ and
$(\bp\cdot\bsig)\psi_b/(E+m)$.

For $m=0$ and $\bp\ne0$, set $E=\lVert\bp\rVert$ and
$\bu=\bp/E$.  The two chiral equations decouple.  The complete normalized
pair at energy $aE$ is now simply
\[
 \{\psi_{-a},0\}^{\mathsf T},\qquad
 \{0,\psi_a\}^{\mathsf T}.
\]
The first entry has helicity $-a/2$, the second $a/2$; helicity means
$S(\bu)$ along momentum.  At nonzero mass an entry has no definite
helicity in general.  For the exceptional $m=0,\bp=0$ mode, $H=0$:
all four constant amplitudes have zero energy, without a preferred
helicity or positive/negative split.

\paragraph{Rotation spin and conserved rest spin.}
For $\rho=\lVert\bPsi\rVert^2>0$, the rotation generator $\bsig/2$
on each entry has the reduced real state paravector
\begin{equation}
 B:=\frac{\Psi_1\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}+\Psi_2\Psi_2^{\mathsf H}}{\rho}
   =1+\bm{s}\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:dirac-spin-state}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:dirac-spin-state}, $\bm{s}(\br,t)\in\R^3$ is the polarization and $\bm{s}/2$ the
mean rotation spin.  Summing over the two entries can give $\bm{s}^2<1$ even for a pure
Dirac amplitude.  The current $J$ instead adjugates its first term and
encodes probability flow; neither reduced paravector specifies the full state.

For a real unit axis $\bu$, let $\Pi_\pm=(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig)/2$
act on each entry.  The local spin fractions are
\begin{equation}
 \scpart{\Pi_\pm B}
 =\frac{\lVert\Pi_\pm\Psi_1\rVert^2+\lVert\Pi_\pm\Psi_2\rVert^2}{\rho}
 =\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bm{s}).
 \label{eq:dirac-rotation-spin-probability}
\end{equation}
For a spatial packet, integrate the numerator and denominator of
\eqref{eq:dirac-rotation-spin-probability}.
At finite momentum the rotation generator differs from conserved rest
spin; a detector's interaction determines which operator it measures.

At each fixed momentum put $\bPsi'=\bT^{\mathsf H}\bPsi$.
Here $\bPsi'$ is the same finite amplitude in the diagonal representation,
and $\rho=\lVert\bPsi\rVert^2=\lVert\bPsi'\rVert^2$ is its real norm.
Energy sign is now the entry label.  The rest-spin operator is
$\bu\cdot\bsig/2$ on either entry, so its projectors in the original
representation are
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_b':=\bT\begin{Bmatrix}\Pi_b&0\\0&\Pi_b\end{Bmatrix}\bT^{\mathsf H}.
 \label{eq:dirac-rest-spin-projectors}
\end{equation}
The projectors \eqref{eq:dirac-rest-spin-projectors} obey $\Pi_b'^2=\Pi_b'=\Pi_b'^{\mathsf H}$ and $[H,\Pi_b']=0$.
Unitary conjugation preserves the Pauli commutators and squared spin
$3/4$.  This is the conserved Foldy--Wouthuysen mean spin for
rotation-free rest axes; the construction here assumes $m>0$.
\cite{caban2013spin}

Since energy and rest-spin projectors commute, their four joint
probabilities are
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{p_{ab}=\frac{\lVert\bQm_a\Pi_b'\bPsi\rVert^2}{\rho}.}
 \label{eq:dirac-joint-probability}
\end{equation}
In the diagonal representation, \eqref{eq:dirac-joint-probability} becomes
\begin{align*}
 p_{+b}&=\frac{\lVert\Pi_b\Psi_1'\rVert^2}{\rho},\\
 p_{-b}&=\frac{\lVert\Pi_b\Psi_2'\rVert^2}{\rho},\\
 \sum_{a,b}p_{ab}&=1.
\end{align*}
For a wave packet, integrate numerator and denominator over momentum.
The normalized expansion \eqref{eq:dirac-free-packet} gives joint weights
$\int\lVert c_{ab}(\bp)\rVert^2\dd^3\bp$ directly.

At a fixed momentum the real rest-spin state paravector is
\begin{equation}
 B'=\frac{\Psi_1'\Psi_1'^{\mathsf H}+\Psi_2'\Psi_2'^{\mathsf H}}{\rho}
    =1+\bm{s}'\cdot\bsig,
 \label{eq:dirac-rest-spin-state}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:dirac-rest-spin-state}, $\bm{s}'\in\R^3$ is its rest polarization.  Summing over energy
gives $p_b=(1+b\bu\cdot\bm{s}')/2$.
For a pure energy eigenmode, use
$(\bp\cdot\bsig)(\bm{s}'\cdot\bsig)(\bp\cdot\bsig)
=2(\bp\cdot\bm{s}')\bp\cdot\bsig-\bp^2\bm{s}'\cdot\bsig$
in the two mode products.  Their linear cross terms cancel, giving
\begin{equation}
 \bm{s}=\frac{m}{E}\bm{s}'
 +\frac{\bp(\bp\cdot\bm{s}')}{E(E+m)}.
 \label{eq:dirac-spin-polarization}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-spin-polarization} leaves parallel polarization
unchanged; transverse polarization in $B$
is reduced by $m/E$.  The full state remains pure and its spin remains
one-half.  Only the reduced rotation-spin state appears mixed.

The untransformed $\Pi_b$ instead measures the Dirac rotation generator.
Acting on both entries, its commutator with energy is
\begin{equation}
 [H,\Pi_b]
 =\begin{Bmatrix}
 -\ii b(\bp\times\bu)\cdot\bsig&0\\
 0&\ii b(\bp\times\bu)\cdot\bsig
 \end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:dirac-spin-energy-commutator}
\end{equation}
The commutator \eqref{eq:dirac-spin-energy-commutator} vanishes at rest
or along $\bp$; there $\Pi_b$ equals the rest-spin projector.
Along nonzero momentum it selects helicity.  Otherwise
$\bQm_a\Pi_b$ is not a joint orthogonal projector.
For ideal sequential measurements, let $p(a,b)$ mean energy $a$ followed
by rotation spin $b$, and $p'(a,b)$ the reverse order:
\begin{align}
 p(a,b)&=\frac{\lVert\Pi_b\bQm_a\bPsi\rVert^2}{\rho},
 \label{eq:sequential-energy-spin-joint}\\
 p'(a,b)&=\frac{\lVert\bQm_a\Pi_b\bPsi\rVert^2}{\rho}.
 \label{eq:sequential-spin-energy-joint}
\end{align}
The joint probabilities \eqref{eq:sequential-energy-spin-joint} and
\eqref{eq:sequential-spin-energy-joint} need not agree.
Conditioning \eqref{eq:sequential-energy-spin-joint} on a nonzero energy outcome gives
\begin{equation}
 p(b\mid a)=\frac{\lVert\Pi_b\bQm_a\bPsi\rVert^2}
 {\lVert\bQm_a\bPsi\rVert^2}.
 \label{eq:sequential-spin-energy-probability}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:sequential-spin-energy-probability} is the joint
probability divided by the probability of the first outcome.
The slow Stern--Gerlach experiment of Section~\ref{sec:stern-gerlach}
measures the ordinary Pauli component in its stated approximation.

\paragraph{Covariant idempotents and charge reversal.}

Recall the future real momentum $P=E+\bp\cdot\bsig$, real mass $m>0$,
and real unit-determinant paravector $U=P/m$.
The real phase is $f(\br,t)=Et-\bp\cdot\br$.
Since $\det U=1$, the block $\Wop(\adj U)$ squares to $\bI$.
Consequently $(\bI\pm\Wop(\adj U))/2$ are idempotents.
Their $+$ eigenspace solves the Dirac equation with phase $e^{-\ii f}$;
the $-$ eigenspace uses $e^{+\ii f}$, with spatial momentum $-\bp$.
The latter corresponds to $\bQm_-(-\bp)$.
Away from rest,
\begin{equation}
 \Wop(\adj U)^{\mathsf H}=\Wop(U)\ne\Wop(\adj U).
 \label{eq:dirac-covariant-adjoint}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-covariant-adjoint} shows why these idempotents are
not Hermitian away from rest.  Born probabilities use squared norms of
the orthogonal $\bQm_\pm$ projections, divided by the initial squared norm.

For a charge-$q$ solution, define its charge-conjugate complex ideal block
using a prime locally:
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi':=\{\Psi_2^*\sigma_1,-\Psi_1^*\sigma_1\}^{\mathsf T}.
 \label{eq:dirac-charge-reversal}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-charge-reversal} solves the charge-$(-q)$ equation.  Indeed,
$\Pi_3^*\sigma_1=\sigma_1\Pi_3$ keeps both entries in the fixed ideal;
applying $^*$ to the coupled equations reverses $\ii$ and the sign of
the charge coupling.  It also reverses the phase, while
$\Pi_\pm^*=\Pi_\mp$ reverses the rotation-spin label.
A negative-frequency charge-$q$ mode thereby becomes a positive-frequency
charge-$(-q)$ mode, with spin evaluated on the resulting amplitude.
After field quantization the original negative-frequency coefficients
become antiparticle creation amplitudes.  Negative energy by itself
is not an antimatter probability.

Proper orthochronous Lorentz transformations preserve the two free mass
shells.  A static external field instead uses the full Hermitian
Hamiltonian's spectrum.  A time-dependent field need not preserve a fixed
energy split; a pair-production interpretation requires field quantization.

\subsubsection{Interacting factorization and reconstruction}
\label{sec:interacting-factorization}

Recall the real potential $\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$, the complex field
$F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$, and
$S=\partial_tV+\pdr\cdot\bA$.
The operators $E,\bp$ and complex ideal entries of $\bPsi$ retain their
preceding meanings.  The scalar part of their ordered product gave KG;
Dirac retains its vector spin coupling as well.

For any complex paravector amplitude $\psi(t,\br)$ the same calculation
can be kept in paravector form:
\begin{align}
 \partial^*(\Phi^*\psi)+\Phi\partial\psi
 &=(S+F)\psi+2(V\partial_t+\bA\cdot\pdr)\psi,
 \label{eq:potential-product-one}\\
 \partial(\Phi\psi)+\Phi^*\partial^*\psi
 &=(S+F^*)\psi+2(V\partial_t+\bA\cdot\pdr)\psi.
 \label{eq:potential-product-two}
\end{align}
The difference of \eqref{eq:potential-product-one} and
\eqref{eq:potential-product-two} is $(F-F^*)\psi=2(\bE\cdot\bsig)\psi$.

For the block map $\Wop$, direct multiplication gives
\begin{align*}
 \Wop(\partial)\Wop(\Phi^*)
   &=\begin{Bmatrix}\partial\Phi&0\\0&\partial^*\Phi^*\end{Bmatrix},\\
 \Wop(\Phi^*)\Wop(\partial)
   &=\begin{Bmatrix}\Phi^*\partial^*&0\\0&\Phi\partial\end{Bmatrix},\\
 \Wop(\Phi^*)^2&=\det\Phi\,\bI.
\end{align*}
Here $\partial\Phi$ denotes composition: on a test field it is
$\partial(\Phi\psi)$, including the product rule.
Define the interacting mass-conjugate blocks
\begin{align*}
 \bDm&:=\Wop(\ii\partial-q\Phi^*)-m\bI,\\
 \bDm'&:=\Wop(\ii\partial-q\Phi^*)+m\bI.
\end{align*}
Multiplying and using the preceding mixed-derivative identities gives
\begin{equation}
 \bDm'\bDm=
 -\begin{Bmatrix}
  -E^2+\bp^2+m^2+\ii qF^*&0\\
  0&-E^2+\bp^2+m^2+\ii qF
 \end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:dirac-field-factorization}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-field-factorization} recovers
\eqref{eq:dirac-free-factorization} at $q=0$.  With coupling, its entries obey
\begin{align*}
 \left((\partial+\ii q\Phi^*)(\partial^*+\ii q\Phi)+m^2\right)\Psi_1&=0,\\
 \left((\partial^*+\ii q\Phi)(\partial+\ii q\Phi^*)+m^2\right)\Psi_2&=0.
\end{align*}
Expanding gives the exact second-order equations
\begin{align}
 (-E^2+\bp^2+m^2+\ii qF^*)\Psi_1&=0,
 \label{eq:dirac-squared-first}\\
 (-E^2+\bp^2+m^2+\ii qF)\Psi_2&=0.
 \label{eq:dirac-squared-field}
\end{align}
The spin terms in \eqref{eq:dirac-squared-first} and
\eqref{eq:dirac-squared-field} also have an explicit coefficient check.  For this calculation only, write
$\bC=\bE+\ii\bBf$ and $\psi=a+\bb\cdot\bsig$, where
$a:\R^4\to\C$ and $\bb,\bC:\R^4\to\C^3$ are complex coefficient
functions.  The star on $\bC$ conjugates its coefficients.  Then
\begin{align}
 \ii qF\psi
 &=\ii q\bC\cdot\bb+
   (\ii qa\bC-q\bC\times\bb)\cdot\bsig,
 \label{eq:spin-field-components}\\
 \ii qF^*\psi
 &=-\ii q\bC^*\cdot\bb+
   (-\ii qa\bC^*+q\bC^*\times\bb)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:spin-field-star-components}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:spin-field-components} and
\eqref{eq:spin-field-star-components} display the scalar and vector
couplings separately.

To construct solutions, take a complex ideal field $\psi$ satisfying
$(-E^2+\bp^2+m^2+\ii qF)\psi=0$ and $\psi\Pi_3=\psi$.  Set
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\bPsi=\begin{Bmatrix}(E-\bp\cdot\bsig)\psi/m\\\psi\end{Bmatrix}.}
 \label{eq:dirac-field-reconstruction}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:dirac-field-reconstruction}, the first Dirac equation holds
by construction; the second is the seed equation.  Every massive solution has this form with $\psi=\Psi_2$.
Independent squared entries need not satisfy their first-order coupling,
and a scalar KG seed generally lacks the required $\ii qF$ term.
Arbitrary potentials still require solving their initial or boundary
problem; this formula reconstructs Dirac from the correct seed.

\paragraph{Closing the matter--field system.}
Let $J$ be the real Dirac current \eqref{eq:dirac-current} and
$D=qJ$ the real electric source paravector.  With
$F^*=\partial\Phi-\scpart{\partial\Phi}$, the coupled Maxwell equation is
\begin{equation}
 \partial F=(qJ)^*.
 \label{eq:maxwell-dirac-source}
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{eq:maxwell-dirac-source} with \eqref{eq:dirac-field} gives
$\scpart{\partial D}=q\scpart{\partial J}=0$, exactly the source
consistency condition.  Replacing the Dirac current by
\eqref{eq:kg-current} gives Maxwell--KG.  These are classical field
systems; photons, particle creation, and radiative corrections require
quantum field theory.

\subsubsection{Schr\"odinger--Pauli limit}

Recall $m>0$, real charge $q$, real potentials $V,\bA$, and the
kinetic operators $E=\ii\partial_t-qV$, $\bp=-\ii\pdr-q\bA$.
The complex field $F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$ enters through
\eqref{eq:kinetic-sigma-square} and \eqref{eq:kinetic-electric-commutator}.
Remove the rest phase of the positive-energy Dirac field by defining
complex ideal envelopes $\Psi_k'(t,\br)$ for $k=1,2$:
\begin{equation}
 \Psi_k':=e^{\ii mt}\Psi_k.
 \label{eq:dirac-rest-phase}
\end{equation}
The product rule gives
\begin{align*}
 E\Psi_k&=e^{-\ii mt}(E+m)\Psi_k',\\
 \bp\Psi_k&=e^{-\ii mt}\bp\Psi_k'.
\end{align*}
On the envelopes \eqref{eq:dirac-rest-phase}, $E$ measures energy above rest.  Cancelling the
common phase gives
\begin{align*}
 (E+m-\bp\cdot\bsig)\Psi_2'&=m\Psi_1',\\
 (E+m+\bp\cdot\bsig)\Psi_1'&=m\Psi_2'.
\end{align*}
Eliminating $\Psi_1'$ retains both parts of $F$:
\begin{equation}
 \left(E^2+2mE-\bp^2+q\bBf\cdot\bsig
                   -\ii q\bE\cdot\bsig\right)\Psi_2'=0.
 \label{eq:dirac-residual-squared}
\end{equation}
The imaginary electric term belongs to the second-order equation
\eqref{eq:dirac-residual-squared};
$\Psi_2'$ alone is not a normalized first-order Pauli state.

Define the complex ideal amplitudes $\phi,\chi$ by the sum and difference
of the envelopes:
\begin{align}
 \phi&:=\frac{\Psi_1'+\Psi_2'}{\sqrt2},\label{eq:dirac-large-amplitude}\\
 \chi&:=\frac{\Psi_2'-\Psi_1'}{\sqrt2}.\label{eq:dirac-small-amplitude}
\end{align}
Substituting \eqref{eq:dirac-large-amplitude} and
\eqref{eq:dirac-small-amplitude} gives the exact pair
\begin{equation}
 E\phi=(\bp\cdot\bsig)\chi,
 \label{eq:dirac-large-component}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 (E+2m)\chi=(\bp\cdot\bsig)\phi.
 \label{eq:dirac-large-small}
\end{equation}
The norm is $\int(\lVert\phi\rVert^2+\lVert\chi\rVert^2)\dd^3\br$.
For residual energies and momenta small compared with $m$,
\eqref{eq:dirac-large-component} and \eqref{eq:dirac-large-small} give
\begin{align*}
 \chi&\simeq\frac{\bp\cdot\bsig}{2m}\phi,\\
 E\phi&\simeq\frac{(\bp\cdot\bsig)^2}{2m}\phi.
\end{align*}
Thus $\chi$ is small and the leading amplitude $\phi$ obeys Pauli's equation:
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\ii\partial_t\phi=\left(
  \frac{(-\ii\pdr-q\bA)^2}{2m}+qV
  -\frac{q}{2m}\bBf\cdot\bsig\right)\phi.}
 \label{eq:pauli}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:pauli}, $\phi\Pi_3=\phi$ and
$\int\scpart{\phi^{\mathsf H}\phi}\dd^3\br=1$ at leading order:
$\phi$ is the Pauli amplitude denoted by $\psi$ in the spin discussion.
Its two complex coefficients in the normalized ideal basis
$\psi_+,\psi_-$ are precisely the conventional Pauli column.  Since spin is $\bsig/2$, the magnetic energy is
$-(q/m)\bBf\cdot(\bsig/2)$: the magnetic moment is $q/m$ times spin,
and the gyromagnetic factor is $2$ before radiative corrections.

For a nonzero static uniform field, define the real unit vector
$\bu=\bBf/\lVert\bBf\rVert$ and projectors
$\Pi_\pm=(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig)/2$.  The projected ideal fields
$\phi_\pm=\Pi_\pm\phi$ obey separate equations:
\begin{equation}
 \ii\partial_t\phi_\pm=\left(
 \frac{\bp^2\mp q\lVert\bBf\rVert}{2m}+qV\right)\phi_\pm.
 \label{eq:pauli-fixed-spin-sectors}
\end{equation}
The separation \eqref{eq:pauli-fixed-spin-sectors} uses a fixed direction.
For a varying direction the projectors are functions of $(t,\br)$.
Writing $H$ for the Pauli Hamiltonian in \eqref{eq:pauli}, differentiation gives
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \ii\partial_t(\Pi_\pm\phi)
 &=\ii(\partial_t\Pi_\pm)\phi+\Pi_\pm H\phi\\
 &=H(\Pi_\pm\phi)
   +\left(\ii\partial_t\Pi_\pm
          +\frac{[\Pi_\pm,\bp^2]}{2m}\right)\phi.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:pauli-varying-spin-sectors}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:pauli-varying-spin-sectors}, only projector derivatives and the kinetic commutator couple the sectors;
the magnetic term commutes with its own projectors.

\subsubsection{First relativistic corrections}
\label{sec:relativistic-corrections}

Recall the complex ideal amplitudes $\phi,\chi$ in
\eqref{eq:dirac-large-small}, real $m>0,q,V,\bA$, and
$E=\ii\partial_t-qV$, $\bp=-\ii\pdr-q\bA$.
Assume static potentials and a positive-energy state separated from the
negative-energy sector.  Use a small real counting parameter $v$ with
\[
 \bp/m=O(v),\qquad qV/m=O(v^2),\qquad q\bBf/m^2=O(v^2).
\]
Spatial variation on scales $1/(mv)$ or longer then gives
$q\bE/m^2=O(v^3)$.  This is a smooth-field low-velocity expansion.

Iteration of the small-component equation preserves operator order:
\begin{align*}
 \chi&=\frac{(\bp\cdot\bsig)\phi}{2m}
       -\frac{E(\bp\cdot\bsig)\phi}{4m^2}+O(v^5\phi),\\
 E\phi&=\frac{(\bp\cdot\bsig)^2\phi}{2m}
       -\frac{(\bp\cdot\bsig)E(\bp\cdot\bsig)\phi}{4m^2}
       +O(mv^6\phi).
\end{align*}
For static potentials,
$E(\bp\cdot\bsig)=(\bp\cdot\bsig)E+[\bp\cdot\bsig,qV]$.
Using the leading $E\phi=(\bp\cdot\bsig)^2\phi/(2m)$ gives
\begin{equation}
 E\phi=\left(\frac{(\bp\cdot\bsig)^2}{2m}
       -\frac{(\bp\cdot\bsig)^4}{8m^3}
       -\frac{(\bp\cdot\bsig)[\bp\cdot\bsig,qV]}{4m^2}
       \right)\phi+O(mv^6\phi).
 \label{eq:dirac-unnormalized-envelope}
\end{equation}
The last term in \eqref{eq:dirac-unnormalized-envelope} appears
non-Hermitian because $\phi$ alone does not yet
carry the full conserved norm:
\begin{equation}
 \int(\lVert\phi\rVert^2+\lVert\chi\rVert^2)\dd^3\br
 =\int\scpart{\phi^{\mathsf H}
       \left(1+\frac{(\bp\cdot\bsig)^2}{4m^2}\right)\phi}
       \dd^3\br+O(v^4).
 \label{eq:dirac-envelope-norm}
\end{equation}
Define the normalized complex ideal amplitude
\begin{equation}
 \phi':=\left(1+\frac{(\bp\cdot\bsig)^2}{8m^2}\right)\phi
          +O(v^4\phi).
 \label{eq:pauli-normalized-envelope}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:pauli-normalized-envelope} absorbs the norm correction
\eqref{eq:dirac-envelope-norm}.  Conjugating the evolution operator adds
$[(\bp\cdot\bsig)^2,qV]/(8m^2)$, combining the electric terms into a
Hermitian double commutator.  In $\ii\partial_t\phi'=H\phi'$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 H={}&qV+\frac{(\bp\cdot\bsig)^2}{2m}
       -\frac{(\bp\cdot\bsig)^4}{8m^3}\\
    &-\frac{[\bp\cdot\bsig,[\bp\cdot\bsig,qV]]}{8m^2}
       +O(mv^6).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:pauli-correction-commutator}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:pauli-correction-commutator} is the static
Foldy--Wouthuysen positive-energy Hamiltonian through
order $mv^4$~\cite{foldy-wouthuysen}.  Direct sigma multiplication gives
\[
 [\bp\cdot\bsig,qV]=\ii q\bE\cdot\bsig.
\]
\begin{equation}
 [\bp\cdot\bsig,[\bp\cdot\bsig,qV]]
 =q\pdr\cdot\bE
   +q(\bE\times\bp-\bp\times\bE)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:pauli-double-commutator}
\end{equation}
The ordered cross products in \eqref{eq:pauli-double-commutator} act on everything to their right; their
difference is Hermitian.  The explicit result is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\begin{aligned}
 H={}&qV+\frac{\bp^2-q\bBf\cdot\bsig}{2m}
       -\frac{(\bp^2-q\bBf\cdot\bsig)^2}{8m^3}\\
 &-\frac{q}{8m^2}\pdr\cdot\bE
  -\frac{q}{8m^2}(\bE\times\bp-\bp\times\bE)\cdot\bsig
  +O(mv^6).
 \end{aligned}}
 \label{eq:pauli-relativistic-corrections}
\end{equation}
The square in \eqref{eq:pauli-relativistic-corrections} retains the order
of all noncommuting factors.  For $\bA=0$,
the three corrections are
\begin{align*}
 \text{relativistic kinetic energy:}&\quad-\frac{\bp^4}{8m^3},\\
 \text{Darwin term:}&\quad\frac{q}{8m^2}\pdr^2V,\\
 \text{spin--orbit term:}&\quad
       \frac{q}{4m^2}(\pdr V\times\bp)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align*}
For a real central potential $V(r)$, with $r=\lVert\br\rVert$,
the orbital operator $\bm L=\br\times\bp$ and spin operator
$\bm S=\bsig/2$ put the last term in its familiar form
\begin{equation}
 \frac{q}{4m^2}(\pdr V\times\bp)\cdot\bsig
 =\frac{q}{2m^2r}\frac{\dd V}{\dd r}\,\bm L\cdot\bm S.
 \label{eq:spin-orbit-central}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:spin-orbit-central} includes the relativistic factor of one half.  The gyromagnetic
factor remains $2$ to this order; its anomaly requires radiative corrections.
The imaginary electric part of the exact squared Dirac equation has
become Hermitian electric-field corrections after transforming both the
state and its norm.

\subsection{Worked solutions}
\label{sec:quantum-worked-solutions}

\subsubsection{Stern--Gerlach separation and spin precession}
\label{sec:stern-gerlach}

Let $m>0$ be an atom's mass and $\mu\in\R$ its signed magnetic-moment
coefficient: within an isolated spin-one-half doublet its moment operator
is $\mu\bsig$.  Let $\psi(\br,t)\Pi_3=\psi(\br,t)$ be its complex ideal
wavefunction and $\bBf(\br)\in\R^3$ the magnetic field.  The effective
Pauli Hamiltonian is the Hermitian differential operator
\begin{equation}
 H=-\frac{\pdr^2}{2m}-\mu\bBf\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:stern-gerlach-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
With \eqref{eq:stern-gerlach-hamiltonian}, slow center-of-mass motion
obeys $\ii\partial_t\psi=H\psi$.
Other internal levels are neglected.  A neutral atom can have
$\mu\ne0$ because its constituents carry magnetic moments.  For an
elementary Dirac charge, equation~\eqref{eq:pauli} gives
$\mu=q/(2m)$ with orbital electromagnetic coupling.

Use a local vacuum field with real gradient $G$ and bias $B_0>0$:
\begin{equation}
 \bBf=\{-Gx,0,B_0+Gz\}.
 \label{eq:stern-gerlach-field}
\end{equation}
Here $\br=\{x,y,z\}$.  Both the divergence and curl of
\eqref{eq:stern-gerlach-field} vanish.  For a beam along $y$ assume
$|Gx|,|Gz|\ll B_0$ in the magnet.  The spin axis is then approximately
fixed along $z$, while its longitudinal gradient supplies the force.
If the direction varies along a trajectory, the additional adiabatic
condition
$\lVert\dd(\bBf/\lVert\bBf\rVert)/\dd t\rVert\ll2|\mu|B_0$
suppresses transitions between its local spin states.

Let the initial wavefunction be $\psi(\br,0)=f(\br)\psi_0$, where
$f:\R^3\to\C$ has unit spatial norm and $\psi_0$ is a constant ideal
amplitude with $\lVert\psi_0\rVert=1$.
With $\Pi_\pm=(1\pm\sigma_3)/2$, write
\begin{equation}
 \psi(\br,t)=f_+(\br,t)\Pi_+\psi_0+f_-(\br,t)\Pi_-\psi_0.
 \label{eq:stern-gerlach-spin-split}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:stern-gerlach-spin-split}, the complex scalar functions
$f_\pm:\R^3\times\R\to\C$ each start
from $f$.  Substitution and $\sigma_3\Pi_\pm=\pm\Pi_\pm$ give
\begin{equation}
 \ii\partial_t f_\pm
 =\left(-\frac{\pdr^2}{2m}\mp\mu B_0\mp\mu Gz\right)f_\pm.
 \label{eq:stern-gerlach-components}
\end{equation}
Let $f'(\br,t)$ solve the free scalar Schr\"odinger equation with the
same initial function.  To solve \eqref{eq:stern-gerlach-components},
translate its argument to cancel the derivative
term from a linear phase; the remaining constant term fixes the cubic
phase.  Explicit substitution gives
\begin{equation}
 f_\pm(\br,t)=
 \exp\!\left(\pm\ii\mu B_0t\pm\ii\mu Gtz
              -\frac{\ii\mu^2G^2t^3}{6m}\right)
 f'\!\left(x,y,z\mp\frac{\mu Gt^2}{2m},t\right).
 \label{eq:stern-gerlach-packets}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:stern-gerlach-packets} gives momentum kicks
$\pm\mu Gt$ and center displacements
are $\pm\mu Gt^2/(2m)$ during transit.  Subsequent free flight adds
$\pm\mu Gt\,T/m$ for flight time $T$.  Each scalar packet retains unit
norm.  With the initial real state paravector
$B=\psi_0\psi_0^{\mathsf H}=1+\bm{s}\cdot\bsig$, resolved spots have
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{p_\pm=\lVert\Pi_\pm\psi_0\rVert^2=\tfrac12(1\pm s_3).}
 \label{eq:stern-gerlach-probabilities}
\end{equation}
The probabilities \eqref{eq:stern-gerlach-probabilities} depend on the
initial spin; the sign of $\mu G$ decides which label bends upward.  After selecting
spin $+1/2$ along $\bu$, an analyzer along $\bu'$ gives
$(1\pm\bu\cdot\bu')/2$, or $\cos^2(\theta/2)$ and
$\sin^2(\theta/2)$ at mutual angle $\theta$.\cite{mitspin}

For a uniform field $\bBf=B_0\bu$, there is no separation.  Factoring out
free spatial motion leaves the exact spin solution
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \psi(t)&=\exp(\ii\mu B_0t\,\bu\cdot\bsig)\psi(0)\\
 &=\left[\cos(\mu B_0t)+\ii\bu\cdot\bsig\sin(\mu B_0t)\right]\psi(0).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:spin-precession-amplitude}
\end{equation}
Using \eqref{eq:spin-precession-amplitude} to differentiate $B=\psi\psi^{\mathsf H}/\rho$ gives
$\partial_tB=\ii\mu[\bBf\cdot\bsig,B]$, whose vector part is
\begin{equation}
 \partial_t\bm{s}=2\mu\bm{s}\times\bBf.
 \label{eq:spin-precession-vector}
\end{equation}
For initial polarization along $x$ and field along $z$,
\eqref{eq:spin-precession-vector} gives
\begin{equation}
 \bm{s}(t)=\{\cos(2\mu B_0t),-\sin(2\mu B_0t),0\}.
 \label{eq:spin-precession-example}
\end{equation}
An $x$ analyzer applied to \eqref{eq:spin-precession-example} therefore
gives $p_+(t)=\cos^2(\mu B_0t)$.

\subsubsection{Exact solutions in constant potentials}
\label{sec:constant-potential-solutions}

Let $V\in\R$ and $\bA\in\R^3$ be constant potentials, with real
$m>0,q$.  Their field is $F=0$.  For real canonical frequency
$\omega$ and wavevector $\bk\in\R^3$, take the complex scalar mode
\[
 w(t,\br)=e^{-\ii\omega t+\ii\bk\cdot\br}.
\]
Its real kinetic momentum is $\bp=\bk-q\bA$; the kinetic operators act as
\begin{align*}
 (\ii\partial_t-qV)w&=(\omega-qV)w,\\
 (-\ii\pdr-q\bA)w&=\bp w.
\end{align*}
The mass shell gives $(\omega-qV)^2=m^2+\bp^2$, hence both frequencies:
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\omega=qV\pm\sqrt{m^2+(\bk-q\bA)^2}.}
 \label{eq:constant-potential-energy}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:constant-potential-energy}, $\omega$ is canonical energy;
$\omega-qV$ is mechanical energy,
including rest energy.
Fourier superposition with arbitrary complex coefficients gives every KG
solution, as in \eqref{eq:kg-general-solution}.  For Dirac, each branch has
the two spin modes derived in Section~\ref{sec:dirac-spin-energy}, with
real kinetic momentum $\bk-q\bA$.

Completeness also follows directly by gauge transformation.  On simply
connected space set the real function $\lambda=-Vt+\bA\cdot\br$.
The primed potential $\Phi'=\Phi+\partial^*\lambda$ vanishes, and
arbitrary free fields $\psi',\bPsi'$ give
\begin{align}
 \psi(t,\br)&=e^{-\ii qVt+\ii q\bA\cdot\br}\psi'(t,\br),
 \label{eq:constant-potential-scalar}\\
 \bPsi(t,\br)&=e^{-\ii qVt+\ii q\bA\cdot\br}\bPsi'(t,\br).
 \label{eq:constant-potential-dirac}
\end{align}
The maps \eqref{eq:constant-potential-scalar} and
\eqref{eq:constant-potential-dirac} are invertible and preserve currents
and probabilities.
Constant potentials shift canonical labels and phases; they leave the
kinetic mass shell unchanged.

\subsubsection{Exact magnetic levels: Klein--Gordon, Dirac, and Pauli}
\label{sec:landau-solutions}

Let $\br=\{x,y,z\}\in\R^3$ and prescribe the real uniform magnetic
field $\bBf=\{0,0,B\}$ with $B>0$.  Choose real potentials
$\bA=\{0,Bx,0\}$ and constant $V=V_0$.
Take real $m>0,q\ne0$, and define the positive scalar $d=|qB|$.
The real labels $k_2,k_3$ are canonical momenta along $y,z$.
On a mode with factor $e^{\ii(k_2y+k_3z)}$, the kinetic momentum square is
\[
 \bp^2=-\partial_x^2+(k_2-qBx)^2+k_3^2.
\]
Define the real dimensionless coordinate
$u=\sqrt d\,(x-k_2/(qB))$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \bp^2=d(-\partial_u^2+u^2)+k_3^2.
 \label{eq:landau-oscillator-operator}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Oscillator functions and scalar modes.}
The oscillator in \eqref{eq:landau-oscillator-operator} has
$[\partial_u+u,u-\partial_u]=2$.  This gives the real normalized
functions $f_N:\R\to\R$, with integer $N=0,1,2,\ldots$:
\[
 f_0(u)=\pi^{-1/4}e^{-u^2/2}.
\]
\begin{equation}
 f_N(u)=\frac{(u-\partial_u)^N}{\sqrt{2^N N!}}f_0(u).
 \label{eq:landau-oscillator-functions}
\end{equation}
Raising the index in \eqref{eq:landau-oscillator-functions} preserves
$\int|f_N(u)|^2\dd u=1$ and gives
\begin{equation}
 (-\partial_u^2+u^2)f_N=(2N+1)f_N.
 \label{eq:landau-oscillator-spectrum}
\end{equation}
For fixed $N,k_2,k_3$, define the complex scalar spatial mode
\begin{equation}
 w(\br):=\frac{d^{1/4}}{2\pi}f_N(u)e^{\ii(k_2y+k_3z)}.
 \label{eq:landau-spatial-mode}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:landau-oscillator-spectrum} gives
$\bp^2w=\bigl(k_3^2+d(2N+1)\bigr)w$.
The mode \eqref{eq:landau-spatial-mode} is normalized in $x$ and delta-normalized in $k_2,k_3$.
The positive real KG mode energy is
\begin{equation}
 E=\sqrt{m^2+k_3^2+d(2N+1)}.
 \label{eq:kg-landau}
\end{equation}
Using \eqref{eq:kg-landau} in \eqref{eq:kg-field} gives both exact modes:
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\psi(t,\br)=e^{-\ii(qV_0\pm E)t}w(\br).}
 \label{eq:kg-landau-mode}
\end{equation}
Multiplying \eqref{eq:kg-landau-mode} by $\sqrt{m/E}$ gives unit
magnitude of the KG pairing for
a normalized discrete mode (or unit delta normalization here).  Its sign
is the sign of the mechanical frequency $\pm E$, regardless of the shift
$qV_0$ of the canonical frequency.

\paragraph{Dirac modes and all four components.}
For $s=\pm1$, let $\psi_s$ be a constant complex ideal spin amplitude
with $\sigma_3\psi_s=s\psi_s$ and $\lVert\psi_s\rVert^2=1$.
Explicitly, $\psi_+=\sqrt2\Pi_3$ and
$\psi_-=\sqrt2\sigma_1\Pi_3$.  Define the ideal spatial seed
$a(\br)=w(\br)\psi_s$.  Sigma multiplication gives
\begin{align*}
 (\bp\cdot\bsig)^2&=\bp^2-qB\sigma_3,\\
 (\bp\cdot\bsig)^2a
   &=\bigl(k_3^2+d(2N+1)-qBs\bigr)a.
\end{align*}
For these spinor modes the positive real energy is
\begin{equation}
 E:=\sqrt{m^2+k_3^2+d(2N+1)-qBs}.
 \label{eq:dirac-landau-energy}
\end{equation}
The energy \eqref{eq:dirac-landau-energy} gives two exact Dirac blocks:
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi_+(t,\br)
 =\frac{e^{-\ii(qV_0+E)t}}{2\sqrt{E(E+m)}}
 \begin{Bmatrix}(E+m-\bp\cdot\bsig)a\\(E+m+\bp\cdot\bsig)a\end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:dirac-landau-positive}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi_-(t,\br)
 =\frac{e^{-\ii(qV_0-E)t}}{2\sqrt{E(E+m)}}
 \begin{Bmatrix}(E+m+\bp\cdot\bsig)a\\-(E+m-\bp\cdot\bsig)a\end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:dirac-landau-negative}
\end{equation}
For \eqref{eq:dirac-landau-positive}, the first component equation follows from
\[
 (E-\bp\cdot\bsig)(E+m+\bp\cdot\bsig)a
       =m(E+m-\bp\cdot\bsig)a,
\]
since $(\bp\cdot\bsig)^2a=(E^2-m^2)a$.  The other component and the
negative branch \eqref{eq:dirac-landau-negative} follow by the same multiplication.  Hermiticity under
spatial integration gives each block the seed's normalization because
\[
 \frac{(E+m)^2+E^2-m^2}{2E(E+m)}=1.
\]
No division by $E-m$ occurs, so this includes the lowest level.
Every spatial component is explicit:
\begin{equation}
 (\bp\cdot\bsig)a
 =\frac{d^{1/4}e^{\ii(k_2y+k_3z)}}{2\pi}
 \left(-\ii\sqrt d\,\sigma_1\partial_u
       -\frac{qB}{\sqrt d}u\sigma_2+k_3\sigma_3\right)f_N(u)\psi_s.
 \label{eq:dirac-landau-components}
\end{equation}
The derivatives in \eqref{eq:dirac-landau-components} are adjacent
oscillator functions:
\begin{align*}
 u f_N&=\frac{\sqrt{N+1}f_{N+1}+\sqrt N f_{N-1}}{\sqrt2},\\
 \partial_u f_N&=\frac{\sqrt N f_{N-1}-\sqrt{N+1}f_{N+1}}{\sqrt2}.
\end{align*}
For $N=0$ the term proportional to $\sqrt N$ vanishes.
These are the usual four-component Dirac magnetic
solutions~\cite{kuznetsov-okrugin}.

\paragraph{Degeneracy and the Pauli limit.}
Define the combined nonnegative integer
$N'=N+(1-\operatorname{sgn}(qB)s)/2$.  The energy levels are
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{E^2=m^2+k_3^2+2dN'.}
 \label{eq:dirac-landau}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:dirac-landau}, the lowest level $N'=0$ has only $N=0$ and $s=\operatorname{sgn}(qB)$;
each higher level has two spin--oscillator pairs.
The seed's $s/2$ is spin in the representation separating positive and
negative energies.  Through $\bp\cdot\bsig$, the full Dirac block mixes
its raw $\sigma_3$ components; it need not be an eigenstate of
$\sigma_3$ acting on both entries.

Expanding the positive branch gives the residual energy
\begin{equation}
 E-m=\frac{k_3^2+d(2N+1)-qBs}{2m}
 -\frac{\bigl(k_3^2+d(2N+1)-qBs\bigr)^2}{8m^3}+\cdots.
 \label{eq:landau-low-energy-expansion}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:landau-low-energy-expansion} gives orbital spacing
$|qB|/m$ and Zeeman shift $-qBs/(2m)$:
the Pauli result for spin $s/2$ and gyromagnetic factor $2$.
The next term agrees with \eqref{eq:pauli-relativistic-corrections}
when $\bE=0$.  Summing over $N,s$, integrating over $k_2,k_3$, and
including both energy branches spans arbitrary initial data in this field.

\section{Rotations and Lorentz boosts}
\label{sec:transformations}

We now apply the algebraic actions of Section~\ref{sec:preserved-quantities}
to the physical variables already defined.  Primed fields are evaluated at
$X'$ and unprimed fields at $X$.  Factors are constant until the final
subsection.

\subsection{Covariance of the physical equations}

Let $T$ be a complex paravector with $\det T=1$.  It may be any ordered
product of $R=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ and
$L=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$, with real unit $\bu$ and real angle
or rapidity $\theta$.  Recall the real event $X$, momentum $P$, potential
$\Phi$, electric current $D$, and Dirac probability current $J$.
Each transforms by
\begin{equation}
 Z'=T^{-1}Z(T^{-1})^{\mathsf H},
 \qquad Z\in\{X,P,\Phi,D,J\}.
 \label{eq:physical-lorentz-congruence}
\end{equation}
The derivative, complex field $F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$, and complex
ideal Dirac entries $\Psi_1,\Psi_2$ use the corresponding laws
\begin{align}
 \partial'&=T^{\mathsf H}\partial T, \label{eq:derivative-transformation}\\
 F'&=T^{-1}FT, \label{eq:field-transformation}\\
 \Psi_1'&=T^{\mathsf H}\Psi_1, \label{eq:chiral-first-transformation}\\
 \Psi_2'&=T^{-1}\Psi_2. \label{eq:chiral-second-transformation}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:physical-lorentz-congruence} acts on real four-vectors;
\eqref{eq:derivative-transformation} is their real-coordinate chain rule.
The field similarity~\eqref{eq:field-transformation} and chiral laws
\eqref{eq:chiral-first-transformation}--\eqref{eq:chiral-second-transformation}
are fixed by covariance.  Since
$T^*=(T^{\mathsf H})^{-1}$, Maxwell covariance follows by cancellation:
\begin{equation}
 \partial'F'=T^{\mathsf H}(\partial F)T
 =T^{\mathsf H}D^*T=D'^*.
 \label{eq:maxwell-covariance}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:maxwell-covariance} recovers \eqref{eq:maxwell}.
For real charge $q$ and mass $m>0$, the coupled Dirac equations transform as
\begin{align}
 (\ii\partial'-q\Phi'^*)\Psi_2'
 &=T^{\mathsf H}(\ii\partial-q\Phi^*)\Psi_2=m\Psi_1',
 \label{eq:dirac-first-covariance}\\
 (\ii\partial'^*-q\Phi')\Psi_1'
 &=T^{-1}(\ii\partial^*-q\Phi)\Psi_1=m\Psi_2'.
 \label{eq:dirac-second-covariance}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:dirac-first-covariance} and
\eqref{eq:dirac-second-covariance} verify the opposite chiral factors.  A single ideal amplitude needs
a specified chirality before it has a boost law.
The current law follows from \eqref{eq:dirac-current}, since
\[
 \adj{\Psi_1'\Psi_1'^{\mathsf H}}
 =T^{-1}\adj{\Psi_1\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}}(T^{-1})^{\mathsf H},
\]
and the second current term has the same congruence.

Adjugation of $P'$ and scalar-part cyclicity give
\begin{equation}
 \scpart{\adj{P'}X'}
 =\scpart{T^{\mathsf H}\adj P\,X(T^{\mathsf H})^{-1}}
 =\scpart{\adj P\,X}.
 \label{eq:phase-invariance}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:phase-invariance} makes $Et-\bp\cdot\br$ an
invariant wave phase.  Replacing $P,X$
by a real future normal $N$ with $\det N=1$ and current $J$ gives the invariant flux
$\scpart{\adj N\,J}$ in \eqref{eq:dirac-hypersurface-norm}.
These are different actions on different physical objects, all within the
same algebra.

\subsection{Rotations and measurement probabilities}

Recall $R=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$, with real angle $\theta$
and real unit axis $\bu$.  Since $R^{\mathsf H}=R^{-1}$, the preceding
laws give $X'=R^{\mathsf H}XR$ and $F'=R^{\mathsf H}FR$.
Time is unchanged; Rodrigues' formula~\eqref{eq:rodrigues} rotates the
position and both field vectors.

For a nonzero complex ideal amplitude $\psi$ and real observable $Z$,
\begin{align*}
 \psi'&=R^{\mathsf H}\psi,\\
 Z'&=R^{\mathsf H}ZR,\\
 \psi'^{\mathsf H}Z'\psi'&=\psi^{\mathsf H}Z\psi,\\
 \psi'^{\mathsf H}\psi'&=\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi.
\end{align*}
The expectation definition~\eqref{eq:paravector-expectation} therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 \langle Z'\rangle_{\psi'}=\langle Z\rangle_\psi.
 \label{eq:rotation-expectation}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:rotation-expectation} includes projector probabilities.
Both Dirac entries have this same one-sided rotation, and their current has
the two-sided action.  At $2\pi$, amplitudes change sign while quadratic
observables remain unchanged.  The column translation is in
Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-spinors}.

\subsection{Moving frames, clock rates, and lengths}
\label{sec:boosts}

Recall $L=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$, with real rapidity $\theta$,
real unit $\bu$, $\beta=\tanh\theta$, and $\gamma=\cosh\theta$.
The explicit gamma form is \eqref{eq:boost-gamma-form}.
For real $X=t+\br\cdot\bsig$, the passive boost
$X'=L^{-1}XL^{-1}$ means that the primed frame moves at $+\beta\bu$.
Equations~\eqref{eq:boost-scalar-component}--\eqref{eq:boost-vector-component}
become
\begin{align}
 t'&=\gamma(t-\beta\bu\cdot\br), \label{eq:lorentz-time}\\
 \br'&=\br_{\perp}+\gamma(\br_{\parallel}-\beta t\bu).
 \label{eq:lorentz-position}
\end{align}
where $\br_{\parallel}=(\bu\cdot\br)\bu$ and
$\br_{\perp}=\br-\br_{\parallel}$.  Reversing $\theta$ gives $X=LX'L$.
For a clock at rest in the primed frame, \eqref{eq:lorentz-time} uses $\dd\br=\beta\bu\dd t$, hence
\begin{equation}
 \dd t'=\gamma(1-\beta^2)\dd t=\dd t/\gamma.
 \label{eq:time-dilation}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:time-dilation} is the moving clock rate.
For a rod at rest in the unprimed frame, let $\Delta\br$ be its endpoint
separation.  A simultaneous measurement in the primed frame requires
$\Delta t'=0$, so $\Delta t=\beta\bu\cdot\Delta\br$.  Substitution gives
\begin{equation}
 \Delta\br'_{\parallel}=\Delta\br_{\parallel}/\gamma.
 \label{eq:length-contraction}
\end{equation}
The transverse separation remains unchanged by \eqref{eq:lorentz-position}.
Equation~\eqref{eq:length-contraction} is longitudinal contraction measured
at equal primed times.

Collinear boosts add rapidities by \eqref{eq:boost-composition}.
For a second real rapidity $\theta'$, set $\beta'=\tanh\theta'$ and
$\gamma'=\cosh\theta'$.  Their combined parameters are
\begin{align}
 \beta''&=\tanh(\theta+\theta')
 =\frac{\beta+\beta'}{1+\beta\beta'}, \label{eq:velocity-addition}\\
 \gamma''&=\gamma\gamma'(1+\beta\beta'). \label{eq:gamma-composition}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:velocity-addition} and \eqref{eq:gamma-composition}
are the collinear velocity and clock-factor composition laws.
Noncollinear boosts instead have the rotation commutator
\eqref{eq:boost-generator-commutator}, producing the Thomas--Wigner rotation.

\subsection{Boosted fields and energy flux}

For $L=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ with the parameters above,
$F'=L^{-1}FL$.  Apply \eqref{eq:boost-similarity-vector} to
$\bE+\ii\bBf$ and separate its real and imaginary parts:
\begin{align}
 \bE'&=\bE_{\parallel}+\gamma(\bE_{\perp}+\beta\bu\times\bBf),
 \label{eq:electric-boost}\\
 \bBf'&=\bBf_{\parallel}+\gamma(\bBf_{\perp}-\beta\bu\times\bE).
 \label{eq:magnetic-boost}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:electric-boost} and \eqref{eq:magnetic-boost} leave
parallel components unchanged; their projections use the same unit axis as
\eqref{eq:lorentz-position}.
The general laws give the chiral boosts
$\Psi_1'=L\Psi_1$ and $\Psi_2'=L^{-1}\Psi_2$, together with
$\partial'=L\partial L$ and $\partial'^*=L^{-1}\partial^*L^{-1}$.

Recall the stress map $T(N)=FNF^{\mathsf H}/2$, with real paravector
input $N$.  Since $N'=L^{-1}NL^{-1}$,
\begin{equation}
 T'(N')=\tfrac12F'N'F'^{\mathsf H}=L^{-1}T(N)L^{-1}.
 \label{eq:stress-covariance}
\end{equation}
Here $T'$ denotes the map formed from $F'$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:stress-covariance}
shows that each energy--momentum
flux has the real four-vector law; $T(1)$ alone is only the flux through
the chosen time direction.  The same cancellation in
\eqref{eq:lorentz} gives the transformed proper force.

\subsection{Varying frames and local bases}
\label{sec:varying-transformations}

Let $T(s)$ be a smooth complex paravector of determinant one along a real
parameter $s$.  Define the complex paravector
$\Omega(s):=T^{-1}\dd T/\dd s$.  The inverse rule gives
\[
 \frac{\dd T^{-1}}{\dd s}=-\Omega T^{-1}.
\]
For a complex paravector $Z(s)$ transformed by similarity,
$Z'=T^{-1}ZT$, product differentiation yields
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd Z'}{\dd s}
 =T^{-1}\frac{\dd Z}{\dd s}T+[Z',\Omega].
 \label{eq:varying-similarity}
\end{equation}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:varying-similarity} again and replacing
$T^{-1}(\dd Z/\dd s)T$ by its first-derivative expression gives
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \frac{\dd^2 Z'}{\dd s^2}={}&T^{-1}\frac{\dd^2Z}{\dd s^2}T
 +2\left[\frac{\dd Z'}{\dd s},\Omega\right]\\
 &+\left[Z',\frac{\dd\Omega}{\dd s}\right]
 +[\Omega,[Z',\Omega]].
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:varying-similarity-second}
\end{equation}
The last term in \eqref{eq:varying-similarity-second} follows from
$-[ [Z',\Omega],\Omega]=[\Omega,[Z',\Omega]]$.
For a real paravector transformed instead by congruence,
$Z'=T^{-1}Z(T^{-1})^{\mathsf H}$, the same rule gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd Z'}{\dd s}
 =T^{-1}\frac{\dd Z}{\dd s}(T^{-1})^{\mathsf H}
 -\Omega Z'-Z'\Omega^{\mathsf H}.
 \label{eq:varying-congruence}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:varying-similarity} and \eqref{eq:varying-congruence}
include the frame terms omitted by a constant-factor transformation.
For a varying rotation $T=R=\exp(\ii\theta(s)\bu(s)\cdot\bsig/2)$,
with real $\theta(s)$ and real unit $\bu(s)$, unitarity gives
$\Omega^{\mathsf H}=-\Omega$, so both formulas agree.

When $s$ is proper time, applying the appropriate formula to the pointwise
transformed tangent $U$ gives its acceleration law.  This differs from
first transforming the event $X$ and then differentiating: a varying event
map already contributes frame terms to its first derivative.
The similarity formula also gives the local algebra-basis law
$\sigma_k'=T^{-1}\sigma_kT$, with
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd\sigma_k'}{\dd s}=[\sigma_k',\Omega],\qquad k=1,2,3.
 \label{eq:moving-algebra-basis}
\end{equation}
The evolution~\eqref{eq:moving-algebra-basis} preserves all generator products.  A general complex local basis
is nevertheless distinct from a real spacetime frame, whose vectors require
the congruence law.

\section{Flat spacetime in generalized coordinates}
\label{sec:generalized}

Let $f:\R^4\to\R$ and $\bg:\R^4\to\R^3$ be smooth functions on a
coordinate chart with real coordinates $(t,\br)$.  They specify inertial
coordinates through the real paravector
\[
 X=f(t,\br)+\bg(t,\br)\cdot\bsig.
\]
Require the Jacobian of $(f,\bg)$ to be nonsingular.  Differentiating gives
\begin{align*}
 \dd X&=(\partial_tf+\partial_t\bg\cdot\bsig)\dd t
 +\sum_{k=1}^3(\partial_{r_k}f+\partial_{r_k}\bg\cdot\bsig)\dd r_k,\\
 \dd s^2&=\dd X\,\dd X^*=\dd f^2-\dd\bg^2.
\end{align*}
With $x_0=t$, $x_k=r_k$ and $\partial_k=\partial/\partial x_k$, the
interval is $\dd s^2=\sum_{j,k=0}^3 g_{jk}\dd x_j\dd x_k$, with real coefficients
\begin{equation}
 g_{jk}=\partial_jf\,\partial_kf-\partial_j\bg\cdot\partial_k\bg,
 \qquad j,k\in\{0,1,2,3\}.
 \label{eq:coordinate-metric}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:coordinate-metric} is the flat Minkowski metric in changed coordinates; varying
coefficients do not imply curvature.

For a future timelike curve, $s$ is proper time and $\dd f>0$.
Define its real inertial three-velocity $\bv=\dd\bg/\dd f$ and
$\gamma=(1-\bv^2)^{-1/2}$, with $\bv^2<1$.  Then
\begin{align*}
 \dd s&=\sqrt{1-\bv^2}\,\dd f,\\
 U=\frac{\dd X}{\dd s}&=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig).
\end{align*}
Apply \eqref{eq:proper-acceleration-components} with inertial time $f$
and coordinate acceleration $\dd\bv/\dd f$.
For inertial motion, the scalar part gives $\bv\cdot\dd\bv/\dd f=0$,
and its vector part then gives $\dd\bv/\dd f=0$.  Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \bg=\bC_1f+\bC_2.
 \label{eq:inertial-coordinate-path}
\end{equation}
The real constants $\bC_1,\bC_2\in\R^3$ in
\eqref{eq:inertial-coordinate-path} are velocity and initial position.
If primes denote total derivatives along this curve with respect to $t$,
then, wherever $f'\ne0$,
\begin{align*}
 \frac{\dd\bv}{\dd t}&=\frac{\bg''f'-\bg'f''}{(f')^2},\\
 [U,\dd U/\dd s]&=2\ii\gamma^2\left(\bv\times\frac{\dd\bv}{\dd s}\right)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align*}

\section{Magnetic duality and constant-field motion}
\label{sec:magnetic-duality}

This is the standard classical magnetic-source extension on real spacetime.
Its magnetic charge is a hypothetical source parameter; no observed
monopole is assumed.  Setting it to zero recovers ordinary electrodynamics.

\subsection{The imaginary force and magnetic charge}
\label{sec:magnetic-dual-force}

Recall the real proper velocity $U=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig)$ and
momentum $P=mU=E+\bp\cdot\bsig$, with real constant $m>0$ and real
proper time $s$.  The real and imaginary parts of the product
\eqref{eq:force-product} are the electric force and its magnetic dual
per unit charge.
Let $q,q'\in\R$ be electric and magnetic charges; here the prime labels
an independent charge.  The classical dyon force is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\frac{\dd P}{\dd s}=\repart{(q-\ii q')FU}.}
 \label{eq:dyon-force}
\end{equation}
The real components of \eqref{eq:dyon-force} are
\begin{align}
 \frac{\dd E}{\dd t}&=(q\bE+q'\bBf)\cdot\bv, \label{eq:dyon-energy}\\
 \frac{\dd\bp}{\dd t}
 &=q(\bE+\bv\times\bBf)+q'(\bBf-\bv\times\bE).
 \label{eq:dyon-momentum}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dyon-energy} is the work rate;
\eqref{eq:dyon-momentum} contains the electric and magnetic forces.
The minus sign in $q-\ii q'$ follows from $F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$:
a positive magnetic charge feels $+q'\bBf$ at rest.  Both forces preserve
the mass shell, since cyclicity and $UU^*=1$ give
\[
 \scpart{U^*FU}=\scpart{FUU^*}=\scpart F=0.
\]
Its real and imaginary parts give the two tangency conditions separately.

Let $M=d+\bm M\cdot\bsig$ be a real magnetic-current paravector,
with real magnetic charge density $d(t,\br)$ and current
$\bm M(t,\br)\in\R^3$.  Maxwell's extension is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\partial F=D^*+\ii M^*.}
 \label{eq:maxwell-monopoles}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:maxwell-monopoles} retains the electric-source laws
\eqref{eq:gauss-electric}, \eqref{eq:ampere-maxwell}, and
\eqref{eq:charge-continuity}.  Its magnetic parts become
\begin{align}
 \pdr\cdot\bBf&=d, \label{eq:magnetic-charge-law}\\
 \pdr\times\bE+\partial_t\bBf&=-\bm M, \label{eq:magnetic-current-law}\\
 \partial_td+\pdr\cdot\bm M&=0. \label{eq:magnetic-continuity}
\end{align}
The divergence and curl in \eqref{eq:magnetic-charge-law} and
\eqref{eq:magnetic-current-law} imply magnetic-charge conservation
\eqref{eq:magnetic-continuity}.
For any real constant angle $a$, the classical equations admit the
simultaneous duality transformation
\begin{align}
 F&\longmapsto e^{-\ii a}F, \label{eq:duality-field}\\
 q+\ii q'&\longmapsto e^{-\ii a}(q+\ii q'), \label{eq:duality-charge}\\
 D+\ii M&\longmapsto e^{-\ii a}(D+\ii M). \label{eq:duality-current}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:duality-field} and \eqref{eq:duality-current}
multiply both sides of Maxwell's law by the same phase;
\eqref{eq:duality-charge} leaves $(q-\ii q')F$ unchanged.
A specified quantum charge spectrum need not admit every angle.
Since a smooth curl has zero divergence, magnetic charge requires
patched, singular, or enlarged potentials.\cite{tong-monopoles}
This extension uses real spacetime.

\subsection{Exact motion in a constant field}

Recall real charges $q,q'$, mass $m>0$, and complex electromagnetic
field $F$.  For constant $F$, define
\begin{equation}
 T(s)=\exp\!\left(\frac{(q-\ii q')s}{2m}F\right).
 \label{eq:constant-field-factor}
\end{equation}
Using \eqref{eq:constant-field-factor}, the solution for future real
initial momentum $P(0)$ with $\det P(0)=m^2$ is
\begin{equation}
 P(s)=T(s)P(0)T(s)^{\mathsf H}.
 \label{eq:real-force-congruence-solution}
\end{equation}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:real-force-congruence-solution}, with real $U=P/m$,
\begin{align*}
 \frac{\dd P}{\dd s}
 &=\frac{(q-\ii q')FP+P((q-\ii q')F)^{\mathsf H}}{2m}\\
 &=\repart{(q-\ii q')FU}.
\end{align*}
This congruence preserves Hermiticity, determinant, and the future cone;
$\det T=1$ follows from $\scpart F=0$.  Hence $U=P/m$ remains a
physical proper velocity.
For $q'=0$, real unit $\bu$, and constant real field magnitudes $E,B$,
\begin{align*}
 T(s)&=\exp(\ii qBs\bu\cdot\bsig/(2m))
 &&(\bE=0,\ \bBf=B\bu),\\
 T(s)&=\exp(qEs\bu\cdot\bsig/(2m))
 &&(\bBf=0,\ \bE=E\bu).
\end{align*}
These are $R=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ with $\theta=qBs/m$
and $L=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ with $\theta=qEs/m$.
The evolution uses the inverse sandwiches to
\eqref{eq:rotation-action} and \eqref{eq:boost-action} for the same
factors.  Complex field factors thus already generate real motion.

\section{Extrapolation and speculation}
\label{sec:extensions}

The following coordinate and force laws are additional hypotheses.
Lorentz covariance, the ordinary force law, stability, and the cosmological
stress tensor provide established tests of these toy models.  The tests
do not identify a dark sector or an electroweak interaction with imaginary
coordinates.

\subsection{Testing a complex momentum equation}
\label{sec:complex-momentum-test}

Keep real charges $q,q'$ and electromagnetic field $F$ as above, but
postulate a complex paravector $P(s)$ with
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd P}{\dd s}=(q+\ii q')FU.
 \label{eq:complex-force-proposal}
\end{equation}
Initially let $s$ and $U$ remain real.  Extracting
\eqref{eq:complex-force-proposal} gives
\begin{align*}
 \frac{\dd\repart P}{\dd s}&=q\repart{FU}-q'\impart{FU},\\
 \frac{\dd\impart P}{\dd s}&=q\impart{FU}+q'\repart{FU}.
\end{align*}
The real magnetic force has the opposite sign to \eqref{eq:dyon-force}.
Even when $q'=0$, the imaginary force $q\impart{FU}$ remains.
At rest in a pure magnetic field, $FU=\ii\bBf\cdot\bsig$: the proposed
law produces imaginary momentum where an electric charge stays at rest.
Thus $P=mU$ cannot remain real in general.  If $P$ is independent of the
real trajectory, these equations only accumulate two impulses; they do
not prescribe a complex trajectory.

To close the model, impose $U=P/m$ with real $m>0$, allowing both
paravectors to be complex.  Now $s\in\R$ is an evolution parameter,
not established physical proper time.  The law becomes
\[
 \frac{\dd P}{\dd s}=\frac{q+\ii q'}m FP.
\]
It preserves the complex determinant because
\begin{align*}
 \frac{\dd\det P}{\dd s}
 &=2\scpart{\adj P\,\frac{\dd P}{\dd s}}\\
 &=\frac{2(q+\ii q')}m\det P\,\scpart F=0.
\end{align*}
For constant $F$, the complete solution is
\begin{equation}
 P(s)=\exp\!\left(\frac{(q+\ii q')s}{m}F\right)P(0).
 \label{eq:complex-momentum-solution}
\end{equation}
Take $q'=0$, $q\ne0$, $\bE=0$, constant $\bBf=\{0,0,B\}$ with
real $B\ne0$, and $P(0)=m$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:complex-momentum-solution} gives
\begin{equation}
 P(s)=m\cos(qBs/m)+\ii m\sigma_3\sin(qBs/m).
 \label{eq:complex-magnetic-counterexample}
\end{equation}
Integrating \eqref{eq:complex-magnetic-counterexample} for a complex
coordinate $Z(s)$ defined by $\dd Z/\dd s=P/m$ and
$Z(0)=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
 Z(s)=\frac{m}{qB}\sin(qBs/m)
 +\ii\frac{m}{qB}\bigl[1-\cos(qBs/m)\bigr]\sigma_3.
 \label{eq:complex-coordinate-counterexample}
\end{equation}
The real energy in \eqref{eq:complex-magnetic-counterexample} changes sign,
and the time in \eqref{eq:complex-coordinate-counterexample} ceases to
increase, despite $\det P=m^2$.  This fails the ordinary at-rest electric
charge limit even with zero magnetic charge.  Determinant conservation
alone is insufficient for a physical mass shell or causality.

\subsection{Testing complex spacetime and its null set}

Let $Z=X+\ii Y$ be a complex coordinate, with real paravectors
$X=t+\br\cdot\bsig$ and $Y=u+\bm w\cdot\bsig$.
Here $t,u\in\R$ and $\br,\bm w\in\R^3$ are independent coordinates.
Multiplication gives
\begin{equation}
 \det Z=t^2-\br^2-u^2+\bm w^2+2\ii(tu-\br\cdot\bm w).
 \label{eq:complex-coordinate-determinant}
\end{equation}
Complex nullity in \eqref{eq:complex-coordinate-determinant} requires both
\[
 \det X=\det Y,\qquad tu=\br\cdot\bm w.
\]
For example, $Z=\sigma_1+\ii\sigma_2$ is complex-null but its real part
is spacelike.  Identifying every complex-null separation with observable
light propagation would therefore permit real spacelike connections.
Taking only the real determinant gives instead
\begin{equation}
 \repart{\det(\dd Z)}
 =\dd t^2-\dd\br^2-\dd u^2+\dd\bm w^2,
 \label{eq:complex-real-metric}
\end{equation}
The metric~\eqref{eq:complex-real-metric} has signature $(4,4)$.
Either proposal needs an operationally defined
real causal slice.  Complex field values on real spacetime require no
such change of coordinates.

\subsection{Separate masses and small couplings}

Consider a real four-vector field $A(t,\br)=a(t,\br)+\bA(t,\br)\cdot\bsig$.
Assign proposed real masses $m_0,m_1\geq0$ to its scalar and third vector
components:
\begin{align*}
 (\Box+m_0^2)a&=0,\\
 (\Box+m_1^2)A_3&=0.
\end{align*}
For $L=\exp(\theta\sigma_3/2)$, define $\beta=\tanh\theta$ and
$\gamma=\cosh\theta$ with real rapidity $\theta$.  The passive boost gives
\begin{align*}
 a'&=\gamma(a-\beta A_3).
\end{align*}
\begin{equation}
 (\Box'+m_0^2)a'=-\gamma\beta(m_0^2-m_1^2)A_3.
 \label{eq:component-mass-test}
\end{equation}
Covariance of \eqref{eq:component-mass-test} for arbitrary fields forces $m_0^2=m_1^2$, and similarly
for every spatial component.  Distinct masses can label distinct fields,
but cannot label time and space entries of one Lorentz vector.

As a separate toy model, mix two scalar fields.  Let
$z=a+\ii b$, with $a,b:\R^4\to\R$, and let $m>0$ and $h$ be real
constants; $h$ has units of mass squared.  The real Lagrangian density
\begin{equation}
 L=\tfrac12\det(\partial a)+\tfrac12\det(\partial b)
   -\tfrac12m^2(a^2+b^2)-hab
 \label{eq:scalar-mixing-action}
\end{equation}
has positive time-derivative terms, since
$\det(\partial a)=(\partial_ta)^2-(\pdr a)^2$.
The equations of \eqref{eq:scalar-mixing-action} and their diagonalizing real combinations are
\begin{align*}
 (\Box+m^2)a+hb&=0,\\
 (\Box+m^2)b+ha&=0,\\
 a'&=(a+b)/\sqrt2,\\
 b'&=(a-b)/\sqrt2.
\end{align*}
\begin{align}
 (\Box+m^2+h)a'&=0, \label{eq:mixed-scalar-first}\\
 (\Box+m^2-h)b'&=0. \label{eq:mixed-scalar-second}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:mixed-scalar-first} and \eqref{eq:mixed-scalar-second}
have squared masses $m^2\pm h$, both nonnegative when $|h|\leq m^2$.
The mixing term breaks continuous $z\mapsto e^{\ii c}z$ symmetry
($c\in\R$ constant), but leaves the principal operator $\Box$ and its
light cone unchanged.  Small coupling does not create complex coordinates.
The electroweak interaction likewise requires additional internal gauge
and chiral structure.\cite{tong-electroweak}

By contrast, the alternative kinetic term
\[
 \tfrac12\repart{\det(\partial z)}
 =\tfrac12\det(\partial a)-\tfrac12\det(\partial b)
\]
has real kinetic Hamiltonian density $H$:
\begin{equation}
 H=\tfrac12[(\partial_ta)^2+(\pdr a)^2]
 -\tfrac12[(\partial_tb)^2+(\pdr b)^2].
 \label{eq:ghost-kinetic-energy}
\end{equation}
The density~\eqref{eq:ghost-kinetic-energy} is unbounded below: the independent $b$ mode is a ghost, whose
negative kinetic sign persists at arbitrarily small coupling.
\cite{carroll-ghosts}

\subsection{What would be needed for a dark sector?}

A dark-matter model must supply abundance, lifetime, gravitational
clustering, and interaction rates.  Complex notation alone fixes none.
To distinguish matter from accelerated expansion, additionally assume
homogeneous general-relativistic cosmology.  Let $\phi(t)\in\R$ be a
canonical scalar field, $V(\phi)$ its real potential-energy density,
$u(t)$ its energy density, and $p(t)$ its isotropic pressure.
Its stress tensor gives\cite{carroll2001}
\begin{align}
 u&=\tfrac12(\partial_t\phi)^2+V, \label{eq:scalar-cosmic-energy}\\
 p&=\tfrac12(\partial_t\phi)^2-V. \label{eq:scalar-cosmic-pressure}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:scalar-cosmic-energy} and \eqref{eq:scalar-cosmic-pressure}
are the established stress-tensor relations used to test the model.
For real mass $m>0$, take $V=m^2\phi^2/2$ and oscillations faster than cosmic expansion:
$\phi\simeq A\cos(mt)$ over a cycle, with slowly varying real amplitude
$A$.  Cycle averaging gives
\begin{align*}
 \left\langle\tfrac12(\partial_t\phi)^2\right\rangle
 &=\langle V\rangle=m^2A^2/4,\\
 \langle p\rangle&=0.
\end{align*}
This behaves as pressureless matter.  Potential domination instead gives
$p\simeq-u$.  For the real positive cosmic scale factor $a(t)$ and
Newton's constant $G>0$, the acceleration equation is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial_t^2a}{a}=-\frac{4\pi G}{3}(u+3p).
 \label{eq:cosmic-acceleration}

Equation~\eqref{eq:cosmic-acceleration} requires $u+3p<0$ for acceleration,
not merely an additional positive
mass.  A complex-spacetime hypothesis still needs a stable action, a
physical causal structure, and distinct testable predictions.

\section{Pauli-matrix representation}
\label{sec:matrix}

This appendix translates the established paravector operations into
matrices.  Let $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, with $S\in\C$ and $\bV\in\C^3$,
and write $\bM=\operatorname{mat}(Z)\in\C^{2\times2}$.
The identity matrices are $\bI_2$ and $\bI_4$ in their stated dimensions.

\subsection{Faithful representation}

The Pauli images of the generators are
\[
 \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_1)=\begin{Bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{Bmatrix},\quad
 \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_2)=\begin{Bmatrix}0&-\ii\\\ii&0\end{Bmatrix},\quad
 \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_3)=\begin{Bmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{Bmatrix}.
\]
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(S+\bV\cdot\bsig)=
 \begin{Bmatrix}S+V_3&V_1-\ii V_2\\V_1+\ii V_2&S-V_3\end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:matrix}
\end{equation}
The images in \eqref{eq:matrix} obey the sigma product rule and are linearly independent over
$\C$ together with $\bI_2$.  The map is therefore faithful, preserves
addition, sends $1$ to $\bI_2$, and satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(Z'Z)=\operatorname{mat}(Z')\operatorname{mat}(Z).
 \label{eq:matrix-product}
\end{equation}
Here $Z'$ is any second complex paravector; \eqref{eq:matrix-product}
identifies algebra multiplication with ordinary matrix multiplication.

\subsection{Conjugations and product order}

For complex matrices $\bM,\bN$, transpose $^{\mathsf T}$ exchanges row
and column indices; entrywise conjugation $^{\mathsf C}$ sends each
$a+\ii b$ to $a-\ii b$ for real $a,b$.  Their composition is
$\bM^{\mathsf H}=(\bM^{\mathsf C})^{\mathsf T}$.
Introduce the fixed real matrix
\[
 \bm S=\begin{Bmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{Bmatrix},\qquad
 \bm S^{-1}=-\bm S.
\]
The paravector sigma conjugation has the matrix image
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(Z^*)=\bm S^{-1}\bM^{\mathsf C}\bm S=: \bM^*.
 \label{eq:matrix-sigma-conjugation}
\end{equation}
Substituting the basis in \eqref{eq:matrix-sigma-conjugation} gives
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c c c c c}
\toprule
Algebra basis & $1$ & $\ii$ & $\sigma_k$ & $\ii\sigma_k$\\
\midrule
Hermitian image & $\bI_2$ & $-\ii\bI_2$
 & $\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_k)$ & $-\ii\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_k)$\\
Sigma-conjugate image & $\bI_2$ & $-\ii\bI_2$
 & $-\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_k)$ & $\ii\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_k)$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Here $k\in\{1,2,3\}$.  These are exactly the operations of
Section~\ref{sec:conjugate-operators}, so
$\operatorname{mat}(Z^{\mathsf H})=\bM^{\mathsf H}$.
Their product-order rules are
\begin{align*}
 (\bM\bN)^{\mathsf T}&=\bN^{\mathsf T}\bM^{\mathsf T},\\
 (\bM\bN)^{\mathsf C}&=\bM^{\mathsf C}\bN^{\mathsf C},\\
 (\bM\bN)^{\mathsf H}&=\bN^{\mathsf H}\bM^{\mathsf H}.
\end{align*}
For sigma conjugation the middle factors cancel:
\[
 (\bM\bN)^*=\bm S^{-1}\bM^{\mathsf C}\bm S
                   \bm S^{-1}\bN^{\mathsf C}\bm S.
\]
Thus sigma conjugation preserves product order:
\begin{equation}
 (\bM\bN)^*=\bM^*\bN^*.
 \label{eq:matrix-star-product}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:matrix-star-product} verifies
\eqref{eq:sigma-conjugate-product} in matrices.
Both $^{\mathsf H}$ and $^*$ are commuting involutions.
The matrix-only operations can be recovered as
\begin{align*}
 \bM^{\mathsf C}&=\bm S\bM^*\bm S^{-1},\\
 \bM^{\mathsf T}&=\bm S(\bM^*)^{\mathsf H}\bm S^{-1}.
\end{align*}

\subsection{Scalar part, determinant, and norm}

For $\bM=(m_{jk})$, its trace is $m_{11}+m_{22}$ and its determinant
is $m_{11}m_{22}-m_{12}m_{21}$.  Substituting \eqref{eq:matrix} gives
\begin{align}
 \scpart Z&=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}\bM=S,
 \label{eq:matrix-scalar-part}\\
 (\vecpart Z)_k&=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}
                    (\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_k)\bM)=V_k,\notag\\
 \det Z&=\det\bM=S^2-\bV^2.
 \label{eq:matrix-determinant}
\end{align}
By \eqref{eq:matrix-scalar-part}, matrix-trace cyclicity is precisely the scalar-part cyclicity used
in the main text.  For $\bM'=\operatorname{mat}(Z')$,
\[
 \scpart{Z'Z}=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bM'\bM)
             =\scpart{ZZ'}.
\]
The remaining extraction and norm formulas are
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}(\vecpart Z\cdot\bsig)&=\bM-\scpart Z\,\bI_2,\notag\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\repart Z)&=\tfrac12(\bM+\bM^{\mathsf H}),
 \label{eq:matrix-real-part}\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\impart Z)&=(\bM-\bM^{\mathsf H})/(2\ii),
 \label{eq:matrix-imaginary-part}\\
 \lVert Z\rVert^2&=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bM^{\mathsf H}\bM)
                  =\lVert S\rVert^2+\lVert\bV\rVert^2.
 \label{eq:matrix-norm}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:matrix-real-part}--\eqref{eq:matrix-imaginary-part}
extract Hermitian matrices, so a real paravector has a Hermitian image.
The norm in \eqref{eq:matrix-norm} is half the squared Frobenius norm;
the determinant in \eqref{eq:matrix-determinant} may be complex.

The matrix adjugate is
\begin{equation}
 \adj\bM=(\operatorname{tr}\bM)\bI_2-\bM
        =\operatorname{mat}(\adj Z)=(\bM^*)^{\mathsf H}.
 \label{eq:matrix-adjugate}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:matrix-adjugate} satisfies
\begin{equation*}
 \operatorname{mat}(\adj Z\,Z)=\det(\bM)\bI_2.
\end{equation*}
Consequently $\bM^{-1}=\adj\bM/\det\bM$ when the determinant is
nonzero.  The determinant product and adjugate order rules are the same
in both representations.  The quadratic identity~\eqref{eq:minimal} is
precisely Cayley--Hamilton for a $2\times2$ matrix.

\subsection{Matrix amplitudes and column spinors}
\label{sec:matrix-spinors}

Let $\psi,\psi'$ be complex paravector amplitudes, with matrix images
$\bM_\psi,\bM_{\psi'}$, and let $Z=Z^{\mathsf H}$ be a real observable.
For fields these depend on real $(t,\br)$.  The inner products
\eqref{eq:paravector-inner-product} and
\eqref{eq:paravector-matrix-element} become
\begin{align*}
 \langle\psi'\mid\psi\rangle
 &=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bM_{\psi'}^{\mathsf H}\bM_\psi),\\
 \langle\psi'\mid Z\mid\psi\rangle
 &=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}
    (\bM_{\psi'}^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(Z)\bM_\psi).
\end{align*}
An unrestricted $\bM_\psi$ has two independently transforming columns.
The fixed ideal $\psi\Pi_3=\psi$, with $\Pi_3=(1+\sigma_3)/2$,
sets the second column to zero because
$\operatorname{mat}(\Pi_3)=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$.
Its conventional Pauli column is
\begin{equation}
 \bpsi:=\frac1{\sqrt2}\bM_\psi\begin{Bmatrix}1\\0\end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:matrix-ideal-column}
\end{equation}
Applying \eqref{eq:matrix-ideal-column} to the normalized basis $\psi_+,\psi_-$ of
\eqref{eq:ideal-spin-basis}, the complete correspondence is
\[
 \psi=c_+\psi_++c_-\psi_-
 \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
 \bM_\psi=\sqrt2\begin{Bmatrix}c_+&0\\c_-&0\end{Bmatrix}
 \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
 \bpsi=\begin{Bmatrix}c_+\\c_-\end{Bmatrix}.
\]
Here $c_+,c_-\in\C$, or complex scalar functions of $(t,\br)$.
Substitution cancels the factor of two from the trace pairing:
\begin{align*}
 \langle\psi'\mid\psi\rangle&=\bpsi'^{\mathsf H}\bpsi,\\
 \langle\psi'\mid Z\mid\psi\rangle
 &=\bpsi'^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(Z)\bpsi.
\end{align*}
Left action $T\psi$ maps to $\operatorname{mat}(T)\bpsi$ for any
complex paravector $T$.
For nonzero norm $\rho=\bpsi^{\mathsf H}\bpsi$, the real state
paravector $B$ and projectors $\Pi_\pm=(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig)/2$
($\bu\in\R^3$ a unit measurement axis) translate to
\begin{align}
 \tfrac12\operatorname{mat}(B)&=\bpsi\bpsi^{\mathsf H}/\rho,
 \label{eq:matrix-state}\\
 p_\pm(\bu)&=\bpsi^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(\Pi_\pm)\bpsi/\rho.
 \label{eq:matrix-born}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:matrix-state} and \eqref{eq:matrix-born} are the ordinary
density matrix and Born rule.  An unrestricted
matrix amplitude instead gives
$\bM_\psi\bM_\psi^{\mathsf H}/\operatorname{tr}(\bM_\psi\bM_\psi^{\mathsf H})$,
which is pure exactly when $\bM_\psi$ has rank one.

\subsection{Dirac columns and currents}
\label{sec:matrix-dirac-columns}

For a Dirac block $\bPsi=\{\Psi_1,\Psi_2\}^{\mathsf T}$, let
$\bpsi_1,\bpsi_2\in\C^2$ be the corresponding ideal columns.  Then
\[
 \Wop(Z)\bPsi\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
 \begin{Bmatrix}0&\operatorname{mat}(Z)\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\adj Z)&0\end{Bmatrix}
 \begin{Bmatrix}\bpsi_1\\\bpsi_2\end{Bmatrix}.
\]
The usual Dirac-adjoint row is
$\{\bpsi_2^{\mathsf H},\bpsi_1^{\mathsf H}\}$ in this chiral ordering.
The real current and rotation-spin state become
\begin{align}
 \rho&=\bpsi_1^{\mathsf H}\bpsi_1+\bpsi_2^{\mathsf H}\bpsi_2,
 \label{eq:matrix-dirac-density}\\
 J_k&=-\bpsi_1^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_k)\bpsi_1
       +\bpsi_2^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_k)\bpsi_2,
 \label{eq:matrix-dirac-current}\\
 \tfrac12\operatorname{mat}(B)
 &=\frac{\bpsi_1\bpsi_1^{\mathsf H}+\bpsi_2\bpsi_2^{\mathsf H}}{\rho}.
 \label{eq:matrix-dirac-spin-state}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:matrix-dirac-density}--\eqref{eq:matrix-dirac-current}
are the real components of $J=\rho+\bJ\cdot\bsig$.
The spin state~\eqref{eq:matrix-dirac-spin-state} requires $\rho>0$.
For rest-spin statistics, apply the matrix image of $\bT^{\mathsf H}$
from \eqref{eq:free-fw-map} before forming the same sum.

Recall $R=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$ and
$L=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$, with real angle or rapidity $\theta$.
The chiral transformation laws are
\begin{align}
 \begin{Bmatrix}\bpsi_1'\\\bpsi_2'\end{Bmatrix}
 &=\begin{Bmatrix}\operatorname{mat}(R)^{\mathsf H}&0\\
                  0&\operatorname{mat}(R)^{\mathsf H}\end{Bmatrix}
   \begin{Bmatrix}\bpsi_1\\\bpsi_2\end{Bmatrix},
 \label{eq:matrix-chiral-rotation}\\
 \begin{Bmatrix}\bpsi_1'\\\bpsi_2'\end{Bmatrix}
 &=\begin{Bmatrix}\operatorname{mat}(L)&0\\
                  0&\operatorname{mat}(L)^{-1}\end{Bmatrix}
   \begin{Bmatrix}\bpsi_1\\\bpsi_2\end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:matrix-chiral-boost}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:matrix-chiral-rotation}--\eqref{eq:matrix-chiral-boost}
give equal rotation generators and opposite boost generators, as in
\eqref{eq:chiral-first-transformation}--\eqref{eq:chiral-second-transformation}.

Finally, the ideal map sends $\psi^*\sigma_1$ to
$\bm S^{-1}\bpsi^{\mathsf C}$, since
$\bm S=\operatorname{mat}(\ii\sigma_2)$.
The charge-conjugate block of \eqref{eq:dirac-charge-reversal} therefore is
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi'\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
 \begin{Bmatrix}\bm S^{-1}\bpsi_2^{\mathsf C}\\
                -\bm S^{-1}\bpsi_1^{\mathsf C}\end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:matrix-charge-reversal}
\end{equation}
The prime in \eqref{eq:matrix-charge-reversal} denotes charge reversal.

\Needspace{14\baselineskip}
\subsection{Four components and the standard Dirac basis}
\label{sec:matrix-dirac-components}

Write $\Psi_1=a\psi_++b\psi_-$ and $\Psi_2=c\psi_++d\psi_-$,
where $a,b,c,d:\R^4\to\C$ are the four scalar components in the
normalized ideal basis.  Recall real mass $m>0$, real charge $q$, and
real potentials $V:\R^4\to\R$ and $\bA:\R^4\to\R^3$.
The mechanical energy and momentum operators are $E=\ii\partial_t-qV$ and
$\bp=-\ii\pdr-q\bA$.
The coupled paravector equations give exactly
\begin{align}
 (E-p_3)c-(p_1-\ii p_2)d&=ma,
 \label{eq:matrix-dirac-a}\\
 -(p_1+\ii p_2)c+(E+p_3)d&=mb,
 \label{eq:matrix-dirac-b}\\
 (E+p_3)a+(p_1-\ii p_2)b&=mc,
 \label{eq:matrix-dirac-c}\\
 (p_1+\ii p_2)a+(E-p_3)b&=md.
 \label{eq:matrix-dirac-d}
\end{align}
Operators in \eqref{eq:matrix-dirac-a}--\eqref{eq:matrix-dirac-d}
act on the scalar immediately to their right.  Their Hamiltonian
on $\{a,b,c,d\}^{\mathsf T}$ is
\begin{equation}
 H=\begin{Bmatrix}
 qV-p_3&-p_1+\ii p_2&m&0\\
 -p_1-\ii p_2&qV+p_3&0&m\\
 m&0&qV+p_3&p_1-\ii p_2\\
 0&m&p_1+\ii p_2&qV-p_3
 \end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:matrix-dirac-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
This is the component image of \eqref{eq:dirac-hamiltonian}.

To identify conventional gamma matrices, put
$\bm\sigma_k=\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_k)$ for $k=1,2,3$ and define
\begin{align}
 \gamma^0&=\begin{Bmatrix}0&\bI_2\\\bI_2&0\end{Bmatrix},
 \label{eq:gamma-time}\\
 \gamma^k&=\begin{Bmatrix}0&\bm\sigma_k\\-\bm\sigma_k&0\end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:gamma-space}
\end{align}
Equations~\eqref{eq:gamma-time}--\eqref{eq:gamma-space} obey
$(\gamma^0)^2=\bI_4$, $(\gamma^k)^2=-\bI_4$, and
$\gamma^j\gamma^k+\gamma^k\gamma^j=0$ for distinct
$j,k\in\{0,1,2,3\}$.  Thus
\begin{equation}
 \Wop(\ii\partial-q\Phi^*)
 \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
 \gamma^0E-\sum_{k=1}^3\gamma^kp_k,
 \label{eq:matrix-covariant-dirac}
\end{equation}
so \eqref{eq:matrix-covariant-dirac} is the standard chiral Dirac operator with signature
$(+,-,-,-)$.\cite{tong-qft}  Here $\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$ is the
real potential paravector.

The unitary change to the standard Dirac basis, followed by removal
of the rest phase, defines the envelope columns
\begin{equation}
 \begin{Bmatrix}\bm\phi\\\bm\chi\end{Bmatrix}
 =\frac{e^{\ii mt}}{\sqrt2}
 \begin{Bmatrix}\bI_2&\bI_2\\-\bI_2&\bI_2\end{Bmatrix}
 \begin{Bmatrix}\bpsi_1\\\bpsi_2\end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:matrix-envelope-map}
\end{equation}
Here $\bm\phi,\bm\chi\in\C^2$ are the column images of the ideal
fields $\phi=e^{\ii mt}(\Psi_1+\Psi_2)/\sqrt2$ and
$\chi=e^{\ii mt}(\Psi_2-\Psi_1)/\sqrt2$.
Before removing the rest phase, the basis change in
\eqref{eq:matrix-envelope-map} sends \eqref{eq:matrix-dirac-hamiltonian} to
\begin{equation}
 H'=\begin{Bmatrix}
 (qV+m)\bI_2&\sum_kp_k\bm\sigma_k\\
 \sum_kp_k\bm\sigma_k&(qV-m)\bI_2
 \end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:matrix-standard-dirac}
\end{equation}
Removing the rest energy from \eqref{eq:matrix-standard-dirac} gives
the exact envelope equations
\begin{align*}
 E\bm\phi&=\sum_kp_k\bm\sigma_k\bm\chi,\\
 (E+2m)\bm\chi&=\sum_kp_k\bm\sigma_k\bm\phi.
\end{align*}
These identify the large and small amplitudes used in the Pauli limit.

For real electric and magnetic components $E_k,B_k$, the squared
Dirac field coupling has the explicit image
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}(\ii qF)
 &=q\begin{Bmatrix}
 \ii E_3-B_3&\ii E_1+E_2-B_1+\ii B_2\\
 \ii E_1-E_2-B_1-\ii B_2&-\ii E_3+B_3
 \end{Bmatrix},
 \label{eq:matrix-field-coupling}\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\ii qF^*)&=\operatorname{mat}(\ii qF)^{\mathsf H}.
 \notag
\end{align}
The indexed $E_k$ in \eqref{eq:matrix-field-coupling} are field components;
unindexed $E$ remains the
mechanical energy operator.  The two couplings are
$-q\bBf\cdot\bsig\pm\ii q\bE\cdot\bsig$.

\subsection{Quaternion subalgebra}
\label{sec:quaternions}

Define $q_k=-\ii\sigma_k$ for $k=1,2,3$, and
$\bq=\{q_1,q_2,q_3\}$.  These are bivectors, not electric charges.
Their product follows from the sigma rule:
\begin{equation}
 q_jq_k=-\delta_{jk}+\sum_{l=1}^3\epsilon_{jkl}q_l.
 \label{eq:quaternion-product}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:quaternion-product} gives
$q_k^2=-1$, $q_1q_2=q_3$, and $q_1q_2q_3=-1$.
For real $a$ and $\bA\in\R^3$, the quaternion
\begin{equation}
 Q=a+\bA\cdot\bq=a-\ii\bA\cdot\bsig
 \label{eq:quaternion-paravector}
\end{equation}
is a complex paravector with real quaternion coordinates.
Elements~\eqref{eq:quaternion-paravector} form the real scalar--bivector
subalgebra.  For a second such element
$Q'=a'+\bA'\cdot\bq$, expansion gives
\[
 QQ'=(aa'-\bA\cdot\bA')
 +(a\bA'+a'\bA+\bA\times\bA')\cdot\bq.
\]
Because $q_k^{\mathsf H}=-q_k$ and $q_k^*=q_k$, quaternion conjugation
is the existing Hermitian operation:
\begin{align}
 Q^{\mathsf H}&=a-\bA\cdot\bq,\notag\\
 Q^*&=Q,\notag\\
 \adj Q&=Q^{\mathsf H},\notag\\
 QQ^{\mathsf H}&=Q^{\mathsf H}Q=a^2+\bA^2=\det Q.
 \label{eq:quaternion-norm}
\end{align}
For nonzero $Q$, \eqref{eq:quaternion-norm} gives the inverse
$Q^{\mathsf H}/(a^2+\bA^2)$.
The explicit matrix image is
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(Q)=
 \begin{Bmatrix}a-\ii A_3&-A_2-\ii A_1\\
                 A_2-\ii A_1&a+\ii A_3\end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:quaternion-matrix}
\end{equation}
In \eqref{eq:quaternion-matrix},
$\operatorname{mat}(q_k)=-\ii\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_k)$.
The conjugation and norm identities become
\begin{align*}
 \operatorname{mat}(Q^{\mathsf H})&=\operatorname{mat}(Q)^{\mathsf H},\\
 \operatorname{mat}(Q)^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(Q)
 &=(a^2+\bA^2)\bI_2,\\
 \det(\operatorname{mat}(Q))&=a^2+\bA^2.
\end{align*}
A unit quaternion therefore corresponds to a unitary matrix of determinant
one.  For real unit $\bu$ and angle $\theta$, let
\[
 Q=\cos(\theta/2)+\bu\cdot\bq\sin(\theta/2).
\]
Then $Q=R^{\mathsf H}$ for $R=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig/2)$.
For a real vector $\br$, the quaternion rotation
\begin{equation}
 \br'\cdot\bq=Q(\br\cdot\bq)Q^{\mathsf H}
 \label{eq:quaternion-rotation}
\end{equation}
identifies \eqref{eq:quaternion-rotation} with
Rodrigues' formula~\eqref{eq:rodrigues}.

\section{Notation and conventions}
\label{sec:conventions-cautions}

\subsection{Reading conventions}

\begin{itemize}
 \item Natural units are $\hbar=c=1$, with metric signature $(+,-,-,-)$.
 The central pseudoscalar $\ii=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3$ maps to the
 ordinary matrix complex unit; unindexed $e$ denotes Euler's number.
 \item Vectors and matrices are bold; real paravectors have real scalar
 and vector coefficients.  Curly braces display component lists and
 matrices.  A vector square means its dot product with itself.
 \item $\scpart Z$ is the scalar coefficient and $\vecpart Z$ its vector
 coefficients.  $\repart Z$ and $\impart Z$ are real paravectors.
 The positive norm $\lVert Z\rVert^2$ is distinct from the possibly
 complex determinant $\det Z$.
 \item $^{\mathsf H}$ reverses product order, $^*$ preserves it, and
 $\adj Z=Z^{*\mathsf H}$.  The matrix-only $^{\mathsf T}$ and
 $^{\mathsf C}$ mean transpose and entrywise conjugation.
 \item A unit has a two-sided inverse.  Proper velocity is a real
 determinant-one unit: $U^{\mathsf H}=U$ and $U^{-1}=U^*$.
 Hermitian unitarity instead means $U^{\mathsf H}U=1$.
 \item Differential operators act to the right within their displayed
 grouping.  Gauge-covariant energy and momentum do not commute, so their
 ordered products retain electromagnetic field terms.  Hermiticity of
 differential operators additionally requires a domain, boundary
 conditions, and integration by parts.
 \item On Dirac blocks, $^{\mathsf H}$ transposes the block and adjoints
 each entry; $^*$ acts entrywise.  The fixed right projector $\Pi_3$
 selects one ideal.  Left projectors $\Pi_\pm$ select spin outcomes;
 these are distinct actions even when their algebra elements coincide.
 \item Primes denote locally specified transformations; $q'$ is the
 explicitly defined magnetic-charge exception.  Plus/minus and
 parallel/perpendicular subscripts label physical alternatives and
 projections.  Scalar Lagrangians and Hamiltonians are defined locally
 as $L$ and $H$.
\end{itemize}

In the added quantum examples, $\rho$ is density, $\omega$ canonical
mode frequency, and $N$ oscillator level.  The product $\bp\cdot\bsig$

$q'$.  These choices avoid using unindexed $e$, density $n$, current $j$,
