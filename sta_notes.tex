\begin{abstract}
This document develops a three-generator sigma algebra and its complex
paravectors, then applies them to relativity, electromagnetism, and quantum
mechanics.  Natural units $\hbar=c=1$ and signature $(+,-,-,-)$ are used.
\end{abstract}

\section{The algebra at a glance}
\label{sec:algebra}

The generators $\sigma_n$ are algebraic objects with ordered multiplication
rules.  They are not ordinary vector components: their products generally do
not commute, so the order of the factors is part of every expression.  The
following panel previews the generators, their product, and the general
complex element developed below.

\begin{center}
\setlength{\fboxsep}{1.2em}
\fcolorbox{MidnightBlue}{MidnightBlue!4}{%
\begin{minipage}{0.88\textwidth}
\begin{align*}
 \sigma_n^2&=1,
 &\sigma_n\sigma_m&=-\sigma_m\sigma_n\quad(n\ne m),\\
 \ii&=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3,
 &\ii^2&=-1,\\
 \bsig&=\{\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3\},
 &\ii\bsig&=\{\sigma_2\sigma_3,\sigma_3\sigma_1,\sigma_1\sigma_2\},\\
 Z&=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig,
 &a,b&\in\R,\quad \bA,\bB\in\R^3.
\end{align*}
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

Ordinary three-vector operations use
\begin{align}
 \bA\cdot\bB&=A_1B_1+A_2B_2+A_3B_3,\\
 \bA\times\bB&=\{A_2B_3-A_3B_2,
 A_3B_1-A_1B_3,A_1B_2-A_2B_1\},\\
 \bA^2&:=\bA\cdot\bA.
\end{align}
Thus the square of a vector always denotes its self dot product.

The set $\{1,\bsig,\ii\bsig,\ii\}$ is a complete basis, displayed by type as
\[
  \underbrace{1}_{\text{scalar}},\qquad
  \underbrace{\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3}_{\text{vectors}},\qquad
  \underbrace{\ii\sigma_1,\ii\sigma_2,\ii\sigma_3}_{\text{bivectors}},\qquad
  \underbrace{\ii}_{\text{pseudoscalar}}.
\]
Anticommutation gives
$\ii^2=-\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2\sigma_3^2=-1$.  The pseudoscalar is central:
moving $\sigma_n$ through the other two generators produces two sign
changes, so
\begin{equation}
 \ii\sigma_n=\sigma_n\ii.
\end{equation}
Thus its order of multiplication does not matter.

The commutator is
\begin{gather}
 [X,Y]:=XY-YX\\
 [Y,X]=-[X,Y].
\end{gather}
Thus $[X,Y]=0$ exactly when $X$ and $Y$ commute.
For $n,m,k\in\{1,2,3\}$, define the Kronecker delta by
\begin{equation}
 \delta_{nm}:=
 \begin{cases}
  1,&n=m,\\
  0,&n\ne m,
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
and the Levi--Civita symbol by
\begin{equation}
 \epsilon_{nmk}:=
 \begin{cases}
  +1,&(n,m,k)\text{ is an even permutation of }(1,2,3),\\
  -1,&(n,m,k)\text{ is an odd permutation of }(1,2,3),\\
  0,&\text{two or more indices are equal}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The ordered sigma multiplication rule is then
For $n,m\in\{1,2,3\}$,
\begin{align}
  \sigma_n\sigma_m=\delta_{nm}
  +\sum_{k=1}^{3}\epsilon_{nmk}\,\ii\sigma_k
  \label{eq:sigma-product}\\
  [\sigma_n,\sigma_m]
  =2\sum_{k=1}^{3}\epsilon_{nmk}\,\ii\sigma_k.
\end{align}
\section{Hermitian and sigma-conjugate operators}
\label{sec:conjugate-operators}

Define the two operations by
\begin{align}
 (\,\cdot\,)^{\mathsf H}
 &:=\text{reverse the order of every product of sigma generators},\\
 (\,\cdot\,)^*
 &:=\text{replace every $\sigma_n$ by $-\sigma_n$ without changing product order}.
\end{align}
Thus, for two generators,
\begin{align}
 (\sigma_m\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}&=\sigma_n\sigma_m,\\
 (\sigma_m\sigma_n)^*&=(-\sigma_m)(-\sigma_n).
 \label{eq:two-sigma-operators}
\end{align}
Both operations act term by term on sums.  Their actions on the four basis
types are
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c c c c c}
  \toprule
  type & scalar & vector & bivector & pseudoscalar\\
  representative & $1$ & $\sigma_n$ & $\ii\sigma_n$ & $\ii$\\
  \midrule
  $(\cdot)^*$ & $+$ & $-$ & $+$ & $-$\\
  $(\cdot)^{\mathsf H}$ & $+$ & $+$ & $-$ & $-$\\
  $(\cdot)^{*\mathsf H}$ & $+$ & $-$ & $-$ & $+$\\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In particular,
\begin{align}
 \sigma_n^*&=-\sigma_n,\\
 \sigma_n^{\mathsf H}&=\sigma_n,\\
 \ii^*&=-\ii,\\
 \ii^{\mathsf H}&=-\ii.
\end{align}
For the bivectors, these actions combine to give
\begin{gather}
 (\ii\sigma_n)^*=\ii^*\sigma_n^*=\ii\sigma_n\\
 (\ii\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}
 =\sigma_n^{\mathsf H}\ii^{\mathsf H}=-\ii\sigma_n.
\end{gather}
Thus sigma conjugation preserves the original order of the factors, whereas
the Hermitian operator changes their order.
For arbitrary algebra elements $Z,Z'$, these definitions give
\[
  (ZZ')^{\mathsf H}=Z'^{\mathsf H}Z^{\mathsf H},
  \qquad
  (ZZ')^*=Z^* Z'^*,
  \qquad
  Z^{*\mathsf H}=Z^{\mathsf H*}.
\]
Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix} reproduces this as Hermitian conjugation
in the Pauli-matrix representation.

\section{Paravectors}
\label{sec:paravectors}

\subsection{Core operations}
\label{sec:algebra-operations}

Let $a,b\in\R$ and $\bA,\bB\in\R^3$.  First form the two real
paravectors
\begin{gather}
 X:=a+\bA\cdot\bsig\\
 Y:=b+\bB\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
Their complex combination is the general complex paravector
\begin{equation}
 Z:=X+\ii Y
 =(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:paravector}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, collect its complex scalar and vector coefficients as
\begin{gather}
 S:=a+b\ii\\
 \bV:=\bA+\bB\ii\\
 Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
The corresponding coefficient-extraction operations are
\begin{gather}
 \scpart Z:=S\\
 \vecpart Z:=\bV.
\end{gather}
Sigma conjugation changes the sign of every sigma generator without changing
multiplied order; the Hermitian operator changes multiplied sigma order.
For the complex coefficients, write
\begin{gather}
 S^{\mathsf H}=S^*:=a-b\ii\\
 \bV^{\mathsf H}=\bV^*:=\bA-\bB\ii.
\end{gather}
For a paravector, the two operations are then
\begin{align}
 Z^*&:=S^*-\bV^*\cdot\bsig
 =(a-b\ii)+(-\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig,\\
 Z^{\mathsf H}&:=S^*+\bV^*\cdot\bsig
 =(a-b\ii)+(\bA-\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:algebra-star-H}
\end{align}
Their composition is the adjugate:
\begin{equation}
 \adj Z:=Z^{*\mathsf H}=S-\bV\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:adjugate}
\end{equation}
Define the paravector trace and determinant by
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{tr}(Z)&:=Z+\adj Z=2S,\\
 \det(Z)&:=\adj Z\,Z=Z\adj Z
 =S^2-\bV\cdot\bV.
 \label{eq:det}
\end{align}
For any paravectors $Z$ and $Z'$, the adjugate reverses product order:
\begin{equation}
 \adj{ZZ'}=\adj{Z'}\adj Z.
\end{equation}
Consequently, for $z^2\ne1$ where the inverse occurs,
\begin{align}
 \det(ZZ')
 &=\adj{ZZ'}ZZ'\\
 &=\adj{Z'}(\adj Z\,Z)Z'\\
 &=\det(Z)\det(Z').
 \label{eq:det-product}
\end{align}
For any complex scalar $c$,
\begin{equation}
 \adj{cZ}=c\adj Z.
\end{equation}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \det(cZ)=c^2\det(Z).
 \label{eq:det-scalar-scaling}
\end{equation}
Whenever $\det(Z)\ne0$, define
\begin{gather}
 Z^{-1}:=\frac{\adj Z}{\det(Z)}
 =\frac{S-\bV\cdot\bsig}{S^2-\bV^2}\\
 Z^{-1}Z=ZZ^{-1}=1.
\end{gather}
Differentiating $Z^{-1}Z=1$ gives the general inverse rule
\begin{equation}
 \dd(Z^{-1})=-Z^{-1}\,\dd Z\,Z^{-1}.
 \label{eq:inverse-differential}
\end{equation}
Both trace and determinant are cyclic:
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{tr}(ZZ')&=\operatorname{tr}(Z'Z),\\
 \det(ZZ')&=\det(Z'Z).
 \label{eq:det-cyclic}
\end{align}
For three factors,
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{tr}(ZZ'Z'')
 &=\operatorname{tr}(Z'Z''Z)
 =\operatorname{tr}(Z''ZZ'),
 \label{eq:trace-cyclic}\\
 \det(ZZ'Z'')
 &=\det(Z'Z''Z)
 =\det(Z''ZZ').
\end{align}
For any complex paravector $Z$ and invertible complex paravector $T$, let
\begin{equation}
 Z':=T^{-1}ZT.
\end{equation}
Cyclicity then gives
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{tr}(Z')
 &=\operatorname{tr}(T^{-1}ZT)
 =\operatorname{tr}(ZTT^{-1})
 =\operatorname{tr}(Z),
 \label{eq:trace-similarity}\\
 \det(Z')
 &=\det(T^{-1}ZT)
 =\det(ZTT^{-1})
 =\det(Z).
 \label{eq:det-similarity}
\end{align}
Replacing $T$ by $T^{-1}$ gives the same two invariances for
$Z'=TZT^{-1}$.
Finally, define real and imaginary paravector extraction by
\begin{align}
 \repart Z&:=\tfrac12(Z+Z^{\mathsf H})=X,\\
 \impart Z&:=\frac{Z-Z^{\mathsf H}}{2\ii}=Y.
 \label{eq:algebra-re-im}
\end{align}
The functions $\operatorname{re}$ and $\operatorname{im}$ also act
componentwise on complex scalars and vectors.  The scalar and vector parts
are recovered by
\begin{align}
  S&=\scpart Z=\tfrac12(Z+Z^{*\mathsf H}),\\
  \bV&=\vecpart Z,\\
  \bV\cdot\bsig&=\tfrac12(Z-Z^{*\mathsf H}).
\end{align}
The four real coefficients then follow by composition:
\begin{align}
 a&=\repart S=\scpart X=\scpart{\repart Z},\\
 b&=\impart S=\scpart Y=\scpart{\impart Z},\\
 \bA&=\repart{\bV}=\vecpart X=\vecpart{\repart Z},\\
 \bB&=\impart{\bV}=\vecpart Y=\vecpart{\impart Z}.
\end{align}
These definitions are intrinsic to the algebra.  Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix}
later reproduces them through the faithful Pauli-matrix representation.

\subsection{Products and quadratic forms}

From the ordered sigma multiplication rule,
\begin{align}
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)(\bB\cdot\bsig)
 &=\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sum_{m=1}^{3}
   A_nB_m\sigma_n\sigma_m\\
 &=\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sum_{m=1}^{3}A_nB_m\delta_{nm}
   +\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sum_{m=1}^{3}\sum_{k=1}^{3}
   A_nB_m\epsilon_{nmk}\ii\sigma_k\\
 &=\bA\cdot\bB+\ii(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:vector-product-derivation}
\end{align}
Adding the product with $\bA$ and $\bB$ interchanged gives
\begin{equation}
 2(\bA\cdot\bB)
 =(\bA\cdot\bsig)(\bB\cdot\bsig)
 +(\bB\cdot\bsig)(\bA\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Consequently, for $z^2\ne1$ where the inverse occurs,
\begin{align}
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)^2&=\bA^2,\\
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)^{2n}&=(\bA^2)^n,\\
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)^{2n+1}&=(\bA^2)^n\bA\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}
Also,
\begin{equation}
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)\bsig=\bA+\ii\bA\times\bsig.
\end{equation}
The corresponding commutator is
\begin{equation}
 [\bA\cdot\bsig,\bB\cdot\bsig]
 =2\ii(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{align}
 [\bA\cdot\bsig,\bsig]&=-2\ii\bA\times\bsig,\\
 [\bA\cdot\ii\bsig,\bsig]&=2\bA\times\bsig,\\
 [\bA\cdot\bsig,\bsig]\cdot\bB
 &=2\ii(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}

\subsection{Quadratic identity and eigenvalues}

Since $Z-S=\bV\cdot\bsig$ and
$(\bV\cdot\bsig)^2=\bV^2$,
\begin{align}
 (Z-S)^2&=\bV^2,\\
 Z^2-2SZ+(S^2-\bV^2)&=0.
\end{align}
Using $\operatorname{tr}(Z)=2S$ and
$\det(Z)=S^2-\bV^2$ gives the equivalent trace--determinant identity
\begin{equation}
 Z^2-\operatorname{tr}(Z)Z+\det(Z)=0.
  \label{eq:minimal}
\end{equation}
Replacing $Z$ by a scalar eigenvalue $\lambda$ and solving this quadratic
gives
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_\pm
 =\frac{\operatorname{tr}(Z)
 \pm\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(Z)^2-4\det(Z)}}{2}
 =S\pm\sqrt{\bV^2}.
\end{equation}
Because $\bV$ is generally complex, $\bV^2=\bV\cdot\bV$ and its square
root may also be complex.
The quadratic identity therefore factors as
\begin{equation}
 (Z-\lambda_-)(Z-\lambda_+)=0.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Real products}

Let
\begin{gather}
 X=a+\bA\cdot\bsig\\
 Y=b+\bB\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
Here $a,b\in\R$ and $\bA,\bB\in\R^3$.
Then
\begin{align}
 XY&=(ab+\bA\cdot\bB)
 +(a\bB+b\bA+\ii\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig,\\
 \scpart{XY}&=ab+\bA\cdot\bB,\\
 \vecpart{XY}&=a\bB+b\bA+\ii\bA\times\bB,\\
 [X,Y]&=2\ii(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig,\\
 X^2&=(a^2+\bA^2)+2a\bA\cdot\bsig,\\
 \scpart{X^2}&=a^2+\bA^2,\\
 \vecpart{X^2}&=2a\bA,\\
 X^2-2aX+(a^2-\bA^2)&=0.
\end{align}
The condition $\bA\times\bB=0$ implies
$XY=YX$ and $X^{-1}Y=YX^{-1}$.

\subsection{Complex products}

For $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$ and $Z'=S'+\bV'\cdot\bsig$,
\begin{equation}
 ZZ'=(SS'+\bV\cdot\bV')+
 \bigl(S\bV'+S'\bV+\ii\bV\times\bV'\bigr)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:para-product}
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{align}
 \scpart{ZZ'}&=SS'+\bV\cdot\bV',\\
 \vecpart{ZZ'}&=S\bV'+S'\bV+\ii\bV\times\bV'.
\end{align}
Equivalently, if $Z=X+\ii Y$ and $Z'=X'+\ii Y'$, then
\begin{equation}
 ZZ'=(XX'-YY')+\ii(XY'+YX').
\end{equation}

Let
\begin{gather}
 Z=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig,
\end{gather}
where $a,b\in\R$ and $\bA,\bB\in\R^3$,
and use primes for a second paravector with real primed components.  The four
real components of $ZZ'$ are
\begin{align}
 \text{real scalar: }&aa'-bb'+\bA\cdot\bA'-\bB\cdot\bB',\\
 \text{imaginary scalar: }&ab'+ba'+\bA\cdot\bB'+\bB\cdot\bA',\\
 \text{real vector: }&a\bA'-b\bB'+a'\bA-b'\bB
                    -\bA\times\bB'-\bB\times\bA',\\
 \text{imaginary vector: }&a\bB'+b\bA'+a'\bB+b'\bA
                    +\bA\times\bA'-\bB\times\bB'.
\end{align}
For the important quadratic products:
\begin{align}
 Z^2&=(a^2-b^2+\bA^2-\bB^2)
 +2\ii(ab+\bA\cdot\bB)
 +2(a\bA-b\bB)\cdot\bsig
 +2\ii(a\bB+b\bA)\cdot\bsig,\\
 ZZ^*&=(a^2+b^2-\bA^2-\bB^2)
 -2(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig
 +2\ii(a\bB-b\bA)\cdot\bsig,\\
 Z^*Z&=(a^2+b^2-\bA^2-\bB^2)
 +2(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig
 +2\ii(a\bB-b\bA)\cdot\bsig,\\
 ZZ^{\mathsf H}&=a^2+b^2+\bA^2+\bB^2
 +2(a\bA+b\bB+\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig,\\
 Z^{\mathsf H}Z&=a^2+b^2+\bA^2+\bB^2
 +2(a\bA+b\bB-\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig,\\
 ZZ^{*\mathsf H}&=\det(Z)
 =a^2-b^2-\bA^2+\bB^2
 +2\ii(ab-\bA\cdot\bB).
\end{align}
The scalar part is cyclic, $\scpart{ZZ'}=\scpart{Z'Z}$.  The operator laws
already established also give
$(ZZ')^{*\mathsf H}=Z'^{*\mathsf H}Z^{*\mathsf H}$.
The opposite cross-product signs follow the displayed product order.  Thus
$ZZ^*=Z^*Z$ and $ZZ^{\mathsf H}=Z^{\mathsf H}Z$ precisely when
$\bA\times\bB=0$.

\begin{samepage}
\paragraph{Norms.}
For $S=a+b\ii$ and $\bV=\bA+\bB\ii$, with real components
$a,b,\bA,\bB$, define
\begin{align}
 \lVert S\rVert^2&:=SS^*=S^*S=a^2+b^2,\\
 \lVert\bV\rVert^2&:=\bV\cdot\bV^*=\bV^*\cdot\bV
 =\bA^2+\bB^2,\\
 \lVert Z\rVert^2&:=\lVert S\rVert^2+\lVert\bV\rVert^2.
 \label{eq:coefficient-norm}
\end{align}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
 \lVert Z\rVert^2=\scpart{Z^{\mathsf H}Z}
 =\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(Z^{\mathsf H}Z)
 =\scpart{ZZ^{\mathsf H}}
 =\scpart{Z^*Z^{*\mathsf H}}
 =\scpart{Z^{*\mathsf H}Z^*}.
 \label{eq:paravector-norm}
\end{equation}
These norms are not algebraic squares; in general,
\begin{gather}
 S^2\ne\lVert S\rVert^2\\
 \bV^2\ne\lVert\bV\rVert^2.
\end{gather}
For any complex scalar $c$,
\begin{equation}
 \lVert cZ\rVert^2=\lVert c\rVert^2\lVert Z\rVert^2.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Comparison with the determinant.}
The norm is real and nonnegative, whereas $\det(Z)$ may be
complex.  For $c=r e^{\ii\theta}$ with real $r$ and $\theta$, the
determinant retains the phase while the norm does not:
\begin{align}
 \det(r e^{\ii\theta}Z)
 &=r^2\det(Z)e^{2\ii\theta},\\
 \lVert r e^{\ii\theta}Z\rVert^2
 &=r^2\lVert Z\rVert^2.
\end{align}
\end{samepage}

\subsection{Projection operators}
\label{sec:algebra-projectors}

For a real unit vector $\bu$, define
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_\pm:=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Since $(\bu\cdot\bsig)^2=1$,
\begin{align}
 \Pi_\pm^2&=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_+\Pi_-&=0,\\
 \Pi_++\Pi_-&=1,\\
 \Pi_\pm^{\mathsf H}&=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_\pm^*&=\Pi_\mp,\\
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)\Pi_\pm&=\pm \Pi_\pm.
\end{align}
Thus $\bu\cdot\bsig=\Pi_+-\Pi_-$.  For two unit directions,
\begin{align}
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)(\bu'\cdot\bsig)
 &=\bu\cdot\bu'+\ii(\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 [\bu\cdot\bsig,\bu'\cdot\bsig]
 &=2\ii(\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)(\bu'\cdot\bsig)(\bu\cdot\bsig)
 &=\left(2(\bu\cdot\bu')\bu-\bu'\right)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}
Define $\bu'_{\parallel}=(\bu'\cdot\bu)\bu$ and
$\bu'_{\perp}=\bu'-\bu'_{\parallel}$.  Then
\begin{align}
 \Pi_\pm(\bu'\cdot\bsig)\Pi_\pm
 &=\pm(\bu\cdot\bu')\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_\pm(\bu'\cdot\bsig)\Pi_\mp
 &=\tfrac12(\bu'_{\perp}\pm\ii(\bu\times\bu'))\cdot\bsig\,
 \Pi_\mp.
\end{align}
For comparison, define
$\Pi_\pm':=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu'\cdot\bsig)$.  Their products are
\begin{align}
 \Pi_+\Pi_+'&=\tfrac14(1+\bu\cdot\bu')
 +\tfrac14(+\bu+\bu'+\ii\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 \Pi_+\Pi_-'&=\tfrac14(1-\bu\cdot\bu')
 +\tfrac14(+\bu-\bu'-\ii\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 \Pi_-\Pi_+'&=\tfrac14(1-\bu\cdot\bu')
 +\tfrac14(-\bu+\bu'-\ii\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 \Pi_-\Pi_-'&=\tfrac14(1+\bu\cdot\bu')
 +\tfrac14(-\bu-\bu'+\ii\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}

\subsection{Ladder operators}
\label{sec:ladder-operators}

For projections along the third axis, define
\begin{gather}
 Z_\pm=\tfrac12(\sigma_1\pm\ii\sigma_2)\\
 \Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm\sigma_3).
\end{gather}
Then
\begin{align}
 [\sigma_3,Z_\pm]&=\pm2Z_\pm,\\
 Z_\pm^2&=0,\\
 Z_\pm Z_\mp&=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \sigma_1&=Z_++Z_-,\\
 \sigma_2&=(Z_+-Z_-)/\ii,\\
 \sigma_3&=\Pi_+-\Pi_-.
\end{align}
The ladder operators map between the two projected sectors according to
\begin{align}
 Z_+\Pi_+&=0,\\Z_+\Pi_-&=Z_+,\\
 \Pi_+Z_+&=Z_+,\\\Pi_-Z_+&=0,\\
 Z_-\Pi_-&=0,\\Z_-\Pi_+&=Z_-,\\
 \Pi_-Z_-&=Z_-,\\\Pi_+Z_-&=0.
\end{align}

\subsection{Functions of paravectors}
\label{sec:spectral}

\paragraph{General projector rule.}
Let
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_+,\lambda_-\in\C.
\end{equation}
Suppose $\Pi_\pm^2=\Pi_\pm$, $\Pi_++\Pi_-=1$,
$\Pi_+\Pi_-=\Pi_-\Pi_+=0$, and
\begin{equation}
 Z=\lambda_+\Pi_++\lambda_-\Pi_-.
\end{equation}
Then orthogonality gives
\begin{equation}
 Z^n=\lambda_+^n\Pi_++\lambda_-^n\Pi_-.
\end{equation}
Thus, for a convergent power series $f(w)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_nw^n$,
\begin{align}
 f(Z)&=\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_nZ^n\\
 &=f(\lambda_+)\Pi_++f(\lambda_-)\Pi_-.
\end{align}

\paragraph{Functions of a unit vector.}
For $X=\bu\cdot\bsig=\Pi_+-\Pi_-$, the eigenvalues are $\pm1$.
The general rule and $\Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig)$ give
\begin{equation}
 f(\bu\cdot\bsig)=f(1)\Pi_++f(-1)\Pi_-
 =\tfrac12(f(1)+f(-1))+\tfrac12(f(1)-f(-1))\bu\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{align}
 \sqrt{\bu\cdot\bsig}&=\pm \Pi_+\pm\ii \Pi_-,\\
 \log(\bu\cdot\bsig)&=2\ii\pi(n\Pi_++(\tfrac12+m)\Pi_-),\\
 (z+\bu\cdot\bsig)^{-1}&=\frac{z-\bu\cdot\bsig}{z^2-1},\\
 \frac{\ii-\bu\cdot\bsig}{\ii+\bu\cdot\bsig}
 &=\ii\bu\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}
For $0<\lVert\br\rVert<1$, take $\bu=\br/\lVert\br\rVert$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{1-\br\cdot\bsig}{1+\br\cdot\bsig}
 =\exp(2\tanh^{-1}(\lVert\br\rVert))^{-1}\Pi_+
 +\exp(2\tanh^{-1}(\lVert\br\rVert))\Pi_-.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Real paravectors.}
Let $X=a+\bA\cdot\bsig$ and $Y=b+\bB\cdot\bsig$, where
$a,b\in\R$ and $\bA,\bB\in\R^3$.  For $\bA\ne0$, put
\begin{gather}
 \bu:=\frac{\bA}{\lVert\bA\rVert}\\
 \theta:=\lVert\bA\rVert.
\end{gather}
Then define
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_\pm:=a\pm\theta.
\end{equation}
Using
$1=\Pi_++\Pi_-$ and $\bu\cdot\bsig=\Pi_+-\Pi_-$ gives
\begin{align}
 X&=a(\Pi_++\Pi_-)
 +\lVert\bA\rVert(\Pi_+-\Pi_-)\\
 &=\lambda_+\Pi_++\lambda_-\Pi_-.
\end{align}
The general rule therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 f(X)=f(\lambda_+)\Pi_++f(\lambda_-)\Pi_-.
\end{equation}
With nonzero eigenvalues where required and chosen function branches, this
includes
\begin{align}
 X^n&=\tfrac12(\lambda_+^n+\lambda_-^n)
 +\tfrac12(\lambda_+^n-\lambda_-^n)\bu\cdot\bsig,\\
 X^{-1}&=\lambda_+^{-1}\Pi_++\lambda_-^{-1}\Pi_-,\\
 \sqrt X&=\pm\sqrt{\lambda_+}\Pi_+\pm\sqrt{\lambda_-}\Pi_-,\\
 \log X&=\log(\lambda_+)\Pi_++\log(\lambda_-)\Pi_-,\\
 \exp(\bA\cdot\bsig)
 &=\exp(\theta\bu\cdot\bsig)
 =\cosh\theta+\sinh\theta\,\bu\cdot\bsig,\\
 \exp(\ii\bA\cdot\bsig)
 &=\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig)
 =\cos\theta+\ii\sin\theta\,\bu\cdot\bsig,\\
 \exp(X)&=\exp(a)\exp(\bA\cdot\bsig)
 =\exp(a)\left(\cosh\theta
 +\sinh\theta\,\bu\cdot\bsig\right).
\end{align}
The real vector exponential has a direct kinematic interpretation.  Define
\begin{gather}
 \gamma:=\cosh\theta\\
 \bv:=\tanh\theta\,\bu.
\end{gather}
Then $\gamma=(1-\bv^2)^{-1/2}$ and
\begin{equation}
 \exp(\bA\cdot\bsig)
 =\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Thus the magnitude of the exponent is the rapidity, while its direction is
the direction of motion.  This is the normalized future-directed
paravector used below for four-velocity.
The spectral inverse agrees with the adjugate--determinant formula developed
earlier.  Indeed,
\begin{gather}
 \adj{X}=\lambda_-\Pi_++\lambda_+\Pi_-\\
 \det(X)=\lambda_+\lambda_-,
\end{gather}
and therefore, when $\lambda_+\lambda_-\ne0$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\adj{X}}{\det(X)}
 =\lambda_+^{-1}\Pi_++\lambda_-^{-1}\Pi_-
 =X^{-1}.
\end{equation}
The formulas extend continuously to $\bA=0$.

For direct component formulas, if $X^2=Y$, then
\begin{gather}
 a^2=\tfrac12\left(b\pm\sqrt{b^2-\bB^2}\right)\\
 \bA^2=b-a^2,
\end{gather}
with signs and vector direction chosen consistently.  Conversely, where
$b^2>\bB^2$, $b>0$, and $\bB\ne0$,
\begin{equation}
 \log Y=\tfrac12\log(b^2-\bB^2)
 +\tanh^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\lVert\bB\rVert}{b}\right)
 \frac{\bB}{\lVert\bB\rVert}\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Complex paravectors.}
For $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, with $S\in\C$ and $\bV\in\C^3$, let
$z:=\sqrt{\bV^2}\in\C$ and define
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_\pm:=S\pm z.
\end{equation}
When $z\ne0$, define
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_\pm:=\tfrac12\left(1\pm\frac{\bV\cdot\bsig}{z}\right).
\end{equation}
Because $(\bV\cdot\bsig)^2=\bV^2=z^2$,
\begin{align}
 \Pi_\pm^2
 &=\tfrac14\left(1\pm2\frac{\bV\cdot\bsig}{z}
 +\frac{(\bV\cdot\bsig)^2}{z^2}\right)=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_+\Pi_-
 &=\tfrac14\left(1-\frac{(\bV\cdot\bsig)^2}{z^2}\right)=0.
\end{align}
Also, $1=\Pi_++\Pi_-$ and
$\bV\cdot\bsig=z(\Pi_+-\Pi_-)$, so
\begin{align}
 Z&=S(\Pi_++\Pi_-)+z(\Pi_+-\Pi_-)\\
 &=\lambda_+\Pi_++\lambda_-\Pi_-.
\end{align}
The general rule gives
\begin{equation}
 f(Z)=f(\lambda_+)\Pi_++f(\lambda_-)\Pi_-.
 \label{eq:complex-function-projectors}
\end{equation}
The adjugate and determinant have the corresponding spectral forms
\begin{gather}
 \adj{Z}=\lambda_-\Pi_++\lambda_+\Pi_-\\
 \det(Z)=\lambda_+\lambda_-.
\end{gather}
Consequently, when $\lambda_+\lambda_-\ne0$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\adj{Z}}{\det(Z)}
 =\lambda_+^{-1}\Pi_++\lambda_-^{-1}\Pi_-
 =Z^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Choosing square-root branches gives
\begin{align}
 \sqrt Z
 &=\sqrt{\lambda_+}\Pi_++\sqrt{\lambda_-}\Pi_-\\
 &=\tfrac12(\sqrt{\lambda_+}+\sqrt{\lambda_-})
 +\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_+}-\sqrt{\lambda_-}}{2z}\,
 \bV\cdot\bsig,
\end{align}
and, when $\lambda_\pm\ne0$, choosing logarithm branches gives
\begin{align}
 \log Z
 &=\log(\lambda_+)\Pi_++\log(\lambda_-)\Pi_-\\
 &=\tfrac12(\log(\lambda_+)+\log(\lambda_-))
 +\frac{\log(\lambda_+)-\log(\lambda_-)}{2z}\,
 \bV\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}
\paragraph{Exponential of a general complex paravector.}
Because the scalar $S$ commutes with $\bV\cdot\bsig$,
\begin{equation}
 \exp(Z)=\exp(S)\exp(\bV\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
For every nonnegative integer $n$, the identity
$(\bV\cdot\bsig)^2=z^2$ gives
\begin{gather}
 (\bV\cdot\bsig)^{2n}=z^{2n}\\
 (\bV\cdot\bsig)^{2n+1}=z^{2n}\bV\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
Separating the defining power series into even and odd powers therefore gives
\begin{align}
 \exp(\bV\cdot\bsig)
 &=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2n}}{(2n)!}
 +\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{2n}}{(2n+1)!}\,\bV\cdot\bsig\\
 &=\cosh z+\frac{\sinh z}{z}\,\bV\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}
Equivalently, the projector rule gives
\begin{equation}
 \exp(Z)
 =\exp(\lambda_+)\Pi_++\exp(\lambda_-)\Pi_-
 =\exp(S)\left(\cosh z+\frac{\sinh z}{z}\,\bV\cdot\bsig\right).
\end{equation}

For the fully general coefficients
\begin{equation}
 Z=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig,
\end{equation}
the complex vector magnitude is fixed by
\begin{equation}
 z^2=\bV^2
 =\bA^2-\bB^2+2\ii\bA\cdot\bB.
\end{equation}
Thus, after choosing either square-root branch,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \exp(Z)
 ={}&\exp(a)(\cos b+\ii\sin b)\\
 &{}\times\left[
 \cosh\!\sqrt{\bA^2-\bB^2+2\ii\bA\cdot\bB}\right.\\
 &\left.\qquad
 +\frac{\sinh\!\sqrt{\bA^2-\bB^2+2\ii\bA\cdot\bB}}
 {\sqrt{\bA^2-\bB^2+2\ii\bA\cdot\bB}}\,
 (\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig
 \right].
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Changing the square-root branch changes both $z$ and $\sinh(z)$ in sign, so
the result is branch independent.  If $z=0$, including the case of a
nonzero complex-null vector $\bV$, the analytic limit is
\begin{equation}
 \exp(Z)=\exp(S)(1+\bV\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Finally, the power series respects both conjugation operations:
\begin{gather}
 \exp(Z^*)=\exp(Z)^*\\
 \exp(Z^{\mathsf H})=\exp(Z)^{\mathsf H}.
\end{gather}
The square-root and logarithm formulas above are likewise understood by
analytic continuation wherever their chosen branches are regular.

\subsection{Differentiated fields}

For products of fields,
\begin{equation}
 \partial(ZZ')=(\partial Z)Z'+Z(\partial Z')
 +[\bsig,Z]\cdot\pdr Z'.
\end{equation}
Let $F=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig$ depend on $(t,\br)$.  Then
\begin{align}
 \partial F={}&(\partial_ta+\pdr\cdot\bA)
 +\ii(\partial_tb+\pdr\cdot\bB)\\
 &+(\pdr a+\partial_t\bA-\pdr\times\bB)\cdot\bsig\\
 &+\ii(\pdr b+\pdr\times\bA+\partial_t\bB)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}
Multiplication by $\ii$ rotates these four components: the real scalar becomes
$-\partial_tb-\pdr\cdot\bB$, the imaginary scalar becomes
$\partial_ta+\pdr\cdot\bA$, the real vector becomes the negative of the
imaginary vector above, and the imaginary vector becomes the real vector.

\section{Transformations and invariants}
\label{sec:preserved-quantities}

The transformations used below are organized by the quantities they leave
unchanged:
\begin{gather}
 \operatorname{tr}(Z)=2\scpart Z\\
 \det(Z)\\
 \lVert Z\rVert^2
 =\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(Z^{\mathsf H}Z).
\end{gather}
The trace is linear; the determinant and norm are quadratic.  Here $T$ and
$T'$ denote general complex paravectors, with $T'$ a second transformation
factor rather than a derivative.  The symbol $U$ denotes a complex unitary
vector:
\begin{align}
 U^{\mathsf H}U=UU^{\mathsf H}&=1
 \label{eq:unitary-transform}\\
 U^{\mathsf H}&=U^{-1}.
\end{align}
Here $U$ is a transformation; Section~\ref{sec:kinematics} later reuses the
symbol for four-velocity.

\paragraph{Trace invariance.}
For invertible $T$, consider either similarity:
\begin{gather}
 Z'=T^{-1}ZT,\\
 Z'=TZT^{-1}.
\end{gather}
For example, cyclicity gives
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{tr}(T^{-1}ZT)
 =\operatorname{tr}(ZTT^{-1})
 =\operatorname{tr}(Z).
\end{equation}
Thus $\operatorname{tr}(Z')=\operatorname{tr}(Z)$.  For unitary $U$, the
same similarities are
\begin{gather}
 Z'=U^{\mathsf H}ZU,\\
 Z'=UZU^{\mathsf H}.
\end{gather}

\paragraph{Determinant invariance.}
For the general two-sided transformation $Z'=T'ZT$,
\begin{equation}
 \det(Z')=\det(T')\det(T)\det(Z).
\end{equation}
Hence $\det(T')\det(T)=1$ guarantees
$\det(Z')=\det(Z)$, and is required if this equality is to hold for arbitrary
$Z$.  Equivalently, when $\det(T)\ne0$, any $T'$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
 \det(T')=\frac{1}{\det(T)}
\end{equation}
preserves the determinant of every $Z$.  The automatic choice
$T'=T^{-1}$ gives the similarity
\begin{equation}
 Z'=T^{-1}ZT.
\end{equation}
The opposite ordering $Z'=TZT^{-1}$ preserves the determinant as well.
For unitary $U$,
\begin{equation}
 \det(U^{\mathsf H})\det(U)
 =\det(U^{-1})\det(U)=1,
\end{equation}
so both unitary sandwiches preserve the determinant.

\paragraph{Norm invariance.}
Norm invariance requires the unitary condition
\eqref{eq:unitary-transform}.  For example, if $Z'=U^{\mathsf H}ZU$, then
\begin{align}
 \lVert Z'\rVert^2
 &=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}
   (U^{\mathsf H}Z^{\mathsf H}UU^{\mathsf H}ZU)\\
 &=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(Z^{\mathsf H}Z)
 =\lVert Z\rVert^2.
\end{align}
The same equality $\lVert Z'\rVert^2=\lVert Z\rVert^2$ holds for
\begin{gather}
 Z'=UZ\\
 Z'=ZU\\
 Z'=U^{\mathsf H}ZU\\
 Z'=UZU^{\mathsf H}.
\end{gather}
It does not hold in general when $T$ is nonunitary.

Rotations and boosts preserve different combinations of these invariants.
A rotation satisfies
\begin{gather}
 R^{\mathsf H}=R^{-1}\\
 R^{\mathsf H}R=RR^{\mathsf H}=1,
\end{gather}
so $R$ is unitary; $Z'=R^{\mathsf H}ZR$ preserves trace, determinant, and
norm.  A nonzero boost satisfies
\begin{gather}
 L^{\mathsf H}=L\\
 \det(L)=1\\
 L^{\mathsf H}L=L^2\ne1,
\end{gather}
so $L$ is not unitary.  The transformation $Z'=L^{-1}ZL^{-1}$ preserves the
determinant but does not generally preserve the trace or norm.

\section{Space--time kinematics}
\label{sec:kinematics}

\subsection{Intervals, velocity, and momentum}

Represent a differential event by the real paravector
\begin{gather}
  \dd X=\dd t+\dd\br\cdot\bsig\\
  \dd X^*=\dd t-\dd\br\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
The Minkowski interval is
\begin{equation}
 \dd s^2=\Det{\dd X}=\dd X\,\dd X^*
 =\dd t^2-\dd\br^2.
 \label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
For any invertible complex paravector $T$, define
\begin{equation}
 \dd X':=T^{-1}\,\dd X\,T.
\end{equation}
The cyclic determinant property gives
\begin{equation}
 \Det{\dd X'}
 =\Det{T^{-1}\,\dd X\,T}
 =\Det{\dd X\,T\,T^{-1}}
 =\Det{\dd X}
 =\dd s^2.
\end{equation}
Thus similarity by any such $T$ leaves the determinant unchanged.  This is
an algebraic change of basis; even when $T=L$, it is not the physical boost
of a space--time paravector.  The latter is the congruence
$\dd X'=L^{-1}\dd X L^{-1}$ developed in Section~\ref{sec:boosts}.  The alternative
similarity ordering $T\,\dd X\,T^{-1}$ has the same determinant invariance.
For a future-directed time-like interval, choose the positive scalar root
\begin{equation}
 \dd s:=\sqrt{\det(\dd X)}.
\end{equation}
The sign of the invariant squared interval classifies the causal relation
between the two neighboring events:
\begin{align}
 \dd s^2>0&:\quad\text{time-like; a massive particle can follow the interval},\\
 \dd s^2=0&:\quad\text{null; light can follow the interval},\\
 \dd s^2<0&:\quad\text{space-like; no causal signal can follow the interval}.
\end{align}
Only in the time-like case is \(\dd s\) real and nonzero under the chosen
positive-root convention; along a time-like worldline it is the proper-time
differential.
Thus the following kinematics explores the determinant of $\dd X$ and its
normalization by this scalar.  For two intervals,
\begin{equation}
 [\dd X,\dd X']=2\ii(\dd\br\times\dd\br')\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
When $\dd s^2\ne0$,
\begin{equation}
 \dd X^{-1}
 =\frac{\adj{\dd X}}{\det(\dd X)}
 =\frac{\dd X^*}{\dd s^2}.
\end{equation}
A null interval has $\dd t^2=\dd\br^2$ and no inverse.  Along a
future-directed time-like worldline, $s$ is proper time.  Define the
coordinate velocity by
\begin{equation}
 \bv:=\frac{\dd\br}{\dd t}.
\end{equation}
Then $\dd\br=\bv\dd t$, and the positive proper-time increment satisfies
\begin{gather}
 \dd s^2=(1-\bv^2)\dd t^2\\
 \dd s=\sqrt{1-\bv^2}\,\dd t\\
 \gamma:=\frac1{\sqrt{1-\bv^2}}\\
 \dd s=\frac{\dd t}{\gamma}\\
 \gamma=\frac{\dd t}{\dd s}.
\end{gather}
For any quantity evaluated along the worldline, the one-variable chain rule
therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd}{\dd s}
 =\frac{\dd t}{\dd s}\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
 =\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}.
\end{equation}
In particular, the proper velocity is
\begin{equation}
 U:=\frac{\dd X}{\dd s}=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Using the scalar-scaling rule~\eqref{eq:det-scalar-scaling}, its determinant is
\begin{equation}
 \det(U)
 =\det\left(\frac{\dd X}{\dd s}\right)
 =\frac{\det(\dd X)}{\dd s^2}
 =1.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $\det(U)=\gamma^2(1-\bv^2)=1$, so
\begin{equation}
 U^{-1}=\frac{\adj U}{\det(U)}=U^*.
\end{equation}
Proper time therefore obeys
\begin{equation}
 \tau=\int\dd s
 =\int\sqrt{1-\bv^2}\,\dd t
 =\int\frac{\dd t}{\gamma}.
\end{equation}
The four-momentum is the paravector
\begin{equation}
 mU=m\gamma+m\gamma\bv\cdot\bsig
 =E+\bP\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:momentum}
\end{equation}
Its scalar and vector components define
\begin{gather}
 E:=m\gamma\\
 \bP:=m\gamma\bv.
\end{gather}
Taking its determinant gives the mass-shell relation
\begin{equation}
 \det(mU)=m^2\det(U)=m^2
 =\det(E+\bP\cdot\bsig)=E^2-\bP^2,
\end{equation}
or $E^2=\bP^2+m^2$; for $m=0$, $E^2=\bP^2$.

For coordinate acceleration $\ba=\dd\bv/\dd t$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd\gamma}{\dd t}=\gamma^3\bv\cdot\ba,
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd U}{\dd s}
 =\gamma^4(\bv\cdot\ba)
 +\left(\gamma^4(\bv\cdot\ba)\bv+\gamma^2\ba\right)\cdot\bsig,
\end{equation}
Differentiating $U^*U=1$ gives
\begin{gather}
 \frac{\dd U^*}{\dd s}U+U^*\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}=0\\
 \scpart{U^*\dd U/\dd s}=0.
\end{gather}
If $A=\dd U/\dd s$, then
\begin{equation}
 \Det A=-\gamma^4(\ba^2+\gamma^2(\bv\cdot\ba)^2).
\end{equation}
For $\bu=\bv/\lVert\bv\rVert$, define
$\ba_{\parallel}=(\ba\cdot\bu)\bu$ and
$\ba_{\perp}=\ba-\ba_{\parallel}$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \Det A=-\gamma^4(\ba_{\perp}^2+\gamma^2\ba_{\parallel}^2).
\end{equation}
The product $U^* A$ has zero scalar part, real vector
$\gamma^3\ba$, and imaginary vector $-\gamma^3\bv\times\ba$; while $UA$
has real scalar $2\gamma^5\bv\cdot\ba$, real vector
$\gamma^3\ba+2\gamma^5(\bv\cdot\ba)\bv$, and imaginary vector
$\gamma^3\bv\times\ba$.

\subsection{Varying transformations and second differentials}
\label{sec:varying-transformations}

Along a path, let $\dd$ denote successive differentiation with respect to
its parameter and define
\begin{equation}
 \dd X'=T^{-1}\,\dd X\,T.
\end{equation}
Although $\det(\dd X')=\det(\dd X)$ at each point, a varying $T$ adds a
term to the second differential.  Define
\begin{gather}
 \dd\Theta:=T^{-1}\dd T\\
 \dd T^{-1}=-\dd\Theta\,T^{-1}.
\end{gather}
Direct differentiation gives
\begin{align}
 \dd^2X'
 &=\dd(T^{-1}\,\dd X\,T)\\
 &=T^{-1}\,\dd^2X\,T-\dd\Theta\,\dd X'+\dd X'\,\dd\Theta\\
 &=T^{-1}\,\dd^2X\,T+[\dd X',\dd\Theta].
\end{align}
Dividing by the corresponding powers of $\dd s$, define
\begin{equation}
 \Omega:=\frac{\dd\Theta}{\dd s}
 =T^{-1}\,\frac{\dd T}{\dd s},
\end{equation}
so, with $U=\dd X/\dd s$ and $U'=\dd X'/\dd s$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd U'}{\dd s}
 =T^{-1}\,\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\,T+[U',\Omega].
\end{equation}
For constant $T$, the connection term vanishes.  For varying $T$, it is the
algebraic source of the additional terms in rotating and accelerating
frames and is the pathwise prototype of a covariant derivative.

Here $\dd X'$ is defined by the pointwise transformation of the tangent.
When $T$ varies, it is not generally $\dd(T^{-1}XT)$, which also contains
the transformation of $X$ itself.  Moreover, a general complex $T$ can
make $\dd X'$ complex even when $\dd X$ is real, so a physical space--time
transformation requires an additional reality-preserving condition.

The corresponding local algebra basis is
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_n':=T^{-1}\sigma_nT.
\end{equation}
Similarity preserves all products, so the transformed generators obey the
same sigma algebra.  If $T$ varies along the path, direct differentiation
gives
\begin{gather}
 \dd\sigma_n'=[\sigma_n',\dd\Theta]\\
 \dd\Theta=T^{-1}\dd T.
\end{gather}
This equation describes the change of a local algebra basis.  It should not
be identified with a physical Lorentz transformation until the action of
$T$ and the condition preserving real space--time paravectors have both been
specified.  In particular, the complex boost-like terms obtained from a
completely general $T$ are connection coefficients, not automatically
components of a real physical velocity.

\subsection{Free-particle action}

The free-particle action and Lagrangian are
\begin{gather}
 \int L\dd t=-m\int\dd s\\
 L=-m\sqrt{1-\bv^2}=-\frac m\gamma.
\end{gather}
For a general Lagrangian $L(t,\br,\bv)$,
\begin{gather}
 \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\partial_{\bv}L=\pdr L\\
 H=\bv\cdot\partial_{\bv}L-L\\
 \frac{\dd H}{\dd t}=-\partial_tL.
\end{gather}
Thus spatial translation invariance conserves canonical momentum and time
translation invariance conserves the Hamiltonian.
For the free-particle Lagrangian,
\begin{align}
 \partial_{\bv}L&=m\gamma\bv,\\
 H&=m\gamma\bv^2+\frac m\gamma
 =m\gamma(\bv^2+\gamma^{-2})=m\gamma.
\end{align}
At low velocity, the binomial expansions are
\begin{gather}
 \sqrt{1-\bv^2}=1-\tfrac12\bv^2+O(v^4)\\
 \gamma=1+\tfrac12\bv^2+O(v^4).
\end{gather}
Multiplying the first expansion by $-m$ gives
\begin{equation}
 L=-m\sqrt{1-\bv^2}
 =-m+\tfrac12m\bv^2+O(v^4),
\end{equation}
while the second gives
\begin{equation}
 H=m\gamma=m+\tfrac12m\bv^2+O(v^4).
\end{equation}
The exact relativistic kinetic energy is therefore
\begin{equation}
 K:=H-m=m(\gamma-1),
 \label{eq:kinetic-energy}
\end{equation}
and, apart from the constant rest-energy term, both expansions recover the
nonrelativistic kinetic energy $\tfrac12m\bv^2$.

\section{Electromagnetism}
\label{sec:electromagnetism}

\subsection{Field equation and potentials}

Introduce the paravector differential operator, electromagnetic field, and
source
\begin{gather}
 \partial=\partial_t+\bsig\cdot\pdr\\
 F=(\bE+\bBf\ii)\cdot\bsig\\
 D=\rho+\bJ\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
All four Maxwell equations are the single equation
\begin{equation}
 \partial F=\sconj D=\rho-\bJ\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:maxwell}
\end{equation}
Sigma conjugation gives the equivalent equation
\begin{equation}
 \partial^*F^*=D.
\end{equation}
Expanding the left side by component gives
\begin{align}
 \partial F={}&\underbrace{\pdr\cdot\bE}_{\text{real scalar}}
 +\underbrace{\ii\pdr\cdot\bBf}_{\text{imaginary scalar}}\\
 &+\underbrace{(\partial_t\bE-\pdr\times\bBf)\cdot\bsig}
 _{\text{real vector}}\\
 &+\underbrace{\ii(\partial_t\bBf+\pdr\times\bE)\cdot\bsig}
 _{\text{imaginary vector}}.
\end{align}
Equating these four components with those of
$\rho-\bJ\cdot\bsig$ gives, respectively,
\begin{align}
 \text{real scalar:}\quad&\pdr\cdot\bE=\rho,\\
 \text{imaginary scalar:}\quad&\pdr\cdot\bBf=0,\\
 \text{real vector:}\quad&\pdr\times\bBf=\bJ+\partial_t\bE,\\
 \text{imaginary vector:}\quad&\pdr\times\bE=-\partial_t\bBf.
\end{align}
The conjugate operator products give
\begin{equation}
 \partial\sconj\partial=\sconj\partial\,\partial
 =\partial_t^2-\pdr^2=:\Box,
\end{equation}
whereas
\begin{equation}
 \partial^2=\partial_t^2+\pdr^2+2\partial_t\pdr\cdot\bsig
 \ne\Box.
\end{equation}
Applying $\sconj\partial$ to~\eqref{eq:maxwell} gives
\begin{align}
 \Box F
 &=\sconj\partial\sconj D\\
 &=(\partial_t\rho+\pdr\cdot\bJ)
 +(-\partial_t\bJ-\pdr\rho+\ii\pdr\times\bJ)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}
The real-scalar component is charge conservation, and the real- and
imaginary-vector components are the electric and magnetic wave equations:
\begin{align}
 \text{real scalar:}\quad&\partial_t\rho+\pdr\cdot\bJ=0,\\
 \text{real vector:}\quad&\Box\bE=-\partial_t\bJ-\pdr\rho,\\
 \text{imaginary vector:}\quad&\Box\bBf=\pdr\times\bJ.
\end{align}
In vacuum, $\rho=0$ and $\bJ=0$, so
\begin{gather}
 \Box\bE=0\\
 \Box\bBf=0.
\end{gather}

For the potential $\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$,
\begin{align}
 \partial\Phi={}&
 \underbrace{\partial_tV+\pdr\cdot\bA}_{\text{real scalar}}
 +\underbrace{(\partial_t\bA+\pdr V)\cdot\bsig}_{\text{real vector}}\\
 &+\underbrace{\ii(\pdr\times\bA)\cdot\bsig}_{\text{imaginary vector}}.
\end{align}
Define the scalar component
\begin{equation}
 S:=\scpart{\partial\Phi}=\partial_tV+\pdr\cdot\bA.
\end{equation}
Then $\partial\Phi=S+F^*$: its real-vector and imaginary-vector components
give
\begin{gather}
 \bE=-\partial_t\bA-\pdr V\\
 \bBf=\pdr\times\bA.
\end{gather}
Also, $F=\partial^*\Phi^*-S$, so
\begin{align}
 \partial F
 &=\partial\partial^*\Phi^*-\partial S
 =\Box\Phi^*-\partial S\\
 &=(\Box V-\partial_tS)
 -(\Box\bA+\pdr S)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}
Comparing its real-scalar and real-vector components with
$\partial F=\rho-\bJ\cdot\bsig$ gives
\begin{align}
 \rho&=\Box V-\partial_tS
 \label{eq:sources-from-potential}
 \\
 \bJ&=\Box\bA+\pdr S.
 \label{eq:current-from-potential}
\end{align}
For a scalar field $\lambda$, the gauge transformation is
\begin{equation}
 \Phi' = \Phi+\sconj\partial\lambda,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \partial\Phi'=\partial\Phi+\partial\sconj\partial\lambda
 =\partial\Phi+\Box\lambda.
\end{equation}
Because $\Box\lambda$ is scalar, only the scalar component changes:
\begin{gather}
 S'=S+\Box\lambda\\
 \vecpart{\partial\Phi'}=\vecpart{\partial\Phi}.
\end{gather}
Thus $F$ is unchanged.  In the Lorenz gauge $S=0$, equation
\eqref{eq:sources-from-potential} reduces to
\begin{gather}
 \rho=\Box V\\
 \bJ=\Box\bA.
\end{gather}

\subsection{Field invariants}

Electric and magnetic fields mix under Lorentz transformations, so their
individual magnitudes depend on the observer.  Two combinations remain the
same for every inertial observer and therefore classify the electromagnetic
field itself.

For
\begin{equation}
 F=(\bE+\bBf\ii)\cdot\bsig,
\end{equation}
the scalar part vanishes, and therefore $\adj F=-F$.  Hence
\begin{align}
 \Det F
 &=-F^2\\
 &=-(\bE+\bBf\ii)\cdot(\bE+\bBf\ii)\\
 &=-\bE^2+\bBf^2-2\ii\bE\cdot\bBf.
\end{align}
For any invertible real or complex paravector $T$, let
\begin{equation}
 F'=T^{-1}FT.
\end{equation}
The general similarity identity~\eqref{eq:det-similarity} gives
\begin{equation}
 \Det{F'}=\Det{T^{-1}FT}=\Det F.
\end{equation}
Rotations and boosts are the physical special cases
\begin{gather}
 F'=R^{-1}FR=R^{\mathsf H}FR\\
 F'=L^{-1}FL=L^*FL,
\end{gather}
because $R^{-1}=R^{\mathsf H}$ and $L^{-1}=L^*$.
Hence every inertial observer agrees on
\begin{align}
 a&=\bE^2-\bBf^2=-\repart{\Det F},\\
 b&=\bE\cdot\bBf=-\tfrac12\impart{\Det F}.
 \label{eq:field-invariants}
\end{align}
For nonzero fields, these invariants give the standard classification:
\begin{align}
 b=0&:\quad\text{electric-like when \(a\) is positive; some frame has no magnetic field},\\
 b=0&:\quad\text{magnetic-like when \(a\) is negative; some frame has no electric field},\\
 b\ne0&:\quad\text{no frame can eliminate either field.}
\end{align}
When both $a$ and $b$ vanish, the field is null or radiation-like: the
electric and magnetic fields are orthogonal and have equal magnitude.
Thus the invariants reveal physical properties that cannot be inferred from
one observer's values of $\bE$ and $\bBf$ alone.  Unlike the energy density
and flux below, they do not change with the observer.

\subsection{Lorentz force from the field and action}

The Lorentz proper-force equation is
\begin{equation}
 m\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}=q\repart{FU}
 =\frac q2\bigl(FU+U\rev F\bigr).
 \label{eq:lorentz}
\end{equation}
The Hermitian extraction removes the imaginary magnetic-dual components.
The remaining scalar and vector components are, respectively,
\begin{gather}
 \frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}=q\bv\cdot\bE\\
 \frac{\dd\bP}{\dd t}=q(\bE+\bv\times\bBf).
\end{gather}

The same result follows from the potential through the interacting-particle
action
\begin{align}
 \int L\dd t
 &=-m\int\dd s-q\int\scpart{\Phi^* U}\dd s\\
 &=\int\left(-\frac m\gamma
 -q\scpart{\Phi^* U/\gamma}\right)\dd t\\
 &=\int\left(-\frac m\gamma
 -q\scpart{(V-\bA\cdot\bsig)(1+\bv\cdot\bsig)}\right)\dd t\\
 &=\int\left(-\frac m\gamma-qV+q\bv\cdot\bA\right)\dd t.
\end{align}
Thus
\begin{gather}
 L=-\frac m\gamma-q(V-\bv\cdot\bA)\\
 \partial_{\bv}L=m\gamma\bv+q\bA=\bP+q\bA.
\end{gather}
The Euler--Lagrange terms are
\begin{align}
 \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\partial_{\bv}L
 &=\frac{\dd\bP}{\dd t}+q\partial_t\bA
   +q(\bv\cdot\pdr)\bA,\\
 \pdr L&=-q\pdr V+q\pdr(\bv\cdot\bA).
\end{align}
Using
$(\bv\cdot\pdr)\bA=\pdr(\bv\cdot\bA)
-\bv\times(\pdr\times\bA)$ gives
\begin{align}
 \frac{\dd\bP}{\dd t}
 &=-q\pdr V-q\partial_t\bA
   +q\bigl(\pdr(\bv\cdot\bA)-(\bv\cdot\pdr)\bA\bigr)\\
 &=q\bigl(-\pdr V-\partial_t\bA
          +\bv\times(\pdr\times\bA)\bigr)\\
 &=q(\bE+\bv\times\bBf).
\end{align}
The Hamiltonian follows with the gauge-dependent terms cancelling:
\begin{align}
 H&=\bv\cdot\partial_{\bv}L-L\\
  &=m\gamma\bv^2+q\bv\cdot\bA
    +\frac m\gamma+qV-q\bv\cdot\bA\\
  &=m\gamma(\bv^2+\gamma^{-2})+qV=m\gamma+qV.
\end{align}
Finally, $\dd H/\dd t=-\partial_tL$ gives
\begin{align}
 \frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}+q\partial_tV
 +q\bv\cdot\pdr V
 &=q\partial_tV-q\bv\cdot\partial_t\bA,\\
 \frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}
 &=q\bv\cdot(-\pdr V-\partial_t\bA)
 =q\bv\cdot\bE.
\end{align}

To see equation~\eqref{eq:lorentz} componentwise, expand
\begin{equation}
 \frac{FU}{\gamma}=
 \bv\cdot\bE+\ii\bv\cdot\bBf
 +(\bE+\bv\times\bBf)\cdot\bsig
 +\ii(\bBf+\bE\times\bv)\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Taking the real paravector part removes the two imaginary components.

\subsection{Energy balance}

Since
\begin{gather}
 F=(\bE+\bBf\ii)\cdot\bsig\\
 F^{\mathsf H}=(\bE-\bBf\ii)\cdot\bsig,
\end{gather}
their ordered product is
\begin{equation}
 FF^{\mathsf H}
 =\bE^2+\bBf^2+2(\bE\times\bBf)\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Therefore the standard electromagnetic energy density $u$ and Poynting
vector $\bS$ are the scalar and vector coefficients of
$\tfrac12FF^{\mathsf H}$:
\begin{align}
 u
 &:=\tfrac12(\bE^2+\bBf^2)
 =\tfrac12\scpart{FF^{\mathsf H}},\\
 \bS
 &:=\bE\times\bBf
 =\tfrac12\vecpart{FF^{\mathsf H}}.
\end{align}
These quantities describe energy measured in the chosen frame; they are not
the two Lorentz invariants contained in $F^2$.  The product
$\tfrac12FF^{\mathsf H}$ packages energy density and energy flux into a
paravector, but is not by itself the full electromagnetic stress--energy
tensor.
This product order gives the conventional sign of the Poynting vector
directly.  For comparison, reversing the order preserves the scalar
coefficient and changes the sign of the vector coefficient:
\begin{gather}
 \scpart{F^{\mathsf H}F}=\scpart{FF^{\mathsf H}}\\
 \vecpart{F^{\mathsf H}F}=-\vecpart{FF^{\mathsf H}}.
\end{gather}
Starting only from the paravector field equation $\partial F=D^*$, its
real-vector and imaginary-vector components give
\begin{gather}
 \partial_t\bE=\pdr\times\bBf-\bJ\\
 \partial_t\bBf=-\pdr\times\bE.
\end{gather}
Taking their dot products with $\bE$ and $\bBf$, respectively, yields
\begin{align}
 \partial_tu
 &=\bE\cdot\partial_t\bE+\bBf\cdot\partial_t\bBf\\
 &=\bE\cdot(\pdr\times\bBf)-\bE\cdot\bJ
   -\bBf\cdot(\pdr\times\bE)\\
 &=-\pdr\cdot\bS-\bJ\cdot\bE,
\end{align}
where
$\pdr\cdot(\bE\times\bBf)
=\bBf\cdot(\pdr\times\bE)-\bE\cdot(\pdr\times\bBf)$.
Substitution into $\partial_tu$ therefore derives Poynting's theorem, the
local electromagnetic energy-balance law:
\begin{equation}
 \partial_tu
 +\pdr\cdot\bS=-\bJ\cdot\bE.
 \label{eq:poynting}
\end{equation}
Here $\bJ\cdot\bE$ is the power density transferred from the field to
matter; the minus sign is the corresponding loss of field energy.
Equivalently, in paravector coefficients,
\begin{equation}
 \tfrac12\partial_t\scpart{FF^{\mathsf H}}
 +\tfrac12\pdr\cdot\vecpart{FF^{\mathsf H}}
 =-\bJ\cdot\bE.
\end{equation}

\section{Relativistic wave equations}
\label{sec:waves}

\subsection{Paravector inner products and expectations}
\label{sec:paravector-inner-products}

For paravector amplitudes $\psi,\psi'$ and a paravector $Z$, define the
inner product and matrix element intrinsically by scalar extraction:
\begin{align}
 \langle\psi'\mid\psi\rangle
 &:=\scpart{\psi'^{\mathsf H}\psi},
 \label{eq:paravector-inner-product}\\
 \langle\psi'\mid Z\mid\psi\rangle
 &:=\scpart{\psi'^{\mathsf H}Z\psi}.
 \label{eq:paravector-matrix-element}
\end{align}
The first is the $Z=1$ case of the second, and
$\langle\psi\mid\psi\rangle=\lVert\psi\rVert^2$.  Thus normalization is
$\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}=1$, not $\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi=1$.
For an observable $Z^{\mathsf H}=Z$ acting from the left,
\begin{equation}
 \langle Z\rangle_\psi
 :=\frac{\langle\psi\mid Z\mid\psi\rangle}
 {\langle\psi\mid\psi\rangle}
 =\frac{\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}Z\psi}}
 {\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}}.
 \label{eq:paravector-expectation}
\end{equation}
For normalized $\psi$, this reduces to
$\langle Z\rangle_\psi=\langle\psi\mid Z\mid\psi\rangle$.
Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-spinors} shows that these intrinsic definitions
become the usual trace pairing and, after minimal-ideal projection, the
standard two-component column-spinor formulas.

For a paravector $T$ with $\psi'=T\psi$,
\begin{equation}
 \langle Z\rangle_{\psi'}
 =\frac{\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}T^{\mathsf H}ZT\psi}}
 {\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}T^{\mathsf H}T\psi}}.
 \label{eq:bilinear-congruence}
\end{equation}
If $T=U$, $U^{\mathsf H}U=1$, and
$\langle\psi\mid\psi\rangle=1$, then the denominator is unity and
\begin{equation}
 \langle Z\rangle_{\psi'}
 =\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}U^{\mathsf H}ZU\psi}.
\end{equation}
Thus a unitary rotation preserves the normalization.  A finite boost is not
unitary, so this positive equal-time norm is not itself Lorentz invariant;
the covariant Dirac normalization instead comes from the conserved Dirac
current on a spacelike hypersurface.

\subsection{Physical spinors and minimal ideals}
\label{sec:physical-spinors}

A physical two-component Pauli spinor is represented intrinsically by a
minimal ideal obtained by left projection in the paravector algebra.  Choose a fixed unit direction
$\bu$ and use the projectors from Section~\ref{sec:algebra-projectors},
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_\pm:=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
They satisfy
\begin{align}
 \Pi_\pm^2&=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_+\Pi_-&=0,\\
 \Pi_++\Pi_-&=1,\\
 \Pi_\pm^{\mathsf H}&=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_\pm^*&=\Pi_\mp,\\
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)\Pi_\pm&=\pm \Pi_\pm,
 \end{align}
and hence $\bu\cdot\bsig=\Pi_+-\Pi_-$.

Project an unrestricted paravector amplitude from the left into either
sector:
\begin{equation}
 \psi'_\pm:=\Pi_\pm\psi.
\end{equation}
Then $\Pi_\pm\psi'_\pm=\psi'_\pm$ and
$\Pi_\mp\psi'_\pm=0$.  Hermitian conjugation reverses the multiplied order,
so the projected norm and observable matrix element are
\begin{align}
 \langle\psi'_\pm\mid\psi'_\pm\rangle
 &=\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm\psi}
 =\langle\psi\mid\Pi_\pm\mid\psi\rangle,\\
 \langle\psi'_\pm\mid Z\mid\psi'_\pm\rangle
 &=\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm^{\mathsf H}Z\Pi_\pm\psi}
 =\langle\psi\mid\Pi_\pm Z\Pi_\pm\mid\psi\rangle.
\end{align}
Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-spinors} develops the faithful matrix image of this
ideal and shows how it becomes one conventional two-component Pauli column.
Applied to both paravector components of the Dirac block below, the same
left projections select the conventional four-component Dirac sectors.

\subsection{Spin carried by paravector components}
\label{sec:paravector-spin-content}

Spin is determined by how an amplitude transforms under rotations, not merely
by how many coefficients are used to write it.  Let $R$ be the rotation rotor
defined in Section~\ref{sec:transformations}.  The scalar, minimal-ideal, and
vector carriers transform differently.

A complex scalar amplitude has no spatial direction and is unchanged by the
rotor:
\begin{equation}
 \psi_0'= \psi_0.
\end{equation}
It therefore carries spin zero.  The Klein--Gordon field below is of this
type; its single complex coefficient is proportional to the algebra identity.

A projected minimal-ideal amplitude contains two complex coefficients.  Its
associated Pauli column transforms by one-sided rotor multiplication, which
is represented intrinsically by
\begin{equation}
 \psi_{1/2}'=R\psi_{1/2}.
\end{equation}
Because $R$ contains the half-angle, a full $2\pi$ rotation gives
\begin{equation}
 \psi_{1/2}'=-\psi_{1/2}.
\end{equation}
This is the spin-one-half carrier.  A Dirac amplitude consists of two such
carriers coupled by the mass term; the pair still describes spin one-half,
not spin zero plus spin one.

A pure vector amplitude $\bV\cdot\bsig$ transforms by the two-sided sandwich
\begin{equation}
 \bV'\cdot\bsig
 =R^{\mathsf H}(\bV\cdot\bsig)R.
\end{equation}
The two rotor signs cancel under a $2\pi$ rotation, so this three-component
carrier returns to itself and carries spin one.  The electromagnetic field
$F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$ is a complex pure vector of this type.  Maxwell's
equations and transversality reduce a free massless electromagnetic wave to
its two physical helicities; the three algebraic vector coefficients should
not be confused with three independent photon polarizations.

Consequently, an unrestricted paravector
$Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$ is reducible under rotations: its scalar component carries
spin zero and its vector component carries spin one.  Spin one-half does not
arise by selecting some of those four paravector coefficients.  It arises
from the minimal-ideal projection and its one-sided transformation law.

\subsection{Klein--Gordon equation}

For a free particle, the mass-shell relation is
$E^2-\bP^2=m^2$.  On a plane wave with phase
$\exp(-\ii Et+\ii\bP\cdot\br)$, the operators $\ii\partial_t$ and
$-\ii\pdr$ return the energy and momentum.  The corresponding substitutions
for a scalar amplitude $\psi$ are therefore
\begin{gather}
 E=\ii\partial_t\\
 \bP=-\ii\pdr.
\end{gather}
Applying the mass-shell polynomial to $\psi$ gives
\begin{align*}
 0&=(E^2-\bP^2-m^2)\psi\\
  &=\left((\ii\partial_t)^2-(-\ii\pdr)^2-m^2\right)\psi\\
  &=\left(-\partial_t^2+\pdr^2-m^2\right)\psi.
\end{align*}
Multiplying by $-1$ and defining
$\Box:=\partial_t^2-\pdr^2$ gives the Klein--Gordon equation
\begin{equation}
 (\Box+m^2)\psi=0.
 \label{eq:kg}
\end{equation}

In an electromagnetic potential, minimal coupling replaces the free
operators by
\begin{gather}
 E=\ii\partial_t-qV\\
 \bP=-\ii\pdr-q\bA,
\end{gather}
and the Klein--Gordon equation takes the covariant form
\begin{equation}
 (-E^2+\bP^2+m^2)\psi=0.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Spin-zero low-energy limit}

Denote the relativistic solution of the preceding equation by $\psi'$ and
separate its rest-energy phase by
$\psi'=\exp(-\ii mt)\psi$.  The exact equation for the slowly varying
amplitude becomes
\begin{gather}
 (-E^2-2mE+\bP^2)\psi=0\\
 E=\frac{\bP^2-E^2}{2m}.
\end{gather}
For a free particle the operators commute, and iteration gives
\begin{equation}
 E=\frac{\bP^2}{2m}-\frac{\bP^4}{8m^3}+\cdots.
\end{equation}
More generally, neglecting $E^2$ at leading nonrelativistic order in the
covariant equation gives the Schr\"odinger equation
\begin{equation}
 \ii\partial_t\psi=\left(\frac{(-\ii\pdr-q\bA)^2}{2m}+qV\right)\psi.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Dirac equation in block form}

For a paravector $Z$, define
\begin{equation}
 \Wop(Z):=
 \begin{Bmatrix}0&Z\\ \adj Z&0\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
It satisfies
\begin{gather}
 \Wop(Z)^2=
 \begin{Bmatrix}
  Z\adj Z&0\\
  0&\adj Z\,Z
 \end{Bmatrix}
 =\det(Z)\bI\\
 \Wop(Z)^*=\Wop(Z^*).
\end{gather}
For the free field, define the Dirac operator and its mass conjugate:
\begin{gather}
 \bDm:=\Wop(\ii\partial)-m\bI\\
 \bDm':=\Wop(\ii\partial)+m\bI.
\end{gather}
The Dirac equation is
\begin{equation}
 \bDm\bPsi=0.
 \label{eq:dirac}
\end{equation}
Since $\Wop(\ii\partial)^2=-\Box\bI$, multiplication by the mass conjugate
gives
\begin{equation}
 \bDm'\bDm
 =\Wop(\ii\partial)^2-m^2\bI
 =-(\Box+m^2)\bI.
\end{equation}
Consequently, $\bDm\bPsi=0$ implies
$(\Box+m^2)\bPsi=0$, so every component of the Dirac block satisfies the
Klein--Gordon equation.  This factorization is the direct connection between
the first-order Dirac equation and the relativistic mass-shell equation.  For
$\bPsi=\{\Psi_1,\Psi_2\}^{\mathsf T}$ it reads
\begin{gather}
 \ii\partial\Psi_2-m\Psi_1=0\\
 \ii\sconj\partial\,\Psi_1-m\Psi_2=0.
\end{gather}
Conversely, for $m\ne0$, any scalar solution $\psi'$ of
$(\Box+m^2)\psi'=0$ gives a Dirac solution
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi=
 \left\{\psi',\frac{\ii}{m}\sconj\partial\psi'\right\}^{\mathsf T}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the second component equation holds by definition, and the first
reduces to $-(\Box+m^2)\psi'/m=0$.
More generally, for constant complex scalars $a$ and $b$, the same scalar
solution gives
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi=
 \frac1m
 \begin{Bmatrix}
  ma+\ii b\partial\\
  \ii a\sconj\partial+mb
 \end{Bmatrix}\psi'.
 \label{eq:kg-dirac-general-lift}
\end{equation}
Substitution into the two component equations leaves respectively
$-a(\Box+m^2)\psi'/m$ and $-b(\Box+m^2)\psi'/m$, so both vanish.  This is a
two-constant family generated from one Klein--Gordon solution, not a claim
that one scalar solution parametrizes every Dirac solution.
With a potential, the gauge-covariant form is
\begin{equation}
 \bigl(\Wop(\ii\partial-q\sconj\Phi)-m\bI\bigr)\bPsi=0.
 \label{eq:dirac-field}
\end{equation}
It is invariant under
$\Phi'=\Phi+\sconj\partial\lambda$ and
$\bPsi=\exp(\ii q\lambda)\bPsi'$.

\subsection{Schr\"odinger--Pauli limit}

Each relativistic Dirac component contains the rapid phase associated with
the rest energy.  For $n=1,2$, separate it from the slowly varying component
by
\begin{equation}
 \Psi_n'=\exp(-\ii mt)\Psi_n.
\end{equation}
For the covariant operators $E=\ii\partial_t-qV$ and
$\bP=-\ii\pdr-q\bA$, the exponential is spatially constant and therefore
\begin{align}
 E\Psi_n'&=\exp(-\ii mt)(E+m)\Psi_n,\\
 \bP\Psi_n'&=\exp(-\ii mt)\bP\Psi_n.
\end{align}
After cancelling the common exponential, the two Dirac equations become
\begin{align}
 (E+m-\bP\cdot\bsig)\Psi_2-m\Psi_1&=0,\\
 (E+m+\bP\cdot\bsig)\Psi_1-m\Psi_2&=0.
\end{align}
Eliminating $\Psi_1$ gives the exact second-order equation
\begin{equation}
 \left(E^2+2mE-\bP^2+q\bBf\cdot\bsig
 -\ii q\bE\cdot\bsig\right)\Psi_2=0.
\end{equation}
Under the usual low-energy, slowly varying-field power counting, the $E^2$
term and the electric-field term contribute beyond the leading
nonrelativistic approximation.  Retaining the leading terms gives
\begin{equation}
 E\Psi_2=
 \frac{\bP^2-q\bBf\cdot\bsig}{2m}\Psi_2.
\end{equation}
At this order $\Psi_1$ and $\Psi_2$ have the same leading rest-frame
amplitude.  Writing its faithful two-component column as $\bPsi$ and
substituting the covariant operators gives the Pauli equation
\begin{equation}
 \ii\partial_t\bPsi=\left(
  \frac{(-\ii\pdr-q\bA)^2}{2m}+qV
  -\frac{q}{2m}\bBf\cdot\bsig\right)\bPsi.
 \label{eq:pauli}
\end{equation}
Using the spin projectors,
$\bPsi=\bPsi_++\bPsi_-$ with $\bPsi_\pm=\Pi_\pm\bPsi$.  For a uniform static
magnetic field, $\bu=\bBf/\lVert\bBf\rVert$, the sectors decouple:
\begin{equation}
 \ii\partial_t\bPsi_\pm=\left(
 \frac{\bP^2\mp q\lVert\bBf\rVert}{2m}+qV\right)\bPsi_\pm.
\end{equation}
For a varying field direction the sectors are coupled.  With $H$ denoting the
Pauli Hamiltonian, direct differentiation gives
\begin{align}
 \ii\partial_t\bPsi_\pm
 &=\ii(\partial_t\Pi_\pm)\bPsi+\Pi_\pm H\bPsi\\
 &=H\bPsi_\pm+\left(\ii\partial_t\Pi_\pm
   +[\Pi_\pm,H]\right)\bPsi.
\end{align}
Because the field term commutes with its own instantaneous projectors, this is
\begin{gather}
 \ii\partial_t\bPsi_\pm=H\bPsi_\pm+\bm{S}_\pm\\
 \bm{S}_\pm=\left(\ii\partial_t\Pi_\pm
 +\frac{[\bP^2,\Pi_\pm]}{2m}\right)\bPsi.
\end{gather}

\subsection{Interacting operators and factorization}

The covariant energy and momentum operators do not commute.  Direct
application to a test wavefunction gives
\begin{gather}
 [E,P_m]=\ii qE_m\\
 [P_m,P_n]=\ii q(\partial_mA_n-\partial_nA_m)\\
 \bP\times\bP=\ii q\bBf.
\end{gather}
Therefore
\begin{align}
 (E+\bsig\cdot\bP)(E-\bsig\cdot\bP)&=E^2-\bP^2-\ii qF,\\
 (E-\bsig\cdot\bP)(E+\bsig\cdot\bP)&=E^2-\bP^2-\ii qF^*,\\
 E+\bsig\cdot\bP&=\ii\partial^*-q\Phi,\\
 E-\bsig\cdot\bP&=\ii\partial-q\Phi^*.
\end{align}
Expanding the minimally coupled Klein--Gordon operator gives the fully explicit
result
\begin{equation}
 \left(\Box+\ii qS-q^2\Det\Phi+m^2
 +2\ii q(V\partial_t+\bA\cdot\pdr)\right)\psi=0.
\end{equation}
The two orderings satisfy
\begin{align}
 0&=\left((\partial+\ii q\Phi^*)
          (\partial^*+\ii q\Phi)+m^2\right)\Psi_1
   =(-E^2+\bP^2+m^2+\ii qF^*)\Psi_1,\\
 0&=\left((\partial^*+\ii q\Phi)
          (\partial+\ii q\Phi^*)+m^2\right)\Psi_2
   =(-E^2+\bP^2+m^2+\ii qF)\Psi_2.
\end{align}
These follow from the same mass-conjugate factorization used for the free
Dirac equation.  With the potential included, the two block operators become
\begin{align}
 \bDm&:=\Wop(\ii\partial-q\Phi^*)-m\bI,\\
 \bDm'&:=\Wop(\ii\partial-q\Phi^*)+m\bI.
\end{align}
Then
\begin{equation}
 \bDm'\bDm=
 -\begin{Bmatrix}
  -E^2+\bP^2+m^2+\ii qF^*&0\\
  0&-E^2+\bP^2+m^2+\ii qF
 \end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The electromagnetic field therefore prevents the interacting product from
being proportional to the block identity: its two components acquire the
distinct spin couplings $\ii qF^*$ and $\ii qF$.  In the free limit
$q=0$, both couplings vanish and $-E^2+\bP^2=\Box$, so
\begin{equation}
 \bDm'\bDm=-(\Box+m^2)\bI_2.
\end{equation}
Thus the free Dirac equation $\bDm\bPsi=0$ recovers the Klein--Gordon
equation on both components of the Dirac block.

\subsection{Spin probabilities and energy sectors}

The measurement projectors act from the left, so the projected amplitudes are
$\psi'_\pm=\Pi_\pm\psi$ as defined above.  They are orthogonal and complete:
\begin{gather}
 \psi=\psi'_++\psi'_-\\
 \langle\psi'_+\mid\psi'_-\rangle=0\\
 \langle\psi'_-\mid\psi'_+\rangle=0.
\end{gather}
Define the real Bloch vector of a nonzero amplitude by its intrinsic
expectation values,
\begin{gather}
 s_n:=\frac{\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\sigma_n\psi}}
 {\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}}\\
 \bm{s}:=\{s_1,s_2,s_3\}\\
 \lVert\bm{s}\rVert^2\leq1.
\end{gather}
Equality holds for a pure two-component spin state.
The probabilities for spin along and against $\bu$ are
\begin{equation}
 p_\pm(\bu)
 :=\frac{\langle\psi'_\pm\mid\psi'_\pm\rangle}
 {\langle\psi\mid\psi\rangle}
 =\frac{\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm\psi}}
 {\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}}
 =\frac{\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm\psi}}
 {\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}}
 =\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bm{s}).
 \label{eq:spin-probability}
\end{equation}
Their conventional column-spinor and density-matrix forms are derived in
Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-spinors}.

For a state aligned with $\bu$ and $\bu\cdot\bu'=\cos\theta$, the measurement
probabilities along $\bu'$ are
\begin{gather}
 p_+(\bu')=\tfrac12(1+\bu\cdot\bu')=\cos^2(\theta/2)\\
 p_-(\bu')=\tfrac12(1-\bu\cdot\bu')=\sin^2(\theta/2).
\end{gather}
If $\bu'$ is obtained by rotating $\bu$ through $\phi$ about $\bn$, then
\begin{equation}
 \bu\cdot\bu'=(\bn\cdot\bu)^2
 +\left(1-(\bn\cdot\bu)^2\right)\cos\phi.
\end{equation}
Thus $\theta=\phi$ when $\bn\cdot\bu=0$.

For fluctuations $\dd\bu$ and $\dd\bu'$, the squared determinant identity is
\begin{equation}
 \Det{(\dd\bu\cdot\bsig)(\dd\bu'\cdot\bsig)}
 =(\dd\bu\cdot\dd\bu')^2
 +\frac14\left\lVert
 [\dd\bu\cdot\bsig,\dd\bu'\cdot\bsig]\right\rVert^2.
\end{equation}
Since the first term is nonnegative,
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt{\Det{(\dd\bu\cdot\bsig)(\dd\bu'\cdot\bsig)}}
 \geq\frac12\left\lVert
 [\dd\bu\cdot\bsig,\dd\bu'\cdot\bsig]\right\rVert.
\end{equation}
Finally, on the mass shell let
\begin{equation}
 U=\frac{E+\bP\cdot\bsig}{m}.
\end{equation}
Since $\Det U=1$ and $\Wop(U)^2=\bI$, the positive- and negative-energy
projectors are
\begin{gather}
 \tfrac12(\bI+\Wop(U))\\
 \tfrac12(\bI-\Wop(U)).
\end{gather}
Each squares to itself, their product is zero, and their sum is $\bI$.
The spin projectors act with the same sign on both components of $\bPsi$, so
no additional block notation is needed: their actions are simply
$\Pi_+\bPsi$ and $\Pi_-\bPsi$.  When $\Wop(U)$ commutes with $\Pi_\pm$, as
for a compatible spin quantization axis, the four pairwise products are
joint energy--spin projectors.  They resolve the physical Dirac column selected in
Section~\ref{sec:physical-spinors} as
\begin{align}
 \bPsi={}&\tfrac12(\bI+\Wop(U))
  \Pi_+\bPsi
 +\tfrac12(\bI+\Wop(U))
  \Pi_-\bPsi\\
 &+\tfrac12(\bI-\Wop(U))
  \Pi_+\bPsi
 +\tfrac12(\bI-\Wop(U))
  \Pi_-\bPsi.
\end{align}
Without this commutation condition the displayed sum remains a sequential
decomposition, but the factors are not joint orthogonal projectors.

\section{Rotations and Lorentz boosts}
\label{sec:transformations}

Throughout this section, the transformation factor $T$ is defined from a
positive generator.  Thus $T=R$ for rotations and $T=L$ for boosts.  The
opposite action is written with $T^{-1}$; it is not introduced by redefining
$T$ with a negative exponential.

\subsection{Rotors}

A rotation through angle $\phi$ about unit axis $\bu$ is generated by
\begin{gather}
 R:=\exp\!\left(\ii\frac\phi2\,\bu\cdot\bsig\right)
  =\cos\frac\phi2+\bu\cdot\ii\bsig\,\sin\frac\phi2\\
 R^{-1}=\rev R
 =\cos\frac\phi2-\bu\cdot\ii\bsig\,\sin\frac\phi2.
\end{gather}
The sandwich action $X'=\rev R X R$ fixes the scalar component and rotates
the spatial vector according to Rodrigues' formula,
\begin{equation}
 \br'=\br\cos\phi+(\bu\times\br)\sin\phi
       +(\bu\cdot\br)(1-\cos\phi)\bu.
\end{equation}
The half-angle explains the spinorial sign change $R(\phi+2\pi)=-R(\phi)$.
The corresponding column-spinor and density-matrix transformations are
collected in Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-spinors}.
For a spinor $\psi_R:=R\psi$, expectation values transform as
\begin{gather}
 \psi_R^{\mathsf H}=\psi^{\mathsf H}R^{\mathsf H}\\
 \langle Z\rangle_{\psi_R}
 =\frac{\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}R^{\mathsf H}ZR\psi}}
 {\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}}\\
 R^{\mathsf H}=R^{-1}.
\end{gather}
For $Z=\bu\cdot\bsig$, this gives
$Z'=R^{\mathsf H}ZR=\bu'\cdot\bsig$.

\subsection{Boosts}
\label{sec:boosts}

Let rapidity $\theta$ satisfy $\beta=\tanh\theta$ and
$\gamma=\cosh\theta$.  A boost along $\bu$ is
\begin{gather}
 L:=\exp\!\left(\frac\theta2\,\bu\cdot\bsig\right)
 =\cosh\frac\theta2+\bu\cdot\bsig\,\sinh\frac\theta2\\
 L^{\mathsf H}=L\\
 L^{-1}=L^*
 =\cosh\frac\theta2-\bu\cdot\bsig\,\sinh\frac\theta2.
\end{gather}
For $\psi_L:=L^{-1}\psi$, the inverse boost factor is Hermitian, so
\begin{gather}
 \psi_L^{\mathsf H}=\psi^{\mathsf H}L^{-1}\\
 \langle Z\rangle_{\psi_L}
 =\frac{\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}L^{-1}ZL^{-1}\psi}}
 {\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}L^{-2}\psi}}.
\end{gather}
The similarity $L^{-1}ZL$ is a different operation: it preserves
$\scpart Z$ and is not the spacetime or spinor-bilinear boost used here.
The spacetime paravector therefore transforms by the Hermitian congruence
$X'=L^{-1}XL^{-1}$, which yields
the components
$\br_{\parallel}=(\br\cdot\bu)\bu$, $\br_{\perp}=\br-\br_{\parallel}$,
$\br'_{\parallel}=(\br'\cdot\bu)\bu$, and
$\br'_{\perp}=\br'-\br'_{\parallel}$:
\begin{align}
 t'&=\gamma(t-\beta\bu\cdot\br),\\
 \br'_{\parallel}&=\gamma(\br_{\parallel}-\beta t\bu),\\
 \br'_{\perp}&=\br_{\perp}.
\end{align}
It preserves $\Det X=t^2-\br^2$.  The electromagnetic field instead obeys
$F'=L^{-1}FL$ for $F=(\bE+\bBf\ii)\cdot\bsig$.  Define
$\bE_{\parallel}=(\bE\cdot\bu)\bu$,
$\bE_{\perp}=\bE-\bE_{\parallel}$ and likewise
$\bE'_{\parallel}=(\bE'\cdot\bu)\bu$,
$\bE'_{\perp}=\bE'-\bE'_{\parallel}$; define the four corresponding
components of $\bBf$ and $\bBf'$ by the same formulas.  Then
\begin{align}
 \bE'_{\parallel}&=\bE_{\parallel},\\
 \bE'_{\perp}&=\gamma(\bE_{\perp}+\beta\bu\times\bBf),\\
 \bBf'_{\parallel}&=\bBf_{\parallel},\\
 \bBf'_{\perp}&=\gamma(\bBf_{\perp}-\beta\bu\times\bE).
\end{align}
With the same convention,
\begin{gather}
 \partial'=L^{-1}\partial L\\
 D'^*=L^{-1}D^*L.
\end{gather}
Since $L$ is constant and $LL^{-1}=1$, the field equation is covariant:
\begin{equation}
 \partial'F'=L^{-1}(\partial F)L=L^{-1}D^*L=D'^*.
\end{equation}

Collinear boosts add rapidities and therefore obey
\begin{gather}
 \beta' =\frac{\beta_1+\beta_2}{1+\beta_1\beta_2}\\
 \gamma'=\gamma_1\gamma_2(1+\beta_1\beta_2).
\end{gather}
Inversion changes product order:
\begin{gather}
 (LR)^{-1}=R^{\mathsf H}L^*\\
 (RL)^{-1}=L^* R^{\mathsf H}\\
 (R_1R_2)^{-1}=R_2^{\mathsf H}R_1^{\mathsf H}\\
 (L_1L_2)^{-1}=L_2^* L_1^*.
\end{gather}

\section{Generalized spacetime and metric form}
\label{sec:generalized}

Let
\begin{gather}
 X=f(t,\br)+\bg(t,\br)\cdot\bsig\\
 \dd X=\dd f+\dd\bg\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
Writing $\dd X=A\dd t+\bB\cdot\dd\br$ gives
\begin{gather}
 A=\partial_tf+\partial_t\bg\cdot\bsig\\
 \bB=\pdr f+\pdr(\bg\cdot\bsig),
\end{gather}
and the interval remains
\begin{equation}
 \dd s^2=\dd X\,\dd X^*=\dd f^2-\dd\bg^2.
\end{equation}
For a future-directed time-like curve with $\dd f>0$,
\begin{gather}
 \dd s=\sqrt{1-\bv^2}\,\dd f\\
 \bv^2<1.
\end{gather}
Define the geometric velocity and Lorentz factor by
\begin{gather}
 \bv=\frac{\dd\bg}{\dd f}\\
 \gamma=\frac{\dd f}{\dd s}=\frac1{\sqrt{1-\bv^2}}\\
 U=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig).
\end{gather}
With geometric acceleration
$\ba=\dd\bv/\dd s=\gamma\dd^2\bg/\dd f^2$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd U}{\dd s}
 =\gamma^3(\bv\cdot\ba)
 +\left(\gamma\ba+\gamma^3(\bv\cdot\ba)\bv\right)\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
For inertial motion, $\dd U/\dd s=0$.  Its scalar component gives
$\bv\cdot\ba=0$, and its vector component then gives $\ba=0$.  Hence
$\dd^2\bg/\dd f^2=0$, so $\bg=c_1f+c_2$ and the geometric velocity is
constant.
If $f'=\dd f/\dd t$ and $\bg'=\dd\bg/\dd t$, then
\begin{gather}
 \frac{\dd\bv}{\dd t}
 =\frac{\bg''f'-\bg'f''}{(f')^2}\\
 [U,\dd U/\dd s]=2\gamma^3(\bv\times\ba)\cdot\ii\bsig.
\end{gather}

\section{Pauli-matrix representation}
\label{sec:matrix}

\subsection{Faithful representation}

Every complex element of the displayed algebra basis can be collected as
\begin{equation}
 Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Here $S\in\C$ and $\bV\in\C^3$.
Such an element is called a complex paravector.  Its faithful $2\times2$
complex representation begins with
\[
 \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_1)=\begin{Bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{Bmatrix},\quad
 \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_2)=\begin{Bmatrix}0&-\ii\\\ii&0\end{Bmatrix},\quad
 \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_3)=\begin{Bmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{Bmatrix}.
\]
Thus
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(S+\bV\cdot\bsig)=
 \begin{Bmatrix}
  S+V_3&V_1-\ii V_2\\
  V_1+\ii V_2&S-V_3
 \end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:matrix}
\end{equation}
It preserves identity, addition, and multiplied order:
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}(1)&=\bI_2,\\
 \operatorname{mat}(Z'Z)
 &=\operatorname{mat}(Z')\operatorname{mat}(Z).
 \label{eq:matrix-product}
\end{align}

\subsection{Matrix operations and induced algebra operations}

Let $\bM=\operatorname{mat}(Z)$ with entries
$m_{jk}=a_{jk}+b_{jk}\ii$, where $a_{jk},b_{jk}\in\R$.  Define transpose by
\begin{equation}
 (\bM^{\mathsf T})_{jk}:=m_{kj}.
\end{equation}
Define entrywise complex conjugation independently by
\begin{gather}
 m_{jk}^{\mathsf C}:=a_{jk}-b_{jk}\ii\\
 \bM^{\mathsf C}:=
 \begin{Bmatrix}
  m_{11}^{\mathsf C}&m_{12}^{\mathsf C}\\
  m_{21}^{\mathsf C}&m_{22}^{\mathsf C}
 \end{Bmatrix}.
\end{gather}
The Hermitian transpose is then
\begin{equation}
 \bM^{\mathsf H}:=(\bM^{\mathsf C})^{\mathsf T}
 = (\bM^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf C}.
 \label{eq:matrix-operations}
\end{equation}
Define the algebra operation $^{\mathsf H}$ through the faithful
representation by
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(Z^{\mathsf H}):=\bM^{\mathsf H}.
\end{equation}
For $n=1,2,3$, its action on every basis type is
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}(1)^{\mathsf H}&=\bI_2,\\
 (\ii\bI_2)^{\mathsf H}&=-\ii\bI_2,\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}
 &=\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n),\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\ii\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}
 &=-\operatorname{mat}(\ii\sigma_n).
\end{align}
Hence $1^{\mathsf H}=1$, $\ii^{\mathsf H}=-\ii$,
$\sigma_n^{\mathsf H}=\sigma_n$, and
$(\ii\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}=-\ii\sigma_n$.
Hermitian conjugation changes matrix-product order.  Therefore faithfulness
gives
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}((\sigma_n\sigma_m)^{\mathsf H})
 &=\left(\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n)
          \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_m)\right)^{\mathsf H}\\
 &=\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_m)^{\mathsf H}
   \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}
 =\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_m\sigma_n),
\end{align}
and hence
\begin{equation}
 (\sigma_n\sigma_m)^{\mathsf H}
 =\sigma_m^{\mathsf H}\sigma_n^{\mathsf H}
 =\sigma_m\sigma_n.
 \label{eq:hermitian-product-order}
\end{equation}
Thus $^{\mathsf H}$ is precisely the Hermitian operator defined in
Section~\ref{sec:conjugate-operators}.
To define the distinct sigma conjugation, let
$\bm{S}:=\begin{Bmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{Bmatrix}$ and
$\bm{S}^2=-\bI_2$, so that $\bm S^{-1}=-\bm S$, and set
\begin{align}
 \bM^*&:=\bm{S}^{-1}\bM^{\mathsf C}\bm{S},\\
 \operatorname{mat}(Z^*)&:=\bM^*.
 \label{eq:matrix-sigma-conjugation}
\end{align}
For $n=1,2,3$, this gives the required action on every basis type:
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}(1)^*
 &=\bm S^{-1}\bI_2^{\mathsf C}\bm S=\bI_2,\\
 (\ii\bI_2)^*
 &=\bm S^{-1}(\ii\bI_2)^{\mathsf C}\bm S=-\ii\bI_2,\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n)^*
 &=\bm S^{-1}\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n)^{\mathsf C}\bm S
 =-\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n),\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\ii\sigma_n)^*
 &=\bm S^{-1}\operatorname{mat}(\ii\sigma_n)^{\mathsf C}\bm S
 =\operatorname{mat}(\ii\sigma_n).
\end{align}
The operation also preserves multiplied order.  Since entrywise conjugation
preserves order,
\begin{align}
 (\bM\bN)^*
 &=\bm S^{-1}\bM^{\mathsf C}\bN^{\mathsf C}\bm S\\
 &=(\bm S^{-1}\bM^{\mathsf C}\bm S)
   (\bm S^{-1}\bN^{\mathsf C}\bm S)
 =\bM^*\bN^*.
\end{align}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
 (\bM\bN)^*=\bM^*\bN^*.
 \label{eq:matrix-star-product}
\end{equation}
The four operations must remain distinct:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c l l}
 \toprule
 symbol & matrix operation & relation to the algebra \\
 \midrule
 $\bM^{\mathsf T}$ & transpose & changes matrix-product order \\
 $\bM^{\mathsf C}$ & entrywise complex conjugation & sends $\ii$ to $-\ii$ \\
 $\bM^{\mathsf H}$ & Hermitian transpose
   & $\operatorname{mat}(Z^{\mathsf H})$ \\
 $\bM^*$ & sigma conjugation
   & $\operatorname{mat}(Z^*)$ \\
 \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Their product-order rules are
\begin{align}
 (\bM\bN)^{\mathsf T}&=\bN^{\mathsf T}\bM^{\mathsf T},\\
 (\bM\bN)^{\mathsf C}&=\bM^{\mathsf C}\bN^{\mathsf C},\\
 (\bM\bN)^{\mathsf H}&=\bN^{\mathsf H}\bM^{\mathsf H}.
\end{align}
Sigma conjugation preserves order by equation~\eqref{eq:matrix-star-product}.
Thus the induced algebra operations satisfy
\begin{align}
 (Z^{\mathsf H})^{\mathsf H}&=Z,\\
 (ZZ')^{\mathsf H}&=Z'^{\mathsf H}Z^{\mathsf H},\\
 (Z^*)^*&=Z,\\
 (ZZ')^*&=Z^*Z'^*,\\
 Z^{*\mathsf H}&=Z^{\mathsf H*}.
 \label{eq:algebra-operator-laws}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Trace, determinant, and adjugate.}
For $\bM=\operatorname{mat}(Z)$, define the matrix trace and determinant by
\begin{gather}
 \operatorname{tr}(\bM):=m_{11}+m_{22}\\
 \det(\bM):=m_{11}m_{22}-m_{12}m_{21}.
\end{gather}
The corresponding paravector operations are
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{tr}(Z)&:=\operatorname{tr}(\bM)=2S,
 \label{eq:paravector-tr-det}
 \\
 \det(Z)&:=\det(\bM).
 \label{eq:paravector-matrix-det}
\end{align}
Faithfulness transfers the matrix identities to the algebra:
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{tr}(Z+Z')&=\operatorname{tr}(Z)+\operatorname{tr}(Z'),\\
 \operatorname{tr}(cZ)&=c\operatorname{tr}(Z),\\
 \operatorname{tr}(ZZ')&=\operatorname{tr}(Z'Z),\\
 \det(ZZ')&=\det(Z)\det(Z'),\\
 \det(cZ)&=c^2\det(Z).
\label{eq:trace-det-properties}
\end{align}
The last identity holds for $c\in\C$.
For a $2\times2$ matrix and its paravector preimage, define
\begin{align}
 \adj{\bM}&:=\operatorname{tr}(\bM)\bI_2-\bM,\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\adj Z)&:=\adj{\bM}.
\end{align}
Direct comparison with the induced algebra operators gives
\begin{align}
 \adj Z&=Z^{*\mathsf H}
 \label{eq:matrix-adjugate}\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\adj Z)=\adj{\bM}=(\bM^*)^{\mathsf H}.
\end{align}
Equivalently,
\begin{gather}
 \adj Z=\operatorname{tr}(Z)-Z\\
 \operatorname{tr}(Z)=Z+\adj Z=2S.
\end{gather}
The adjugate changes product order and obeys
\begin{align}
 \adj{ZZ'}&=\adj{Z'}\adj Z,\\
 \adj{\adj Z}&=Z,\\
 \adj Z\,Z&=Z\adj Z=\det(Z),\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\adj Z\,Z)&=\det(\bM)\bI_2.
 \label{eq:matrix-adj-det}
\end{align}
Consequently, when $\det(Z)\ne0$,
\begin{equation}
 Z^{-1}=\frac{\adj Z}{\det(Z)}.
\end{equation}
Finally, Cayley--Hamilton gives
\begin{align}
 \bM^2-\operatorname{tr}(\bM)\bM+\det(\bM)\bI_2&=0,\\
 Z^2-\operatorname{tr}(Z)Z+\det(Z)&=0.
 \label{eq:cayley-hamilton}
\end{align}

\subsection{Algebra--matrix crosswalk}

For $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, define
\begin{gather}
 \scpart Z:=S\\
 \vecpart Z:=\bV.
\end{gather}
Thus $\vecpart Z$ is the coefficient vector, while its algebra element is
$\bV\cdot\bsig$.

For $\bM=\operatorname{mat}(Z)$, the extraction formulas are
\begin{align}
 \scpart Z&=S=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bM),\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\scpart Z)&=S\bI_2,\\
 (\vecpart Z)_n&=V_n=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\sigma}_n\bM),\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\vecpart Z\cdot\bsig)
 &=\bM-\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bM)\bI_2.
 \label{eq:matrix-sc-vec}
\end{align}

Define real and imaginary paravector extraction through Hermitian splitting:
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}(\repart Z)&:=\tfrac12(\bM+\bM^{\mathsf H}),\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\impart Z)&:=\frac{\bM-\bM^{\mathsf H}}{2\ii}.
 \label{eq:matrix-re-im}
\end{align}
By construction, the algebra and matrix operators agree through
\begin{gather}
 \operatorname{mat}(Z^{\mathsf H})=\bM^{\mathsf H}\\
 \operatorname{mat}(Z^*)=\bM^*\\
 \operatorname{mat}(Z^{*\mathsf H})=(\bM^*)^{\mathsf H}.
\end{gather}
The matrix-only operations $^{\mathsf C}$ and $^{\mathsf T}$ are related to
the algebra operations by
\begin{align}
 \bM^{\mathsf C}&=\bm S\bM^*\bm S^{-1}
 \label{eq:matrix-c-t-crosswalk}
 \\
 \bM^{\mathsf T}&=\bm S\operatorname{mat}(Z^{*\mathsf H})\bm S^{-1}.
\end{align}

Finally, scalar extraction of a product and the paravector norm
are
\begin{align}
 \scpart{Z'Z}&=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bM_{Z'}\bM_Z),\\
 \lVert Z\rVert^2&:=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bM^{\mathsf H}\bM)
 =\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bM\bM^{\mathsf H}).
 \label{eq:matrix-norm}
\end{align}

\subsection{Matrix amplitudes and column spinors}
\label{sec:matrix-spinors}

This subsection translates the intrinsic paravector spinor development of
Section~\ref{sec:physical-spinors} into the familiar matrix and column
languages.  For a paravector amplitude $\psi$, write
\begin{equation}
 \bM_\psi:=\operatorname{mat}(\psi).
\end{equation}
Faithfulness and Hermitian compatibility give
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(\psi'^{\mathsf H}Z\psi)
 =\bM_{\psi'}^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(Z)\bM_\psi.
\end{equation}
Scalar extraction is half the matrix trace, so the intrinsic definitions
\eqref{eq:paravector-inner-product} and
\eqref{eq:paravector-matrix-element} become
\begin{align}
 \langle\psi'\mid\psi\rangle
 &=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}
   (\bM_{\psi'}^{\mathsf H}\bM_\psi),\\
 \langle\psi'\mid Z\mid\psi\rangle
 &=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}
   (\bM_{\psi'}^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(Z)\bM_\psi).
\end{align}
Thus $\operatorname{mat}(\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi)
=\bM_\psi^{\mathsf H}\bM_\psi$ is generally Hermitian rather than scalar;
the scalar inner product is obtained by taking half its trace.

For the fixed unit direction $\bu$, the paravector projectors have matrix
images
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(\Pi_\pm)
 =\tfrac12(\bI_2\pm\bu\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Let $\bm v_+$ be a normalized positive eigenvector,
\begin{gather}
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)\bm v_+=\bm v_+\\
 \bm v_+^{\mathsf H}\bm v_+=1.
\end{gather}
A convenient phase choice, valid for $u_3\ne-1$, is
\begin{equation}
 \bm v_+
 =\frac{\{1+u_3,\ u_1+\ii u_2\}^{\mathsf T}}
 {\sqrt{2(1+u_3)}}.
\end{equation}
At the excluded direction $\bu=-\bm e_3$, use
\begin{equation}
 \bm v_+=\{0,1\}^{\mathsf T}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(\Pi_+)
 =\bm v_+\bm v_+^{\mathsf H}.
\end{equation}

For the projected amplitude $\psi'=\Pi_+\psi$, define
\begin{gather}
 \bM_{\psi'}:=\operatorname{mat}(\psi')
 =\operatorname{mat}(\Pi_+)\bM_\psi\\
 \bm\chi:=\bM_\psi^{\mathsf H}\bm v_+.
\end{gather}
The algebra, rank-one matrix, and two-component column are related by
\begin{align}
 \bm\chi&=\bM_{\psi'}^{\mathsf H}\bm v_+
 \label{eq:ideal-column-map}
 \\
 \bM_{\psi'}&=\bm v_+\bm\chi^{\mathsf H}.
 \label{eq:ideal-matrix-map}
\end{align}
Thus $\psi'$ and $\bm\chi\in\C^2$ contain the same two complex
components.  The matrix $\bM_{\psi'}$ has rank one and has
$\bm\chi^{\mathsf H}$ as its nonzero row precisely when the basis begins
with $\bm v_+$.  Conversely, $\bm v_+\bm\chi^{\mathsf H}$ uniquely
determines $\psi'$.  Moreover,
\begin{equation}
 \bm\chi^{\mathsf H}\bm\chi
 =\operatorname{tr}(\bM_{\psi'}^{\mathsf H}\bM_{\psi'})
 =2\scpart{\psi'^{\mathsf H}\psi'},
 \label{eq:minimal-ideal-spinor}
\end{equation}
For an observable acting from the left, the intrinsic matrix element is
\begin{equation}
 2\scpart{\psi'^{\mathsf H}Z\psi'}
 =\operatorname{tr}(\bM_{\psi'}^{\mathsf H}
   \operatorname{mat}(Z)\bM_{\psi'})
 =(\bm\chi^{\mathsf H}\bm\chi)
  (\bm v_+^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(Z)\bm v_+).
\end{equation}

The two left-projected matrix amplitudes are
\begin{equation}
 \bM_{\psi',\pm}
 :=\operatorname{mat}(\Pi_\pm)\bM_\psi.
\end{equation}
Consequently, the intrinsic probabilities in
\eqref{eq:spin-probability} have the matrix form
\begin{equation}
 p_\pm(\bu)
 =\frac{\operatorname{tr}
   (\bM_\psi^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(\Pi_\pm)^{\mathsf H}
    \operatorname{mat}(\Pi_\pm)\bM_\psi)}
  {\operatorname{tr}(\bM_\psi^{\mathsf H}\bM_\psi)}
 =\frac{\operatorname{tr}
   (\bM_\psi^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(\Pi_\pm)\bM_\psi)}
  {\operatorname{tr}(\bM_\psi^{\mathsf H}\bM_\psi)}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Quaternion subalgebra}
\label{sec:quaternions}

The real span of the scalar and bivector basis elements is a quaternion
subalgebra of the complex paravectors.  Define its three imaginary units by
\begin{gather}
 q_1=-\sigma_2\sigma_3=-\ii\sigma_1\\
 q_2=-\sigma_3\sigma_1=-\ii\sigma_2\\
 q_3=-\sigma_1\sigma_2=-\ii\sigma_3,
\end{gather}
or, collectively, $\bq=-\ii\bsig$.  For $n,m\in\{1,2,3\}$, the sigma
multiplication rule gives
\begin{equation}
 q_nq_m=-\delta_{nm}+\sum_{k=1}^{3}\epsilon_{nmk}q_k,
 \label{eq:quaternion-product}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{gather}
 q_1^2=q_2^2=q_3^2=-1\\
 q_1q_2=q_3\\
 q_1q_2q_3=-1,
\end{gather}
with the remaining products fixed by anticommutation.  For $a\in\R$ and
$\bA\in\R^3$, define the real paravector
\begin{equation}
 Q=a+\bA\cdot\bq
 =a-\ii\bA\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:quaternion-paravector}
\end{equation}
These are precisely the quaternions embedded in the paravector algebra.  For a
second quaternion $Q'=a'+\bA'\cdot\bq$, equation
\eqref{eq:quaternion-product} gives the familiar product
\begin{equation}
 QQ'=(aa'-\bA\cdot\bA')
 +(a\bA'+a'\bA+\bA\times\bA')\cdot\bq.
\end{equation}

Hermitian conjugation reverses every bivector, while sigma conjugation fixes
it:
\begin{gather}
 q_n^{\mathsf H}=-q_n\\
 q_n^*=q_n.
\end{gather}
Quaternion conjugation is therefore already the Hermitian operation,
\begin{gather}
 Q^{\mathsf H}=a-\bA\cdot\bq\\
 Q^*=Q\\
 \adj{Q}=Q^{\mathsf H}.
\end{gather}
It follows that
\begin{gather}
 QQ^{\mathsf H}=Q^{\mathsf H}Q=a^2+\bA^2=\det(Q)\\
 Q^{-1}=\frac{Q^{\mathsf H}}{a^2+\bA^2}
\end{gather}
for nonzero $Q$.

The faithful matrix representation sends the quaternion units to
\begin{gather}
 \operatorname{mat}(q_1)=
 \begin{Bmatrix}0&-\ii\\-\ii&0\end{Bmatrix}\\
 \operatorname{mat}(q_2)=
 \begin{Bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{Bmatrix}\\
 \operatorname{mat}(q_3)=
 \begin{Bmatrix}-\ii&0\\0&\ii\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{gather}
Hence equation~\eqref{eq:quaternion-paravector} becomes
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(Q)
 =a\bI_2-\ii\bA\cdot\bsig
 =\begin{Bmatrix}
   a-\ii A_3&-A_2-\ii A_1\\
   A_2-\ii A_1&a+\ii A_3
  \end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:quaternion-matrix}
\end{equation}
The algebraic conjugation and norm become the usual matrix identities
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}(Q^{\mathsf H})
 &=\operatorname{mat}(Q)^{\mathsf H},\\
 \operatorname{mat}(Q)^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(Q)
 &=\operatorname{mat}(Q)\operatorname{mat}(Q)^{\mathsf H}
 =(a^2+\bA^2)\bI_2,\\
 \det(\operatorname{mat}(Q))&=a^2+\bA^2.
\end{align}
Thus a unit quaternion is represented by a unitary matrix of determinant one.

Finally, for a unit axis $\bu$, let
\begin{equation}
 Q=\cos\frac\theta2+\bu\cdot\bq\,\sin\frac\theta2.
\end{equation}
Since $\bq=-\ii\bsig$, this quaternion is $R^{\mathsf H}$ for the rotor $R$
defined in Section~\ref{sec:transformations}.  The quaternion sandwich
\begin{equation}
 \br'\cdot\bq=Q(\br\cdot\bq)Q^{\mathsf H}
\end{equation}
therefore reproduces the same Rodrigues rotation.

\section{Conventions and cautions}
\label{sec:conventions-cautions}

\begin{itemize}
 \item The same symbol $\ii=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3$ is both the algebra's
 pseudoscalar and, in the Pauli representation, the ordinary complex unit.
 \item Sigma conjugation $(\cdot)^*$, the Hermitian operator $(\cdot)^{\mathsf H}$,
 and their composition are distinct.  In the Pauli representation,
 the Hermitian operator corresponds to Hermitian adjunction.
 \item $\det(Z)$ is an indefinite quadratic form and may be
 complex for complex $Z$.
 \item Differential operators act on everything to their right unless
 parentheses state otherwise.  This matters in products involving fields.
 \item Vectors and matrices are bold, and only vectors and matrices are bold.
 In particular, the electromagnetic fields formerly written as calligraphic
 or italic \(E\) and \(B\) are the upright bold vectors \(\bE\) and \(\bBf\),
 while scalar energy remains the unbold symbol \(E\).  Blackboard bold is
 retained only for the number sets \(\R\) and \(\C\).

 \item The action is written as \(\int L\dd t\), avoiding conflict with the
 scalar part \(S\).  The Lagrangian \(L\) and Hamiltonian \(H\) use the regular
 math font.  The Dirac block function is the upright bold \(\Wop(\cdot)\), and
 a vector square is explicitly defined by
 \(\bA^2:=\bA\cdot\bA\).

 \item Curly braces delimit explicit vector-entry lists and matrices, while
 commutators alone use square brackets.  Parentheses delimit functions,
 algebraic grouping, and order-sensitive operator products.
 Redundant grouping parentheses were removed.

 \item The extraction functions \(\operatorname{sc}(\cdot)\),
 \(\operatorname{vec}(\cdot)\), \(\operatorname{re}(\cdot)\), and
 \(\operatorname{im}(\cdot)\) replace \texttt{scalar}, \texttt{vector}, and
 their capitalized variants.  They are defined before use.  The unused
 expectation abbreviation \texttt{Exp} was removed.

 \item The original metric notation
 \(\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2\) and determinant bars were replaced
 by \(\det(Z)\).  Double bars are reserved for squared norms, with
 \[
  \lVert Z\rVert^2=\lVert S\rVert^2+\lVert\bV\rVert^2.
 \]
 Absolute-value bars are not used for paravector norms.  Entrywise
 complex-conjugation notation is confined to the matrix appendix.

 \item Sigma conjugation retains the splat \(^{*}\).  The operation originally
 written \(^{R}\), and elsewhere as a dagger, is written
 \(^{\mathsf H}\) because it reverses multiplied sigma order and becomes
 Hermitian adjunction in the matrix representation.  The abbreviation
 \(\operatorname{adj}(Z):=Z^{*\mathsf H}\) is defined early and used where it
 shortens formulas.

 \item The matrix appendix uses matching upright sans-serif capitals
 \(^{\mathsf T}\), \(^{\mathsf C}\), and \(^{\mathsf H}\) for transpose,
 entrywise complex conjugation, and Hermitian transpose.  It distinguishes
 these operations from sigma conjugation and states which operations reverse
 product order.

 \item Plus/minus labels are subscripts
 \(({}_\pm,{}_{+},{}_{-})\), not superscripts or parenthesized labels.
 Parallel and perpendicular components use \({}_{\parallel}\) and
 \({}_{\perp}\) rather than \texttt{par} and \texttt{prp}; their projection
 formulas are restated where needed.

 \item Spatial derivatives use \(\partial_{\br}\) rather than \(\nabla\), and
 no overbars are used.  Literal or malformed source commands such as
 \texttt{quad} and \texttt{frac} were converted to formatted \LaTeX.

 \item Long product expansions were consolidated while retaining their
 distinct components and results.  Expressions otherwise preserve the compact
 ordering of the source, for example \(\operatorname{sc}(\Phi^*U)\), without
 an extra pair of grouping parentheses.
\end{itemize}
