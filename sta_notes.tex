\begin{abstract}
This document develops a three-generator sigma algebra and its complex
paravectors, then applies them to relativity, electromagnetism, and quantum
mechanics.  Natural units $\hbar=c=1$ and signature $(+,-,-,-)$ are used.
\end{abstract}

\section{The algebra at a glance}
\label{sec:algebra}

The generators $\sigma_n$ are algebraic objects with ordered multiplication
rules.  They are not ordinary vector components: their products generally do
not commute, so the order of the factors is part of every expression.  The
following panel previews the generators, their product, and the general
complex element developed below.

\begin{center}
\setlength{\fboxsep}{1.2em}
\fcolorbox{MidnightBlue}{MidnightBlue!4}{%
\begin{minipage}{0.88\textwidth}
\begin{align*}
 \sigma_n^2&=1,
 &\sigma_n\sigma_m&=-\sigma_m\sigma_n\quad(n\ne m),\\
 \ii&=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3,
 &\ii^2&=-1,\\
 \bsig&=\{\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3\},
 &\ii\bsig&=\{\sigma_2\sigma_3,\sigma_3\sigma_1,\sigma_1\sigma_2\},\\
 Z&=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig,
 &a,b&\in\R,\quad \bA,\bB\in\R^3.
\end{align*}
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

Ordinary three-vector operations use
\begin{align}
 \bA\cdot\bB&=A_1B_1+A_2B_2+A_3B_3,\\
 \bA\times\bB&=\{A_2B_3-A_3B_2,
 A_3B_1-A_1B_3,A_1B_2-A_2B_1\},\\
 \bA^2&:=\bA\cdot\bA.
\end{align}
Thus the square of a vector always denotes its self dot product.

The set $\{1,\bsig,\ii\bsig,\ii\}$ is a complete basis, displayed by type as
\[
  \underbrace{1}_{\text{scalar}},\qquad
  \underbrace{\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3}_{\text{vectors}},\qquad
  \underbrace{\ii\sigma_1,\ii\sigma_2,\ii\sigma_3}_{\text{bivectors}},\qquad
  \underbrace{\ii}_{\text{pseudoscalar}}.
\]
Anticommutation gives
$\ii^2=-\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2\sigma_3^2=-1$.  The pseudoscalar is central:
moving $\sigma_n$ through the other two generators produces two sign
changes, so
\begin{equation}
 \ii\sigma_n=\sigma_n\ii.
\end{equation}
Thus its order of multiplication does not matter.

The commutator is
\begin{gather}
 [X,Y]:=XY-YX\\
 [Y,X]=-[X,Y].
\end{gather}
Thus $[X,Y]=0$ exactly when $X$ and $Y$ commute.
For $n,m,k\in\{1,2,3\}$, define the Kronecker delta by
\begin{equation}
 \delta_{nm}:=
 \begin{cases}
  1,&n=m,\\
  0,&n\ne m,
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
and the Levi--Civita symbol by
\begin{equation}
 \epsilon_{nmk}:=
 \begin{cases}
  +1,&(n,m,k)\text{ is an even permutation of }(1,2,3),\\
  -1,&(n,m,k)\text{ is an odd permutation of }(1,2,3),\\
  0,&\text{two or more indices are equal}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
For $n,m\in\{1,2,3\}$, the ordered sigma multiplication rule is
\begin{align}
  \sigma_n\sigma_m=\delta_{nm}
  +\sum_{k=1}^{3}\epsilon_{nmk}\,\ii\sigma_k
  \label{eq:sigma-product}\\
  [\sigma_n,\sigma_m]
  =2\sum_{k=1}^{3}\epsilon_{nmk}\,\ii\sigma_k.
\end{align}
\section{Hermitian and sigma-conjugate operators}
\label{sec:conjugate-operators}

Define the two operations by
\begin{align}
 (\,\cdot\,)^{\mathsf H}
 &:=\text{reverse the order of every product of sigma generators},\\
 (\,\cdot\,)^*
 &:=\text{replace every $\sigma_n$ by $-\sigma_n$ without changing product order}.
\end{align}
Thus, for two generators,
\begin{align}
 (\sigma_m\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}&=\sigma_n\sigma_m,\\
 (\sigma_m\sigma_n)^*&=(-\sigma_m)(-\sigma_n).
 \label{eq:two-sigma-operators}
\end{align}
Both operations act term by term on sums.  Their actions on the four basis
types are
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c c c c c}
  \toprule
  type & scalar & vector & bivector & pseudoscalar\\
  representative & $1$ & $\sigma_n$ & $\ii\sigma_n$ & $\ii$\\
  \midrule
  $(\cdot)^*$ & $+$ & $-$ & $+$ & $-$\\
  $(\cdot)^{\mathsf H}$ & $+$ & $+$ & $-$ & $-$\\
  $(\cdot)^{*\mathsf H}$ & $+$ & $-$ & $-$ & $+$\\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In particular,
\begin{align}
 \sigma_n^*&=-\sigma_n,\\
 \sigma_n^{\mathsf H}&=\sigma_n,\\
 \ii^*&=-\ii,\\
 \ii^{\mathsf H}&=-\ii.
\end{align}
For the bivectors, these actions combine to give
\begin{gather}
 (\ii\sigma_n)^*=\ii^*\sigma_n^*=\ii\sigma_n\\
 (\ii\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}
 =\sigma_n^{\mathsf H}\ii^{\mathsf H}=-\ii\sigma_n.
\end{gather}
Thus sigma conjugation preserves the original order of the factors, whereas
the Hermitian operator changes their order.
For arbitrary algebra elements $Z,Z'$, these definitions give
\[
  (ZZ')^{\mathsf H}=Z'^{\mathsf H}Z^{\mathsf H},
  \qquad
  (ZZ')^*=Z^* Z'^*,
  \qquad
  Z^{*\mathsf H}=Z^{\mathsf H*}.
\]
Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix} reproduces this as Hermitian conjugation
in the Pauli-matrix representation.

\section{Paravectors}
\label{sec:paravectors}

Throughout this section, a general complex paravector is
$Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig$ unless a subsection
states a real specialization.

\subsection{Core operations}
\label{sec:algebra-operations}

Let $a,b\in\R$ and $\bA,\bB\in\R^3$.  First form the two real
paravectors
\begin{gather}
 X:=a+\bA\cdot\bsig\\
 Y:=b+\bB\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
Their complex combination is the general complex paravector
\begin{equation}
 Z:=X+\ii Y
 =(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:paravector}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, collect its complex scalar and vector coefficients as
\begin{gather}
 S:=a+b\ii\\
 \bV:=\bA+\bB\ii\\
 Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
The corresponding coefficient-extraction operations are
\begin{gather}
 \scpart Z:=S\\
 \vecpart Z:=\bV.
\end{gather}
Sigma conjugation changes the sign of every sigma generator without changing
multiplied order; the Hermitian operator changes multiplied sigma order.
For the complex coefficients, write
\begin{gather}
 S^{\mathsf H}=S^*:=a-b\ii\\
 \bV^{\mathsf H}=\bV^*:=\bA-\bB\ii.
\end{gather}
For a paravector, the two operations are then
\begin{align}
 Z^*&:=S^*-\bV^*\cdot\bsig
 =(a-b\ii)+(-\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig,\\
 Z^{\mathsf H}&:=S^*+\bV^*\cdot\bsig
 =(a-b\ii)+(\bA-\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:algebra-star-H}
\end{align}
Their composition is the adjugate:
\begin{equation}
 \adj Z:=Z^{*\mathsf H}=S-\bV\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:adjugate}
\end{equation}
Define the paravector trace and determinant by
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{tr}(Z)&:=Z+\adj Z=2S,\\
 \det(Z)&:=\adj Z\,Z=Z\adj Z
 =S^2-\bV\cdot\bV.
 \label{eq:det}
\end{align}
For any paravectors $Z$ and $Z'$, the adjugate reverses product order:
\begin{equation}
 \adj{ZZ'}=\adj{Z'}\adj Z.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{align}
 \det(ZZ')
 &=\adj{ZZ'}ZZ'\\
 &=\adj{Z'}(\adj Z\,Z)Z'\\
 &=\det(Z)\det(Z').
 \label{eq:det-product}
\end{align}
For any complex scalar $c$,
\begin{equation}
 \adj{cZ}=c\adj Z.
\end{equation}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \det(cZ)=c^2\det(Z).
 \label{eq:det-scalar-scaling}
\end{equation}
Whenever $\det(Z)\ne0$, define
\begin{gather}
 Z^{-1}:=\frac{\adj Z}{\det(Z)}
 =\frac{S-\bV\cdot\bsig}{S^2-\bV^2}\\
 Z^{-1}Z=ZZ^{-1}=1.
\end{gather}
A paravector denoted by $U$ is called a unit when it has a two-sided
inverse.  Its defining property is
\begin{equation}
 UU^{-1}=U^{-1}U=1.
 \label{eq:unit-paravector}
\end{equation}
For a paravector this is equivalent to $\det(U)\ne0$, with
$U^{-1}=\adj U/\det(U)$.  The definition does not by itself require
$\det(U)=1$ or $U^{\mathsf H}=U^{-1}$; either is an additional property of
particular units.
For another paravector $Z'=S'+\bV'\cdot\bsig$, division by $Z$ is generally
order-sensitive and must be written using an explicitly ordered inverse.  For
invertible $Z$, the unambiguous quotient is defined when $Z'$ and $Z$ commute:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{Z'}{Z}:=Z'Z^{-1}=Z^{-1}Z'
 \qquad\text{if}\qquad \bV\times\bV'=0.
\end{equation}
Differentiating $Z^{-1}Z=1$ gives the general inverse rule
\begin{equation}
 \dd(Z^{-1})=-Z^{-1}\,\dd Z\,Z^{-1}.
 \label{eq:inverse-differential}
\end{equation}
Both trace and determinant are cyclic:
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{tr}(ZZ')&=\operatorname{tr}(Z'Z),\\
 \det(ZZ')&=\det(Z'Z).
 \label{eq:det-cyclic}
\end{align}
For three factors,
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{tr}(ZZ'Z'')
 &=\operatorname{tr}(Z'Z''Z)
 =\operatorname{tr}(Z''ZZ'),
 \label{eq:trace-cyclic}\\
 \det(ZZ'Z'')
 &=\det(Z'Z''Z)
 =\det(Z''ZZ').
\end{align}
For any complex paravector $Z$ and invertible complex paravector $T$, let
\begin{equation}
 Z':=T^{-1}ZT.
\end{equation}
Cyclicity then gives
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{tr}(Z')
 &=\operatorname{tr}(T^{-1}ZT)
 =\operatorname{tr}(ZTT^{-1})
 =\operatorname{tr}(Z),
 \label{eq:trace-similarity}\\
 \det(Z')
 &=\det(T^{-1}ZT)
 =\det(ZTT^{-1})
 =\det(Z).
 \label{eq:det-similarity}
\end{align}
Replacing $T$ by $T^{-1}$ gives the same two invariances for
$Z'=TZT^{-1}$.
Finally, define real and imaginary paravector extraction by
\begin{align}
 \repart Z&:=\tfrac12(Z+Z^{\mathsf H})=X,\\
 \impart Z&:=\frac{Z-Z^{\mathsf H}}{2\ii}=Y.
 \label{eq:algebra-re-im}
\end{align}
The functions $\operatorname{re}$ and $\operatorname{im}$ also act
componentwise on complex scalars and vectors.  The scalar and vector parts
are recovered by
\begin{align}
  S&=\scpart Z=\tfrac12(Z+Z^{*\mathsf H}),\\
  \bV&=\vecpart Z,\\
  \bV\cdot\bsig&=\tfrac12(Z-Z^{*\mathsf H}).
\end{align}
The four real coefficients then follow by composition:
\begin{align}
 a&=\repart S=\scpart X=\scpart{\repart Z},\\
 b&=\impart S=\scpart Y=\scpart{\impart Z},\\
 \bA&=\repart{\bV}=\vecpart X=\vecpart{\repart Z},\\
 \bB&=\impart{\bV}=\vecpart Y=\vecpart{\impart Z}.
\end{align}
These definitions are intrinsic to the algebra.  Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix}
later reproduces them through the faithful Pauli-matrix representation.

\subsection{Products and quadratic forms}

Here $\bA,\bB\in\R^3$ are real coefficient vectors.
From the ordered sigma multiplication rule,
\begin{align}
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)(\bB\cdot\bsig)
 &=\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sum_{m=1}^{3}
   A_nB_m\sigma_n\sigma_m\\
 &=\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sum_{m=1}^{3}A_nB_m\delta_{nm}
   +\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sum_{m=1}^{3}\sum_{k=1}^{3}
   A_nB_m\epsilon_{nmk}\ii\sigma_k\\
 &=\bA\cdot\bB+\ii(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:vector-product-derivation}
\end{align}
Adding the product with $\bA$ and $\bB$ interchanged gives
\begin{equation}
 2(\bA\cdot\bB)
 =(\bA\cdot\bsig)(\bB\cdot\bsig)
 +(\bB\cdot\bsig)(\bA\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Consequently, for every nonnegative integer $n$,
\begin{align}
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)^2&=\bA^2,\\
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)^{2n}&=(\bA^2)^n,\\
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)^{2n+1}&=(\bA^2)^n\bA\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}
Also,
\begin{equation}
 (\bA\cdot\bsig)\bsig=\bA-\ii\bA\times\bsig.
\end{equation}
The corresponding commutator is
\begin{equation}
 [\bA\cdot\bsig,\bB\cdot\bsig]
 =2\ii(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{align}
 [\bA\cdot\bsig,\bsig]&=-2\ii\bA\times\bsig,\\
 [\bA\cdot\ii\bsig,\bsig]&=2\bA\times\bsig,\\
 [\bA\cdot\bsig,\bsig]\cdot\bB
 &=2\ii(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}

\subsection{Quadratic identity and eigenvalues}

Recall $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, with $S\in\C$ and $\bV\in\C^3$.
Since $Z-S=\bV\cdot\bsig$ and
$(\bV\cdot\bsig)^2=\bV^2$,
\begin{align}
 (Z-S)^2&=\bV^2,\\
 Z^2-2SZ+(S^2-\bV^2)&=0.
\end{align}
Using $\operatorname{tr}(Z)=2S$ and
$\det(Z)=S^2-\bV^2$ gives the equivalent trace--determinant identity
\begin{equation}
 Z^2-\operatorname{tr}(Z)Z+\det(Z)=0.
  \label{eq:minimal}
\end{equation}
Replacing $Z$ by a scalar eigenvalue $\lambda$ and solving this quadratic
gives
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_\pm
 =\frac{\operatorname{tr}(Z)
 \pm\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(Z)^2-4\det(Z)}}{2}
 =S\pm\sqrt{\bV^2}.
\end{equation}
Because $\bV$ is generally complex, $\bV^2=\bV\cdot\bV$ and its square
root may also be complex.
The quadratic identity therefore factors as
\begin{equation}
 (Z-\lambda_-)(Z-\lambda_+)=0.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Real products}

Let
\begin{gather}
 X=a+\bA\cdot\bsig\\
 Y=b+\bB\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
Here $a,b\in\R$ and $\bA,\bB\in\R^3$.
Then
\begin{align}
 XY&=(ab+\bA\cdot\bB)
 +(a\bB+b\bA+\ii\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig,\\
 \scpart{XY}&=ab+\bA\cdot\bB,\\
 \vecpart{XY}&=a\bB+b\bA+\ii\bA\times\bB,\\
 [X,Y]&=2\ii(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig,\\
 X^2&=(a^2+\bA^2)+2a\bA\cdot\bsig,\\
 \scpart{X^2}&=a^2+\bA^2,\\
 \vecpart{X^2}&=2a\bA,\\
 X^2-2aX+(a^2-\bA^2)&=0.
\end{align}
The condition $\bA\times\bB=0$ implies
$XY=YX$ and $X^{-1}Y=YX^{-1}$.

\subsection{Complex products}

Let $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$ and $Z'=S'+\bV'\cdot\bsig$, where
$S,S'\in\C$ and $\bV,\bV'\in\C^3$.
\begin{equation}
 ZZ'=(SS'+\bV\cdot\bV')+
 \bigl(S\bV'+S'\bV+\ii\bV\times\bV'\bigr)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:para-product}
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{align}
 \scpart{ZZ'}&=SS'+\bV\cdot\bV',\\
 \vecpart{ZZ'}&=S\bV'+S'\bV+\ii\bV\times\bV'.
\end{align}
Equivalently, if $Z=X+\ii Y$ and $Z'=X'+\ii Y'$, then
\begin{equation}
 ZZ'=(XX'-YY')+\ii(XY'+YX').
\end{equation}

Let
\begin{gather}
 Z=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig,
\end{gather}
where $a,b\in\R$ and $\bA,\bB\in\R^3$,
and use primes for a second paravector with real primed components.  The four
real components of $ZZ'$ are
\begin{align}
 \text{real scalar: }&aa'-bb'+\bA\cdot\bA'-\bB\cdot\bB',\\
 \text{imaginary scalar: }&ab'+ba'+\bA\cdot\bB'+\bB\cdot\bA',\\
 \text{real vector: }&a\bA'-b\bB'+a'\bA-b'\bB
                    -\bA\times\bB'-\bB\times\bA',\\
 \text{imaginary vector: }&a\bB'+b\bA'+a'\bB+b'\bA
                    +\bA\times\bA'-\bB\times\bB'.
\end{align}
For the important quadratic products:
\begin{align}
 Z^2&=(a^2-b^2+\bA^2-\bB^2)
 +2\ii(ab+\bA\cdot\bB)
 +2(a\bA-b\bB)\cdot\bsig
 +2\ii(a\bB+b\bA)\cdot\bsig,\\
 ZZ^*&=(a^2+b^2-\bA^2-\bB^2)
 -2(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig
 +2\ii(a\bB-b\bA)\cdot\bsig,\\
 Z^*Z&=(a^2+b^2-\bA^2-\bB^2)
 +2(\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig
 +2\ii(a\bB-b\bA)\cdot\bsig,\\
 ZZ^{\mathsf H}&=a^2+b^2+\bA^2+\bB^2
 +2(a\bA+b\bB+\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig,\\
 Z^{\mathsf H}Z&=a^2+b^2+\bA^2+\bB^2
 +2(a\bA+b\bB-\bA\times\bB)\cdot\bsig,\\
 ZZ^{*\mathsf H}&=\det(Z)
 =a^2-b^2-\bA^2+\bB^2
 +2\ii(ab-\bA\cdot\bB).
\end{align}
The scalar part is cyclic, $\scpart{ZZ'}=\scpart{Z'Z}$.  The operator laws
already established also give
$(ZZ')^{*\mathsf H}=Z'^{*\mathsf H}Z^{*\mathsf H}$.
The opposite cross-product signs follow the displayed product order.  Thus
$ZZ^*=Z^*Z$ and $ZZ^{\mathsf H}=Z^{\mathsf H}Z$ precisely when
$\bA\times\bB=0$.

\begin{samepage}
\subsubsection{Norms}
For $S=a+b\ii$ and $\bV=\bA+\bB\ii$, with real components
$a,b,\bA,\bB$, define
\begin{align}
 \lVert S\rVert^2&:=SS^*=S^*S=a^2+b^2,\\
 \lVert\bV\rVert^2&:=\bV\cdot\bV^*=\bV^*\cdot\bV
 =\bA^2+\bB^2,\\
 \lVert Z\rVert^2&:=\lVert S\rVert^2+\lVert\bV\rVert^2.
 \label{eq:coefficient-norm}
\end{align}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
 \lVert Z\rVert^2=\scpart{Z^{\mathsf H}Z}
 =\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(Z^{\mathsf H}Z)
 =\scpart{ZZ^{\mathsf H}}
 =\scpart{Z^*Z^{*\mathsf H}}
 =\scpart{Z^{*\mathsf H}Z^*}.
 \label{eq:paravector-norm}
\end{equation}
These norms are not algebraic squares; in general,
\begin{gather}
 S^2\ne\lVert S\rVert^2\\
 \bV^2\ne\lVert\bV\rVert^2.
\end{gather}
For any complex scalar $c$,
\begin{equation}
 \lVert cZ\rVert^2=\lVert c\rVert^2\lVert Z\rVert^2.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Comparison with the determinant}
Recall $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$.  Its norm is real and nonnegative, whereas
$\det(Z)$ may be complex.  For $c=r e^{\ii\theta}$ with real $r$ and
$\theta$, the
determinant retains the phase while the norm does not:
\begin{align}
 \det(r e^{\ii\theta}Z)
 &=r^2\det(Z)e^{2\ii\theta},\\
 \lVert r e^{\ii\theta}Z\rVert^2
 &=r^2\lVert Z\rVert^2.
\end{align}
\end{samepage}

\subsection{Projection operators}
\label{sec:algebra-projectors}

For a real unit vector $\bu$, define
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_\pm:=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Since $(\bu\cdot\bsig)^2=1$,
\begin{align}
 \Pi_\pm^2&=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_+\Pi_-&=0,\\
 \Pi_++\Pi_-&=1,\\
 \Pi_\pm^{\mathsf H}&=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_\pm^*&=\Pi_\mp,\\
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)\Pi_\pm&=\pm \Pi_\pm.
\end{align}
Thus $\bu\cdot\bsig=\Pi_+-\Pi_-$.  For two unit directions,
\begin{align}
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)(\bu'\cdot\bsig)
 &=\bu\cdot\bu'+\ii(\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 [\bu\cdot\bsig,\bu'\cdot\bsig]
 &=2\ii(\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)(\bu'\cdot\bsig)(\bu\cdot\bsig)
 &=\left(2(\bu\cdot\bu')\bu-\bu'\right)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}
Define $\bu'_{\parallel}=(\bu'\cdot\bu)\bu$ and
$\bu'_{\perp}=\bu'-\bu'_{\parallel}$.  Then
\begin{align}
 \Pi_\pm(\bu'\cdot\bsig)\Pi_\pm
 &=\pm(\bu\cdot\bu')\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_\pm(\bu'\cdot\bsig)\Pi_\mp
 &=\tfrac12(\bu'_{\perp}\pm\ii(\bu\times\bu'))\cdot\bsig\,
 \Pi_\mp.
\end{align}
For comparison, define
$\Pi_\pm':=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu'\cdot\bsig)$.  Their products are
\begin{align}
 \Pi_+\Pi_+'&=\tfrac14(1+\bu\cdot\bu')
 +\tfrac14(+\bu+\bu'+\ii\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 \Pi_+\Pi_-'&=\tfrac14(1-\bu\cdot\bu')
 +\tfrac14(+\bu-\bu'-\ii\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 \Pi_-\Pi_+'&=\tfrac14(1-\bu\cdot\bu')
 +\tfrac14(-\bu+\bu'-\ii\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig,\\
 \Pi_-\Pi_-'&=\tfrac14(1+\bu\cdot\bu')
 +\tfrac14(-\bu-\bu'+\ii\bu\times\bu')\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}

For arbitrary real vector increments $\dd\bu$ and $\dd\bu'$, the same
dot--cross decomposition gives the geometric determinant identity
\begin{equation}
 \Det{(\dd\bu\cdot\bsig)(\dd\bu'\cdot\bsig)}
 =(\dd\bu\cdot\dd\bu')^2
 +\frac14\left\lVert
 [\dd\bu\cdot\bsig,\dd\bu'\cdot\bsig]\right\rVert^2.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt{\Det{(\dd\bu\cdot\bsig)(\dd\bu'\cdot\bsig)}}
 \geq\frac12\left\lVert
 [\dd\bu\cdot\bsig,\dd\bu'\cdot\bsig]\right\rVert.
\end{equation}
This is Lagrange's dot--cross identity written in the algebra; it is not a
quantum uncertainty relation.

\subsubsection{Ladder operators}
\label{sec:ladder-operators}

For projections along the third axis, define
\begin{gather}
 Z_\pm=\tfrac12(\sigma_1\pm\ii\sigma_2)\\
 \Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm\sigma_3).
\end{gather}
Then
\begin{align}
 [\sigma_3,Z_\pm]&=\pm2Z_\pm,\\
 Z_\pm^2&=0,\\
 Z_\pm Z_\mp&=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \sigma_1&=Z_++Z_-,\\
 \sigma_2&=(Z_+-Z_-)/\ii,\\
 \sigma_3&=\Pi_+-\Pi_-.
\end{align}
The ladder operators map between the two projected sectors according to
\begin{align}
 Z_+\Pi_+&=0,\\Z_+\Pi_-&=Z_+,\\
 \Pi_+Z_+&=Z_+,\\\Pi_-Z_+&=0,\\
 Z_-\Pi_-&=0,\\Z_-\Pi_+&=Z_-,\\
 \Pi_-Z_-&=Z_-,\\\Pi_+Z_-&=0.
\end{align}

\subsection{Functions of paravectors}
\label{sec:spectral}

Here $Z$ denotes the paravector to which the following constructions apply.

\subsubsection{General projector rule}
Let $\lambda_+,\lambda_-\in\C$.  Suppose
$\Pi_\pm^2=\Pi_\pm$, $\Pi_++\Pi_-=1$,
$\Pi_+\Pi_-=\Pi_-\Pi_+=0$, and
\begin{equation}
 Z=\lambda_+\Pi_++\lambda_-\Pi_-.
\end{equation}
Then orthogonality gives
\begin{equation}
 Z^n=\lambda_+^n\Pi_++\lambda_-^n\Pi_-.
\end{equation}
Thus, for a convergent power series $f(w)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_nw^n$,
\begin{align}
 f(Z)&=\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_nZ^n\\
 &=f(\lambda_+)\Pi_++f(\lambda_-)\Pi_-.
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Functions of a real unit vector}
For a real unit vector $\bu$, let
$X=\bu\cdot\bsig=\Pi_+-\Pi_-$.  Its eigenvalues are $\pm1$.
The general rule and $\Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig)$ give
\begin{equation}
 f(\bu\cdot\bsig)=f(1)\Pi_++f(-1)\Pi_-
 =\tfrac12(f(1)+f(-1))+\tfrac12(f(1)-f(-1))\bu\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
For the square root, the independent scalar choices
$\sqrt{1}=\pm1$ and $\sqrt{-1}=\pm\ii$ give
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt{\bu\cdot\bsig}=\pm\Pi_+\pm\ii\Pi_-.
\end{equation}
\subsubsection{Real paravectors}
Let $X=a+\bA\cdot\bsig$, where $a\in\R$ and $\bA\in\R^3$.
For $\bA\ne0$, put
\begin{gather}
 \bu:=\frac{\bA}{\lVert\bA\rVert}\\
 w:=\lVert\bA\rVert.
\end{gather}
Then define
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_\pm:=a\pm w.
\end{equation}
Using
$1=\Pi_++\Pi_-$ and $\bu\cdot\bsig=\Pi_+-\Pi_-$ gives
\begin{align}
 X&=a(\Pi_++\Pi_-)
 +w(\Pi_+-\Pi_-)\\
 &=\lambda_+\Pi_++\lambda_-\Pi_-.
\end{align}
The general rule therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 f(X)=f(\lambda_+)\Pi_++f(\lambda_-)\Pi_-.
\end{equation}
With nonzero eigenvalues where required and chosen function branches, this
includes
\begin{align}
 X^n&=\tfrac12(\lambda_+^n+\lambda_-^n)
 +\tfrac12(\lambda_+^n-\lambda_-^n)\bu\cdot\bsig,\\
 X^{-1}&=\lambda_+^{-1}\Pi_++\lambda_-^{-1}\Pi_-,\\
 \sqrt X&=\pm\sqrt{\lambda_+}\Pi_+\pm\sqrt{\lambda_-}\Pi_-.
\end{align}
The exponential and logarithm are developed below.
The spectral inverse agrees with the adjugate--determinant formula developed
earlier.  Indeed,
\begin{gather}
 \adj{X}=\lambda_-\Pi_++\lambda_+\Pi_-\\
 \det(X)=\lambda_+\lambda_-,
\end{gather}
and therefore, when $\lambda_+\lambda_-\ne0$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\adj{X}}{\det(X)}
 =\lambda_+^{-1}\Pi_++\lambda_-^{-1}\Pi_-
 =X^{-1}.
\end{equation}
For a fixed analytic branch, the function formula reduces continuously to
$f(a)$ when $\bA=0$.  The projectors themselves are then undefined, and the
independent square roots require the separate treatment below.

\subsubsection{Direct square-root components}
Let
\begin{gather}
 X=a+\bA\cdot\bsig\\
 Y=b+\bB\cdot\bsig,
\end{gather}
where $a,b\in\R$ and $\bA,\bB\in\R^3$.  Direct multiplication gives
\begin{equation}
 X^2=a^2+\bA^2+2a\bA\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Thus $X^2=Y$ requires separate equality of the scalar and vector parts:
\begin{gather}
 a^2+\bA^2=b\\
 2a\bA=\bB.
\end{gather}
For $\bB\ne0$, the second equation implies $a\ne0$ and fixes $\bA$ to be
parallel to $\bB$:
\begin{equation}
 \bA=\frac{\bB}{2a}.
\end{equation}
Substitution into the scalar equation gives
\begin{equation}
 a^4-ba^2+\frac{\bB^2}{4}=0.
\end{equation}
Solving this quadratic in $a^2$ yields
\begin{equation}
 a^2=\tfrac12\left(b\pm\sqrt{b^2-\bB^2}\right).
\end{equation}
For either inner sign, choose either square root of $a^2$ and then set
\begin{gather}
 a=\pm\sqrt{a^2}\\
 \bA=\frac{\bB}{2a}\\
 \bA^2=b-a^2.
\end{gather}
When $b>\lVert\bB\rVert$ and $\bB\ne0$, the two choices of $a^2$ and the
two overall signs give four real roots, in agreement with the independent
projector signs above.  At $b=\lVert\bB\rVert>0$, the two values of $a^2$
coincide and give two distinct real roots.  No real root exists when
$b<\lVert\bB\rVert$.

If $\bB=0$, the division by $a$ used above does not cover every possibility.
For $b>0$, the scalar roots $X=\pm\sqrt b$ coexist with the pure-vector roots
$X=\bA\cdot\bsig$ for any $\bA^2=b$.  For $b=0$, the only real root is
$X=0$, and for $b<0$ there is no real paravector root.  The nondegenerate
complex case is covered by the spectral construction below; complex-null and
scalar cases are stated separately there.

\subsubsection{Complex spectral form}
For $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, with $S\in\C$, $\bV\in\C^3$, and
$\bV^2\ne0$, define
\begin{equation}
 w:=+\sqrt{\bV^2}.
\end{equation}
Here the square root is the principal scalar square root, so
$w^2=\bV^2=\bV\cdot\bV$.  Define
\begin{equation}
 \bu:=\frac{\bV}{w}.
\end{equation}
This normalization uses the complex bilinear dot product:
\begin{equation}
 \bu\cdot\bu=\frac{\bV\cdot\bV}{w^2}=1.
\end{equation}
Because $\bu$ is complex, its Hermitian norm $\lVert\bu\rVert^2$ need not
equal $1$.  Define
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_\pm:=S\pm w.
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_\pm:=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Because $(\bu\cdot\bsig)^2=\bu\cdot\bu=1$,
\begin{align}
 \Pi_\pm^2
 &=\tfrac14\left(1\pm2\bu\cdot\bsig
 +(\bu\cdot\bsig)^2\right)=\Pi_\pm,\\
 \Pi_+\Pi_-
 &=\tfrac14\left(1-(\bu\cdot\bsig)^2\right)=0.
\end{align}
Also, $1=\Pi_++\Pi_-$ and
$\bu\cdot\bsig=\Pi_+-\Pi_-$, so
\begin{align}
 Z&=S(\Pi_++\Pi_-)+w(\Pi_+-\Pi_-)\\
 &=\lambda_+\Pi_++\lambda_-\Pi_-.
\end{align}
The general rule gives
\begin{equation}
 f(Z)=f(\lambda_+)\Pi_++f(\lambda_-)\Pi_-.
 \label{eq:complex-function-projectors}
\end{equation}
The adjugate and determinant have the corresponding spectral forms
\begin{gather}
 \adj{Z}=\lambda_-\Pi_++\lambda_+\Pi_-\\
 \det(Z)=\lambda_+\lambda_-.
\end{gather}
Consequently, when $\lambda_+\lambda_-\ne0$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\adj{Z}}{\det(Z)}
 =\lambda_+^{-1}\Pi_++\lambda_-^{-1}\Pi_-
 =Z^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Let $\sqrt{\lambda_+}$ and $\sqrt{\lambda_-}$ denote the principal scalar
roots.  The two signs below are chosen independently, giving up to four
square roots
\begin{equation}
 \sqrt Z
 =\pm\sqrt{\lambda_+}\Pi_+\pm\sqrt{\lambda_-}\Pi_-.
\end{equation}
They are four distinct roots when both eigenvalues are nonzero.  If one
eigenvalue vanishes, its two sign choices coincide.

For completeness, suppose instead that $\bV^2=0$ and put
$N=\bV\cdot\bsig$, so $N^2=0$.  For any scalar function regular at $S$,
the power series terminates in the nilpotent part:
\begin{equation}
 f(Z)=f(S)+f'(S)N.
\end{equation}
When $S\ne0$, this gives
\begin{gather}
 \sqrt Z=r+\frac{N}{2r},
 \qquad r^2=S,\\
 \log Z=\log S+\frac{N}{S},
\end{gather}
with a consistent scalar branch.  If $S=0$ and $N\ne0$, the nonzero
nilpotent paravector has neither a square root nor a logarithm in this
algebra.  When $N=0$, a fixed analytic branch gives the scalar value
$f(S)$; additional non-scalar roots of a scalar matrix must be found by
solving the square-root equation directly rather than by limiting the
projectors.

\subsubsection{Exponential of a general complex paravector}
Let $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, $w=+\sqrt{\bV^2}$, and $\bu=\bV/w$.

\subsubsection{Projection derivation}
Recall $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, $w=+\sqrt{\bV^2}$,
$\bu=\bV/w$, $\lambda_\pm=S\pm w$, and
$\Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig)$.  The projected function
rule~\eqref{eq:complex-function-projectors} gives
\begin{equation}
 \exp(Z)
 =\frac{\exp(S+w)}{2}(1+\bu\cdot\bsig)
 +\frac{\exp(S-w)}{2}(1-\bu\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Factoring out $\exp(S)$ and collecting the scalar and vector parts gives
\begin{equation}
 \exp(Z)
 =\exp(S)\left(
 \frac{\exp(w)+\exp(-w)}{2}
 +\frac{\exp(w)-\exp(-w)}{2}\,\bu\cdot\bsig
 \right).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Direct series derivation}
Recall $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, $w=+\sqrt{\bV^2}$, and $\bu=\bV/w$.
Because $S$ commutes with $\bV\cdot\bsig$,
\begin{equation}
 \exp(Z)=\exp(S)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
 \frac{(w\bu\cdot\bsig)^n}{n!}.
\end{equation}
The identity $(\bu\cdot\bsig)^2=1$ gives, for every nonnegative
integer $n$,
\begin{gather}
 (w\bu\cdot\bsig)^{2n}=w^{2n}\\
 (w\bu\cdot\bsig)^{2n+1}=w^{2n+1}\bu\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
Separating the series into even and odd powers therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 \exp(Z)=\exp(S)\left(
 \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{2n}}{(2n)!}
 +\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{w^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}\right)
 \bu\cdot\bsig
 \right).
\end{equation}
The scalar definitions of $\cosh w$ and $\sinh w$ reduce both derivations to
the same general result:
\begin{equation}
 \exp(Z)
 =\exp(S)(\cosh w+\sinh w\,\bu\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
This result does not depend on which root is chosen.  Explicitly,
\begin{gather}
 \cosh(-w)=\cosh w\\
 \sinh(-w)(-\bu)=\sinh w\,\bu.
\end{gather}
Replacing the principal root $w$ by the other algebraic root $-w$ also
changes $\bu=\bV/w$ to $-\bu$.  Thus both terms in the general result are
unchanged.

\subsubsection{Component form and real/imaginary limits}
For
\begin{equation}
 Z=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig,
\end{equation}
the principal root is
\begin{equation}
 w=+\sqrt{\bA^2-\bB^2+2\ii\bA\cdot\bB}.
\end{equation}
The corresponding complex bilinear unit direction is
\begin{equation}
 \bu=\frac{\bA+\bB\ii}{w}.
\end{equation}
The fully expanded formula is
\begin{equation}
 \exp(Z)
 =\exp(a)(\cos b+\ii\sin b)
 \left(\cosh w+\frac{\sinh w}{w}
 (\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig\right).
\end{equation}

For a real vector, choose $w=\lVert\bA\rVert$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \exp(\bA\cdot\bsig)
 =\cosh\lVert\bA\rVert
 +\sinh\lVert\bA\rVert
 \frac{\bA}{\lVert\bA\rVert}\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Because the real scalar $a$ commutes with the vector part, the strictly real
paravector $X=a+\bA\cdot\bsig$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \exp(X)=\exp(a)\left(
 \cosh\lVert\bA\rVert
 +\sinh\lVert\bA\rVert
 \frac{\bA}{\lVert\bA\rVert}\cdot\bsig\right).
\end{equation}

For an imaginary vector, choose $w=\ii\lVert\bB\rVert$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \exp(\ii\bB\cdot\bsig)
 =\cos\lVert\bB\rVert
 +\ii\sin\lVert\bB\rVert
 \frac{\bB}{\lVert\bB\rVert}\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Because the imaginary scalar $\ii b$ also commutes with the vector part, the
strictly imaginary paravector $Z=\ii(b+\bB\cdot\bsig)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \exp(Z)=(\cos b+\ii\sin b)\left(
 \cos\lVert\bB\rVert
 +\ii\sin\lVert\bB\rVert
 \frac{\bB}{\lVert\bB\rVert}\cdot\bsig\right).
\end{equation}
Each quotient in these formulas is understood by its analytic limit at a
zero vector.

\subsubsection{Kinematic form}
Let $\bA\in\R^3$ be nonzero.  Its real-vector exponential has a direct
kinematic interpretation.
Define
\begin{gather}
 \gamma:=\cosh\lVert\bA\rVert\\
 \bv:=\tanh\lVert\bA\rVert
 \frac{\bA}{\lVert\bA\rVert}.
\end{gather}
Then $\gamma=(1-\bv^2)^{-1/2}$ and
\begin{equation}
 \exp(\bA\cdot\bsig)=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Thus $\lVert\bA\rVert$ is the rapidity and $\bA/\lVert\bA\rVert$ is the
direction of motion.  This is the normalized future-directed paravector
used below for four-velocity.

\subsubsection{Cayley transform}
For the real paravector $Y=b+\bB\cdot\bsig$, assume
$0<\lVert\bB\rVert<b$ and put
\begin{equation}
 \bu:=\frac{\bB}{\lVert\bB\rVert}.
\end{equation}
Because $Y$ is real,
\begin{equation}
 Y^*=\adj Y=b-\bB\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Its determinant is
\begin{equation}
 \det(Y)=Y\adj Y=b^2-\lVert\bB\rVert^2.
\end{equation}
The assumed range makes $\adj Y$ invertible.  Its vector part is collinear
with that of $Y$, so the unambiguous quotient notation introduced above
applies.  Multiplication by $Y/Y$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{Y}{\adj Y}
 =\frac{Y}{\adj Y}\frac{Y}{Y}
 =\frac{Y^2}{\det(Y)}.
\end{equation}
The numerator on the right expands as
\begin{equation}
 Y^2
 =(b+\bB\cdot\bsig)^2
 =b^2+\lVert\bB\rVert^2+2b\bB\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Dividing this expression by $\det(Y)$ therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{b+\bB\cdot\bsig}{b-\bB\cdot\bsig}
 =\frac{b^2+\lVert\bB\rVert^2}{b^2-\lVert\bB\rVert^2}
 +\frac{2b\lVert\bB\rVert}{b^2-\lVert\bB\rVert^2}
 \bu\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}
 \theta:=2\tanh^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\lVert\bB\rVert}{b}\right).
\end{equation}
The double-angle identities give
\begin{gather}
 \cosh\theta=\frac{b^2+\lVert\bB\rVert^2}
 {b^2-\lVert\bB\rVert^2}\\
 \sinh\theta=\frac{2b\lVert\bB\rVert}
 {b^2-\lVert\bB\rVert^2}.
\end{gather}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \frac{b+\bB\cdot\bsig}{b-\bB\cdot\bsig}
 &=\cosh\theta+\sinh\theta\,\bu\cdot\bsig\\
 &=\exp(\theta\,\bu\cdot\bsig)\\
 &=\exp(\theta)\Pi_++\exp(-\theta)\Pi_-.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Comparing this result with the kinematic form above identifies its
boost-generator vector:
\begin{gather}
 \bA=\theta\bu\\
 \theta=\lVert\bA\rVert.
\end{gather}
The definition of $\theta$ then gives the map from the boost-generator
vector $\bA$ to the Cayley vector $\bB$:
\begin{equation}
 \bB=b\tanh\!\left(\frac{\lVert\bA\rVert}{2}\right)
 \frac{\bA}{\lVert\bA\rVert}.
\end{equation}
Thus $\bA$ is the rapidity vector, whereas $\bB/b$ is the Cayley parameter;
its magnitude is the hyperbolic tangent of half the rapidity.  The choice
$b=1$ recovers the normalized Cayley form, and the case $\bB=0$ follows by
continuity.

Finally, the double-angle identities express the kinematic quantities
directly in terms of the Cayley variables:
\begin{gather}
 \gamma=\frac{b^2+\lVert\bB\rVert^2}
 {b^2-\lVert\bB\rVert^2}\\
 \bv=\frac{2b\bB}{b^2+\lVert\bB\rVert^2}.
\end{gather}
Substitution makes the three equivalent forms explicit:
\begin{equation}
 \exp(\bA\cdot\bsig)
 =\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig)
 =\frac{b+\bB\cdot\bsig}{b-\bB\cdot\bsig}
 =\frac{Y}{Y^*}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Aligned boost and rotation}
Let $\bu\in\R^3$ satisfy $\bu^2=1$, and let $\phi,\theta\in\R$.  Write
$w$ as
\begin{equation}
 w:=\phi+\ii\theta.
\end{equation}
The parallel real and imaginary generators commute, so
\begin{equation}
 \exp(w\bu\cdot\bsig)
 =\exp(\phi\bu\cdot\bsig)\exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
The factors are
\begin{gather}
 \exp(\phi\bu\cdot\bsig)
 =\cosh\phi+\sinh\phi\,\bu\cdot\bsig\\
 \exp(\ii\theta\bu\cdot\bsig)
 =\cos\theta+\ii\sin\theta\,\bu\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
Thus $\phi$ is boost-like and $\theta$ is rotation-like.  Their product is
\begin{equation}
 \exp(w\bu\cdot\bsig)
 =(\cosh\phi\cos\theta+\ii\sinh\phi\sin\theta)
 +(\sinh\phi\cos\theta+\ii\cosh\phi\sin\theta)
 \bu\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
With the conventional factor $1/2$ in the transformation generator,
$2\phi$ is the rapidity and $2\theta$ is the rotation angle.  For complex
$\bu$, the real and imaginary generator vectors need not be parallel, so
this interpretation does not generally apply.

If $\bV^2=0$, then $(\bV\cdot\bsig)^2=0$, even when $\bV$ is a nonzero
complex-null vector, and the exponential terminates:
\begin{equation}
 \exp(Z)=\exp(S)(1+\bV\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Finally, the defining power series respects both conjugation operations:
\begin{gather}
 \exp(Z^*)=\exp(Z)^*\\
 \exp(Z^{\mathsf H})=\exp(Z)^{\mathsf H}.
\end{gather}

\subsubsection{Logarithm of a general complex paravector}
Recall $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, $w=+\sqrt{\bV^2}$,
$\bu=\bV/w$, and $\lambda_\pm=S\pm w$.  For a nonzero complex scalar
$\lambda$, each scalar logarithm has the form
\begin{equation}
 \log\lambda=\log\lVert\lambda\rVert+\ii(\arg\lambda+2n\pi),
 \qquad n\in\mathbb Z.
\end{equation}
For $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, $w=+\sqrt{\bV^2}$,
$\bu=\bV/w$, and $\lambda_\pm=S\pm w$, assume
both eigenvalues are nonzero and choose one scalar logarithm of each.  The
projected function rule gives
\begin{equation}
 \log Z=\log(\lambda_+)\Pi_++\log(\lambda_-)\Pi_-.
\end{equation}
Substitution of the projectors and collection of the scalar and vector parts
gives
\begin{equation}
 \log Z
 =\tfrac12\left(\log(\lambda_+)+\log(\lambda_-)\right)
 +\tfrac12\left(\log(\lambda_+)-\log(\lambda_-)\right)
 \bu\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Changing $w$ to $-w$ changes $\bu$ to $-\bu$ and interchanges
$\lambda_+$ with $\lambda_-$ and $\Pi_+$ with $\Pi_-$, leaving this result
unchanged.  Independent branch
choices for the two scalar logarithms give the different logarithms of $Z$.

\subsubsection{Special cases}
Let $\bu\in\R^3$ satisfy $\bu^2=1$, and recall
$\Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig)$.  The paravector
$\bu\cdot\bsig$ has eigenvalues $1$ and $-1$.  Hence all projected branches
are
\begin{equation}
 \log(\bu\cdot\bsig)
 =2n\pi\ii\Pi_++(2m+1)\pi\ii\Pi_-,
 \qquad n,m\in\mathbb Z.
\end{equation}
For the positive real paravector $Y=b+\bB\cdot\bsig$, with
$b>\lVert\bB\rVert$, the real logarithm is
\begin{equation}
 \log Y=\tfrac12\log(b^2-\bB^2)
 +\tanh^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\lVert\bB\rVert}{b}\right)
 \frac{\bB}{\lVert\bB\rVert}\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
The vector quotient is understood by its limit when $\bB=0$.

The square-root and logarithm formulas above are understood with a fixed
analytic branch wherever that branch is regular.  Independent branch choices
need not have a finite limit when the two eigenvalues coalesce.

\section{Transformations and invariants}
\label{sec:preserved-quantities}

Here $Z$ is a complex paravector, and $T$ and $T'$ are general complex
transformation paravectors.  Recall from equation~\eqref{eq:unit-paravector}
that $U$ denotes a unit paravector.  A unitary transformation is a unit $U$
that additionally satisfies
\begin{align}
 U^{\mathsf H}U=UU^{\mathsf H}&=1
 \label{eq:unitary-transform}\\
 U^{\mathsf H}&=U^{-1}.
\end{align}
The transformations used below are organized by the quantities they leave
unchanged:
\begin{gather}
 \operatorname{tr}(Z)=2\scpart Z\\
 \det(Z)\\
 \lVert Z\rVert^2
 =\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(Z^{\mathsf H}Z).
\end{gather}
The trace is linear; the determinant and norm are quadratic.  The prime on
$T'$ marks a second transformation factor rather than a derivative.  In the
physical sections, the same unit notation is specialized locally to proper
velocity by recalling $U=\dd X/\dd s$.

\subsection{Similarity invariants}

Throughout this subsection, $Z$ is a complex paravector and the displayed
transformation factors act by ordered multiplication.

\subsubsection{Trace invariance}
Let $Z$ be a complex paravector and $T$ an invertible complex paravector.
Consider either similarity:
\begin{gather}
 Z'=T^{-1}ZT,\\
 Z'=TZT^{-1}.
\end{gather}
For example, cyclicity gives
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{tr}(T^{-1}ZT)
 =\operatorname{tr}(ZTT^{-1})
 =\operatorname{tr}(Z).
\end{equation}
Thus $\operatorname{tr}(Z')=\operatorname{tr}(Z)$.  For unitary $U$, the
same similarities are
\begin{gather}
 Z'=U^{\mathsf H}ZU,\\
 Z'=UZU^{\mathsf H}.
\end{gather}

\subsubsection{Determinant invariance}
Let $Z,T,T'$ be complex paravectors and set $Z'=T'ZT$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \det(Z')=\det(T')\det(T)\det(Z).
\end{equation}
Hence $\det(T')\det(T)=1$ guarantees
$\det(Z')=\det(Z)$, and is required if this equality is to hold for arbitrary
$Z$.  Equivalently, when $\det(T)\ne0$, any $T'$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
 \det(T')=\frac{1}{\det(T)}
\end{equation}
preserves the determinant of every $Z$.  The automatic choice
$T'=T^{-1}$ gives the similarity
\begin{equation}
 Z'=T^{-1}ZT.
\end{equation}
The opposite ordering $Z'=TZT^{-1}$ preserves the determinant as well.
For unitary $U$,
\begin{equation}
 \det(U^{\mathsf H})\det(U)
 =\det(U^{-1})\det(U)=1,
\end{equation}
so both unitary sandwiches preserve the determinant.

\subsubsection{Norm invariance}
Let $Z$ be a complex paravector and $U$ a unitary paravector satisfying
$U^{\mathsf H}U=UU^{\mathsf H}=1$.  For example, if
$Z'=U^{\mathsf H}ZU$, then
\begin{align}
 \lVert Z'\rVert^2
 &=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}
   (U^{\mathsf H}Z^{\mathsf H}UU^{\mathsf H}ZU)\\
 &=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(Z^{\mathsf H}Z)
 =\lVert Z\rVert^2.
\end{align}
The same equality $\lVert Z'\rVert^2=\lVert Z\rVert^2$ holds for
\begin{gather}
 Z'=UZ\\
 Z'=ZU\\
 Z'=U^{\mathsf H}ZU\\
 Z'=UZU^{\mathsf H}.
\end{gather}
It does not hold in general when $T$ is nonunitary.

Rotations and boosts preserve different combinations of these invariants.
A rotation satisfies
\begin{gather}
 R^{\mathsf H}=R^{-1}\\
 R^{\mathsf H}R=RR^{\mathsf H}=1,
\end{gather}
so $R$ is unitary; $Z'=R^{\mathsf H}ZR$ preserves trace, determinant, and
norm.  A nonzero boost satisfies
\begin{gather}
 L^{\mathsf H}=L\\
 \det(L)=1\\
 L^{\mathsf H}L=L^2\ne1,
\end{gather}
so $L$ is not unitary.  The transformation $Z'=L^{-1}ZL^{-1}$ preserves the
determinant but does not generally preserve the trace or norm.

\section{Differentiated fields}
\label{sec:differentiated-fields}

\subsection{Paravector differential operators}

In this subsection, $t\in\R$ is a real scalar coordinate and
$\br=\{r_1,r_2,r_3\}\in\R^3$ is a real spatial vector.  Define their
derivatives and the paravector differential operators by
\begin{gather}
 \partial_t:=\frac{\partial}{\partial t},
 \qquad
 \pdr:=\left\{
  \frac{\partial}{\partial r_1},
  \frac{\partial}{\partial r_2},
  \frac{\partial}{\partial r_3}\right\},\\
 \partial:=\partial_t+\bsig\cdot\pdr
 =\partial_t+\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sigma_n\frac{\partial}{\partial r_n},\\
 \partial^*=\adj{\partial}
 =\partial_t-\bsig\cdot\pdr.
 \label{eq:paravector-derivative}
\end{gather}
We call $\partial$ the partial paravector and $\partial^*$ its
sigma-conjugate partial paravector.  They act from the left on every field to
their right unless parentheses state otherwise.  The sigma generators are
constant, while the coefficient derivatives act on the fields.

For fields whose second coordinate derivatives commute, the spatial part
squares to a scalar operator:
\begin{align}
 (\bsig\cdot\pdr)^2
 &={}
 \sum_{n,m=1}^{3}\sigma_n\sigma_m
 \frac{\partial^2}{\partial r_n\partial r_m}\notag\\
 &=\sum_{n=1}^{3}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r_n^2}
 +\sum_{n<m}(\sigma_n\sigma_m+\sigma_m\sigma_n)
 \frac{\partial^2}{\partial r_n\partial r_m}\notag\\
 &=\sum_{n=1}^{3}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r_n^2}
 =\pdr\cdot\pdr.
 \label{eq:spatial-paravector-square}
\end{align}
The mixed time--space terms also cancel because the coordinate derivatives
commute.  Therefore
\begin{align}
 \partial\partial^*
 &=(\partial_t+\bsig\cdot\pdr)
   (\partial_t-\bsig\cdot\pdr)
 =\partial_t^2-\pdr\cdot\pdr,\\
 \partial^*\partial
 &=(\partial_t-\bsig\cdot\pdr)
   (\partial_t+\bsig\cdot\pdr)
 =\partial_t^2-\pdr\cdot\pdr.
\end{align}
Thus the two orders give the same scalar second-order operator,
\begin{equation}
 \partial\partial^*=\partial^*\partial
 =\partial_t^2-\pdr\cdot\pdr
 =\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}
  -\sum_{n=1}^{3}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r_n^2}
 =:\Box,
 \label{eq:dalembertian}
\end{equation}
called the d'Alembertian.  The equalities here are identities of operators:
for example, $\partial\partial^*Z$ means
$\partial(\partial^*Z)$.

\subsection{Differentiated paravector fields}

First let the single paravector field
\begin{equation}
 Z(t,\br)=(a+b\ii)+(\bA+\bB\ii)\cdot\bsig
\end{equation}
be given, with $a$, $b$, $\bA$, and $\bB$ depending on $(t,\br)$.  Direct use
of the ordered vector product gives
\begin{align}
 \partial Z={}&(\partial_ta+\pdr\cdot\bA)
 +\ii(\partial_tb+\pdr\cdot\bB)\\
 &+(\pdr a+\partial_t\bA-\pdr\times\bB)\cdot\bsig\\
 &+\ii(\pdr b+\pdr\times\bA+\partial_t\bB)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:differentiated-paravector}
\end{align}
Multiplication by $\ii$ rotates these four components: the real scalar becomes
$-\partial_tb-\pdr\cdot\bB$, the imaginary scalar becomes
$\partial_ta+\pdr\cdot\bA$, the real vector becomes the negative of the
imaginary vector above, and the imaginary vector becomes the real vector.

Now let $Z(t,\br)$ and $Z'(t,\br)$ be paravector fields; the prime labels the
second field and is not a derivative.  For their product,
\begin{align}
 \partial(ZZ')=(\partial Z)Z'+Z(\partial Z')
 +[\bsig,Z]\cdot\pdr Z',\\
 \partial^*(ZZ')=(\partial^* Z)Z'+Z(\partial^* Z')
 -[\bsig,Z]\cdot\pdr Z'.
 \label{eq:paravector-product-rules}
\end{align}
Here the final contraction is defined componentwise, with multiplied order
retained:
\begin{equation}
 [\bsig,Z]\cdot\pdr Z'
 :=\sum_{n=1}^{3}[\sigma_n,Z]
       \frac{\partial Z'}{\partial r_n}.
 \label{eq:paravector-commutator-contraction}
\end{equation}
To derive the first rule, apply the ordinary scalar product rule to every
coefficient derivative:
\begin{align}
 \partial(ZZ')
 ={}&(\partial_tZ)Z'+Z(\partial_tZ')\notag\\
 &+\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sigma_n
 \left(\frac{\partial Z}{\partial r_n}Z'
       +Z\frac{\partial Z'}{\partial r_n}\right)\notag\\
 ={}&(\partial Z)Z'+Z(\partial Z')
 +\sum_{n=1}^{3}(\sigma_nZ-Z\sigma_n)
       \frac{\partial Z'}{\partial r_n}\notag\\
 ={}&(\partial Z)Z'+Z(\partial Z')
 +[\bsig,Z]\cdot\pdr Z'.
 \label{eq:paravector-product-rule-derivation}
\end{align}
The same expansion with the negative spatial part of $\partial^*$ gives
\begin{align}
 \partial^*(ZZ')
 ={}&(\partial^*Z)Z'+Z(\partial^*Z')\notag\\
 &+\sum_{n=1}^{3}(-\sigma_nZ+Z\sigma_n)
       \frac{\partial Z'}{\partial r_n}\notag\\
 ={}&(\partial^*Z)Z'+Z(\partial^*Z')
 -[\bsig,Z]\cdot\pdr Z'.
 \label{eq:paravector-star-product-rule-derivation}
\end{align}
Thus the opposite signs come directly from the opposite spatial signs in
$\partial$ and $\partial^*$.

As a concrete noncommuting check, take $Z=\sigma_2$ and $Z'=r_1$.  Only
the $r_1$ derivative remains, and
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_1\sigma_2=\ii\sigma_3,
 \qquad
 \sigma_2\sigma_1=-\ii\sigma_3,
 \qquad
 [\sigma_1,\sigma_2]=2\ii\sigma_3.
\end{equation}
The first right-hand side gives
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_2\sigma_1+[\sigma_1,\sigma_2]
 =-\ii\sigma_3+2\ii\sigma_3
 =\ii\sigma_3
 =\partial(\sigma_2r_1),
\end{equation}
while the second gives
\begin{equation}
 -\sigma_2\sigma_1-[\sigma_1,\sigma_2]
 =\ii\sigma_3-2\ii\sigma_3
 =-\ii\sigma_3
 =\partial^*(\sigma_2r_1).
\end{equation}
This verifies both the commutator order and the relative sign in a case where
$Z$ does not commute with the differential basis.

Thus $\partial$ and $\partial^*$ obey the ordinary Leibniz rule when the
left factor commutes with every $\sigma_n$, in particular when it is a
complex scalar.  For a general paravector field, the commutator term is part
of the derivative and multiplied order cannot be rearranged.

\section{Space--time kinematics}
\label{sec:kinematics}

In this section, $X=t+\br\cdot\bsig$ is a real spacetime paravector and
$s$ is proper time along a time-like worldline.

\subsection{Intervals, velocity, and momentum}

Represent a differential event by the real paravector
\begin{gather}
  \dd X=\dd t+\dd\br\cdot\bsig\\
  \dd X^*=\dd t-\dd\br\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
The Minkowski interval is
\begin{equation}
 \dd s^2=\Det{\dd X}=\dd X\,\dd X^*
 =\dd t^2-\dd\br^2.
 \label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
For any invertible complex paravector $T$, define
\begin{equation}
 \dd X':=T^{-1}\,\dd X\,T.
\end{equation}
The cyclic determinant property gives
\begin{equation}
 \Det{\dd X'}
 =\Det{T^{-1}\,\dd X\,T}
 =\Det{\dd X\,T\,T^{-1}}
 =\Det{\dd X}
 =\dd s^2.
\end{equation}
Thus similarity by any such $T$ leaves the determinant unchanged.  This is
an algebraic change of basis; even when $T=L$, it is not the physical boost
of a space--time paravector.  The latter is the congruence
$\dd X'=L^{-1}\dd X L^{-1}$ developed in Section~\ref{sec:boosts}.  The alternative
similarity ordering $T\,\dd X\,T^{-1}$ has the same determinant invariance.
For a future-directed time-like interval, choose the positive scalar root
\begin{equation}
 \dd s:=\sqrt{\det(\dd X)}.
\end{equation}
The sign of the invariant squared interval classifies the causal relation
between the two neighboring events:
\begin{align}
 \dd s^2>0&:\quad\text{time-like; a massive particle can follow the interval},\\
 \dd s^2=0&:\quad\text{null; light can follow the interval},\\
 \dd s^2<0&:\quad\text{space-like; no causal signal can follow the interval}.
\end{align}
Only in the time-like case is \(\dd s\) real and nonzero under the chosen
positive-root convention; along a time-like worldline it is the proper-time
differential.
Thus the following kinematics explores the determinant of $\dd X$ and its
normalization by this scalar.  For two intervals,
\begin{equation}
 [\dd X,\dd X']=2\ii(\dd\br\times\dd\br')\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
When $\dd s^2\ne0$,
\begin{equation}
 \dd X^{-1}
 =\frac{\adj{\dd X}}{\det(\dd X)}
 =\frac{\dd X^*}{\dd s^2}.
\end{equation}
A null interval has $\dd t^2=\dd\br^2$ and no inverse.  Along a
future-directed time-like worldline, $s$ is proper time.  Define the
coordinate velocity by
\begin{equation}
 \bv:=\frac{\dd\br}{\dd t}.
\end{equation}
Then $\dd\br=\bv\dd t$, and the positive proper-time increment satisfies
\begin{gather}
 \dd s^2=(1-\bv^2)\dd t^2\\
 \dd s=\sqrt{1-\bv^2}\,\dd t\\
 \gamma:=\frac1{\sqrt{1-\bv^2}}\\
 \dd s=\frac{\dd t}{\gamma}\\
 \gamma=\frac{\dd t}{\dd s}.
\end{gather}
For any quantity evaluated along the worldline, the one-variable chain rule
therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd}{\dd s}
 =\frac{\dd t}{\dd s}\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
 =\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}.
\end{equation}
Applying this operator a second time gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd^2}{\dd s^2}
 =\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
  \left(\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\right)
 =\gamma^2\frac{\dd^2}{\dd t^2}
  +\gamma\frac{\dd\gamma}{\dd t}\frac{\dd}{\dd t}.
\end{equation}
In particular, the proper-velocity paravector is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd X}{\dd s}=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Using the scalar-scaling rule~\eqref{eq:det-scalar-scaling}, its determinant is
\begin{equation}
 \det\left(\frac{\dd X}{\dd s}\right)
 =\frac{\det(\dd X)}{\dd s^2}
 =1.
\end{equation}
Thus $\dd X/\dd s$ is a unit paravector in the sense of
equation~\eqref{eq:unit-paravector}.  We represent this proper velocity by
$U$, so
\begin{equation}
 U=\frac{\dd X}{\dd s}=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig).
 \label{eq:proper-velocity}
\end{equation}
In terms of $\gamma$ and $\bv$, the same determinant calculation is
\begin{equation}
 \det(U)
 =\det\left(\frac{\dd X}{\dd s}\right)
 =\frac{\det(\dd X)}{\dd s^2}
 =\gamma^2(1-\bv^2)
 =1.
\end{equation}
Since $U$ is real, its adjugate is its sigma conjugate, and therefore
\begin{equation}
 U^{-1}=\frac{\adj U}{\det(U)}=U^*
 =\gamma(1-\bv\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
This sigma-conjugate normalization is distinct from Hermitian unitarity;
proper velocity is not generally a unitary transformation.

Proper time therefore obeys
\begin{equation}
 \tau=\int\dd s
 =\int\sqrt{1-\bv^2}\,\dd t
 =\int\frac{\dd t}{\gamma}.
\end{equation}
For a massive particle with $m>0$, the four-momentum is the real paravector
\begin{equation}
 P:=mU=m\gamma+m\gamma\bv\cdot\bsig
 =E+\bp\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:momentum}
\end{equation}
Its scalar and vector parts define the energy and spatial momentum:
\begin{gather}
 E:=\scpart P=m\gamma\\
 \bp:=\vecpart P=m\gamma\bv\\
 \adj P=E-\bp\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
Taking its determinant gives the mass-shell relation
\begin{equation}
 \det(P)=m^2\det(U)=m^2
 =\det(E+\bp\cdot\bsig)=E^2-\bp^2,
\end{equation}
or $E^2=\bp^2+m^2$.  Since $m>0$, the momentum is invertible and
\begin{equation}
 P^{-1}=\frac{\adj P}{\det(P)}
 =\frac{E-\bp\cdot\bsig}{m^2}.
\end{equation}
A massless worldline is null and has no proper-time velocity $U$.  Its
four-momentum is therefore defined directly as a future-directed null
paravector $P=E+\bp\cdot\bsig$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
 \det(P)=E^2-\bp^2=0,
 \qquad E>0.
\end{equation}
It has no inverse.  Thus the same determinant gives the massive and massless
dispersion relations, while only the massive momentum is constructed as
$mU$.

\subsubsection{Proper acceleration}

Recall that $U=\dd X/\dd s=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig)$ is the proper
velocity.  The required coordinate quantities are
\begin{gather}
 \bv:=\frac{\dd\br}{\dd t}\\
 \ba:=\frac{\dd\bv}{\dd t}\\
 \gamma:=\frac{\dd t}{\dd s}.
\end{gather}
Differentiating $\gamma=(1-\bv^2)^{-1/2}$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \frac{\dd\gamma}{\dd t}
 &=-\tfrac12(1-\bv^2)^{-3/2}
   \frac{\dd(1-\bv^2)}{\dd t}\\
 &=-\tfrac12(1-\bv^2)^{-3/2}(-2\bv\cdot\ba)\\
 &=\gamma^3\bv\cdot\ba.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Hence the second proper-time derivative is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd^2}{\dd s^2}
 =\gamma^2\frac{\dd^2}{\dd t^2}
 +\gamma^4(\bv\cdot\ba)\frac{\dd}{\dd t}.
\end{equation}
Using $U=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig)$ gives the proper-acceleration paravector
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \frac{\dd U}{\dd s}
 &=\frac{\dd^2X}{\dd s^2}
 =\gamma\frac{\dd}{\dd t}
   \left(\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig)\right)\\
 &=\gamma\frac{\dd\gamma}{\dd t}(1+\bv\cdot\bsig)
   +\gamma^2\ba\cdot\bsig\\
 &=\gamma^4(\bv\cdot\ba)
   +\left(\gamma^4(\bv\cdot\ba)\bv+\gamma^2\ba\right)\cdot\bsig.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Differentiate the normalization $U^*U=1$:
\begin{equation}
 0=\frac{\dd(U^*U)}{\dd s}
 =\left(\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\right)^*U
 +U^*\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}.
\end{equation}
Sigma conjugation leaves the scalar part unchanged, and the scalar part is
cyclic, so the two scalar terms are equal.  Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \scpart{U^*\dd U/\dd s}=0.
\end{equation}
This is the paravector statement that four-velocity and four-acceleration are
Minkowski-orthogonal.  Since $U^{-1}=U^*$, right multiplication of the
differentiated normalization by $U^*$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \adj{\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}}
 =\left(\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\right)^*
 =-U^*\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}U^*.
\end{equation}
Here adjugation equals sigma conjugation because $\dd U/\dd s$ is a real
paravector.  Direct multiplication gives the scalar-free form
\begin{equation}
 U^*\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}
 =\gamma^3\left(\ba-\ii\bv\times\ba\right)\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:proper-acceleration-product}
\end{equation}
Its real vector contains the coordinate acceleration, while its imaginary
vector records the turning of the velocity direction.  Since
$\ba\cdot(\bv\times\ba)=0$, its square is scalar.  The adjugate identity
therefore yields the determinant without a separate component expansion:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \det\left(\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\right)
 &=\adj{\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}}\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\\
 &=-\left(U^*\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\right)^2\\
 &=-\gamma^6\left(\ba^2-(\bv\times\ba)^2\right)\\
 &=-\gamma^4\left(\ba^2+\gamma^2(\bv\cdot\ba)^2\right).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:proper-acceleration-determinant}
\end{equation}
The last step uses Lagrange's cross-product identity
$(\bv\times\ba)^2=\bv^2\ba^2-(\bv\cdot\ba)^2$, where each vector square
denotes its Euclidean dot product with itself.  Geometrically, this follows
from $\lVert\bv\times\ba\rVert=\lVert\bv\rVert\lVert\ba\rVert\sin\theta$
and $\bv\cdot\ba=\lVert\bv\rVert\lVert\ba\rVert\cos\theta$.  Together with
$1-\bv^2=\gamma^{-2}$, it shows that the proper acceleration is space-like
unless it vanishes and that $-\det(\dd U/\dd s)$ is its invariant squared
magnitude.

For $\bv\ne0$, resolve the coordinate acceleration parallel and perpendicular
to the velocity by
\begin{gather}
 \bu:=\frac{\bv}{\lVert\bv\rVert}\\
 \ba_{\parallel}:=(\ba\cdot\bu)\bu\\
 \ba_{\perp}:=\ba-\ba_{\parallel}.
\end{gather}
Since $(\bv\cdot\ba)^2=\bv^2\ba_{\parallel}^2$ and
$1+\gamma^2\bv^2=\gamma^2$,
\begin{equation}
 \det\left(\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\right)=-\gamma^4
 \left(\ba_{\perp}^2+\gamma^2\ba_{\parallel}^2\right).
\end{equation}
At $\bv=0$, this reduces continuously to
$\det(\dd U/\dd s)=-\ba^2$.

\subsection{Varying transformations and second differentials}
\label{sec:varying-transformations}

Recall $U=\dd X/\dd s$, and likewise write $U'=\dd X'/\dd s$.  Let $X$
be a paravector along a path and $T$ an invertible transformation
paravector that may vary along it.  Let $\dd$ denote successive
differentiation with respect to the path parameter and define
\begin{equation}
 \dd X'=T^{-1}\,\dd X\,T.
\end{equation}
Although $\det(\dd X')=\det(\dd X)$ at each point, a varying $T$ adds a
term to the second differential.  Define
\begin{gather}
 \dd\Theta:=T^{-1}\dd T\\
 \dd T^{-1}=-\dd\Theta\,T^{-1}.
\end{gather}
Direct differentiation gives
\begin{align}
 \dd^2X'
 &=\dd(T^{-1}\,\dd X\,T)\\
 &=T^{-1}\,\dd^2X\,T-\dd\Theta\,\dd X'+\dd X'\,\dd\Theta\\
 &=T^{-1}\,\dd^2X\,T+[\dd X',\dd\Theta].
\end{align}
Dividing by the corresponding powers of $\dd s$, define
\begin{equation}
 \Omega:=\frac{\dd\Theta}{\dd s}
 =T^{-1}\,\frac{\dd T}{\dd s},
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd U'}{\dd s}
 =T^{-1}\,\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\,T+[U',\Omega].
\end{equation}
For constant $T$, the connection term vanishes.  For varying $T$, it is the
algebraic source of the additional terms in rotating and accelerating
frames and is the pathwise prototype of a covariant derivative.

Here $\dd X'$ is defined by the pointwise transformation of the tangent.
When $T$ varies, it is not generally $\dd(T^{-1}XT)$, which also contains
the transformation of $X$ itself.  Moreover, a general complex $T$ can
make $\dd X'$ complex even when $\dd X$ is real, so a physical space--time
transformation requires an additional reality-preserving condition.

The corresponding local algebra basis is
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_n':=T^{-1}\sigma_nT.
\end{equation}
Similarity preserves all products, so the transformed generators obey the
same sigma algebra.  If $T$ varies along the path, direct differentiation
gives
\begin{gather}
 \dd\sigma_n'=[\sigma_n',\dd\Theta]\\
 \dd\Theta=T^{-1}\dd T.
\end{gather}
This equation describes the change of a local algebra basis.  It should not
be identified with a physical Lorentz transformation until the action of
$T$ and the condition preserving real space--time paravectors have both been
specified.  In particular, the complex boost-like terms obtained from a
completely general $T$ are connection coefficients, not automatically
components of a real physical velocity.

\subsection{Free-particle action}

Let $m$ be the particle mass, $\bv=\dd\br/\dd t$,
$\gamma=(1-\bv^2)^{-1/2}$, and $\dd s=\dd t/\gamma$.  The free-particle
action and Lagrangian are
\begin{gather}
 \int L\dd t=-m\int\dd s\\
 L=-m\sqrt{1-\bv^2}=-\frac m\gamma.
\end{gather}
For a general Lagrangian $L(t,\br,\bv)$,
\begin{gather}
 \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\partial_{\bv}L=\pdr L\\
 H=\bv\cdot\partial_{\bv}L-L\\
 \frac{\dd H}{\dd t}=-\partial_tL.
\end{gather}
Thus spatial translation invariance conserves canonical momentum and time
translation invariance conserves the Hamiltonian.
For the free-particle Lagrangian,
\begin{align}
 \partial_{\bv}L&=m\gamma\bv,\\
 H&=m\gamma\bv^2+\frac m\gamma
 =m\gamma(\bv^2+\gamma^{-2})=m\gamma.
\end{align}
At low velocity, the binomial expansions are
\begin{gather}
 \sqrt{1-\bv^2}=1-\tfrac12\bv^2+O(v^4)\\
 \gamma=1+\tfrac12\bv^2+O(v^4).
\end{gather}
Multiplying the first expansion by $-m$ gives
\begin{equation}
 L=-m\sqrt{1-\bv^2}
 =-m+\tfrac12m\bv^2+O(v^4),
\end{equation}
while the second gives
\begin{equation}
 H=m\gamma=m+\tfrac12m\bv^2+O(v^4).
\end{equation}
The exact relativistic kinetic energy is therefore
\begin{equation}
 K:=H-m=m(\gamma-1),
 \label{eq:kinetic-energy}
\end{equation}
and, apart from the constant rest-energy term, both expansions recover the
nonrelativistic kinetic energy $\tfrac12m\bv^2$.

\section{Electromagnetism}
\label{sec:electromagnetism}

Throughout this section,
$F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$ is the field,
$\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$ is the potential, and
$D=\rho+\bJ\cdot\bsig$ is the source.
For the particle equations, recall
$U=\dd X/\dd s=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig)$ from
equation~\eqref{eq:proper-velocity}.

\subsection{Field equation and potentials}

Recall the paravector differential operator from
equation~\eqref{eq:paravector-derivative}, and define the electromagnetic
field and source by
\begin{gather}
 \partial=\partial_t+\bsig\cdot\pdr\\
 F=(\bE+\bBf\ii)\cdot\bsig\\
 D=\rho+\bJ\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
All four Maxwell equations are the single equation
\begin{equation}
 \partial F=\sconj D=\rho-\bJ\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:maxwell}
\end{equation}
Sigma conjugation gives the equivalent equation
\begin{equation}
 \partial^*F^*=D.
\end{equation}
Expanding the left side by component gives
\begin{align}
 \partial F={}&\underbrace{\pdr\cdot\bE}_{\text{real scalar}}
 +\underbrace{\ii\pdr\cdot\bBf}_{\text{imaginary scalar}}\\
 &+\underbrace{(\partial_t\bE-\pdr\times\bBf)\cdot\bsig}
 _{\text{real vector}}\\
 &+\underbrace{\ii(\partial_t\bBf+\pdr\times\bE)\cdot\bsig}
 _{\text{imaginary vector}}.
\end{align}
Equating these four components with those of
$\rho-\bJ\cdot\bsig$ gives, respectively,
\begin{align}
 \text{real scalar:}\quad&\pdr\cdot\bE=\rho,\\
 \text{imaginary scalar:}\quad&\pdr\cdot\bBf=0,\\
 \text{real vector:}\quad&\pdr\times\bBf=\bJ+\partial_t\bE,\\
 \text{imaginary vector:}\quad&\pdr\times\bE=-\partial_t\bBf.
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dalembertian} gives
$\partial\partial^*=\partial^*\partial=\Box$, whereas
\begin{equation}
 \partial^2=\partial_t^2+\pdr\cdot\pdr
 +2\partial_t\,\bsig\cdot\pdr
 \ne\Box.
\end{equation}
Applying $\sconj\partial$ to~\eqref{eq:maxwell} gives
\begin{align}
 \Box F
 &=\sconj\partial\sconj D\\
 &=(\partial_t\rho+\pdr\cdot\bJ)
 +(-\partial_t\bJ-\pdr\rho+\ii\pdr\times\bJ)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}
The real-scalar component is charge conservation, and the real- and
imaginary-vector components are the electric and magnetic wave equations:
\begin{align}
 \text{real scalar:}\quad&\partial_t\rho+\pdr\cdot\bJ=0,\\
 \text{real vector:}\quad&\Box\bE=-\partial_t\bJ-\pdr\rho,\\
 \text{imaginary vector:}\quad&\Box\bBf=\pdr\times\bJ.
\end{align}
In vacuum, $\rho=0$ and $\bJ=0$, so
\begin{gather}
 \Box\bE=0\\
 \Box\bBf=0.
\end{gather}

For the potential $\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$,
\begin{align}
 \partial\Phi={}&
 \underbrace{\partial_tV+\pdr\cdot\bA}_{\text{real scalar}}
 +\underbrace{(\partial_t\bA+\pdr V)\cdot\bsig}_{\text{real vector}}\\
 &+\underbrace{\ii(\pdr\times\bA)\cdot\bsig}_{\text{imaginary vector}}.
\end{align}
Define the scalar component
\begin{equation}
 S:=\scpart{\partial\Phi}=\partial_tV+\pdr\cdot\bA.
\end{equation}
Then $\partial\Phi=S+F^*$: its real-vector and imaginary-vector components
give
\begin{gather}
 \bE=-\partial_t\bA-\pdr V\\
 \bBf=\pdr\times\bA.
\end{gather}
Also, $F=\partial^*\Phi^*-S$, so
\begin{align}
 \partial F
 &=\partial\partial^*\Phi^*-\partial S
 =\Box\Phi^*-\partial S\\
 &=(\Box V-\partial_tS)
 -(\Box\bA+\pdr S)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}
Comparing its real-scalar and real-vector components with
$\partial F=\rho-\bJ\cdot\bsig$ gives
\begin{align}
 \rho&=\Box V-\partial_tS
 \label{eq:sources-from-potential}
 \\
 \bJ&=\Box\bA+\pdr S.
 \label{eq:current-from-potential}
\end{align}
For a scalar field $\lambda$, the gauge transformation is
\begin{equation}
 \Phi' = \Phi+\sconj\partial\lambda,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \partial\Phi'=\partial\Phi+\partial\sconj\partial\lambda
 =\partial\Phi+\Box\lambda.
\end{equation}
Because $\Box\lambda$ is scalar, only the scalar component changes:
\begin{gather}
 S'=S+\Box\lambda\\
 \vecpart{\partial\Phi'}=\vecpart{\partial\Phi}.
\end{gather}
Thus $F$ is unchanged.  In the Lorenz gauge $S=0$, equation
\eqref{eq:sources-from-potential} reduces to
\begin{gather}
 \rho=\Box V\\
 \bJ=\Box\bA.
\end{gather}

\subsection{Field invariants}

Recall $F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$.
Electric and magnetic fields mix under Lorentz transformations, so their
individual magnitudes depend on the observer.  Two combinations remain the
same for every inertial observer and therefore classify the electromagnetic
field itself.

The scalar part of $F$ vanishes, and therefore $\adj F=-F$.  Hence
\begin{align}
 \Det F
 &=-F^2\\
 &=-(\bE+\bBf\ii)\cdot(\bE+\bBf\ii)\\
 &=-\bE^2+\bBf^2-2\ii\bE\cdot\bBf.
\end{align}
For any invertible real or complex paravector $T$, let
\begin{equation}
 F'=T^{-1}FT.
\end{equation}
The general similarity identity~\eqref{eq:det-similarity} gives
\begin{equation}
 \Det{F'}=\Det{T^{-1}FT}=\Det F.
\end{equation}
Rotations and boosts are the physical special cases
\begin{gather}
 F'=R^{-1}FR=R^{\mathsf H}FR\\
 F'=L^{-1}FL=L^*FL,
\end{gather}
because $R^{-1}=R^{\mathsf H}$ and $L^{-1}=L^*$.
Hence every inertial observer agrees on
\begin{align}
 a&=\bE^2-\bBf^2=-\repart{\Det F},\\
 b&=\bE\cdot\bBf=-\tfrac12\impart{\Det F}.
 \label{eq:field-invariants}
\end{align}
For nonzero fields, these invariants give the standard classification:
\begin{align}
 b=0&:\quad\text{electric-like when \(a\) is positive; some frame has no magnetic field},\\
 b=0&:\quad\text{magnetic-like when \(a\) is negative; some frame has no electric field},\\
 b\ne0&:\quad\text{no frame can eliminate either field.}
\end{align}
When both $a$ and $b$ vanish, the field is null or radiation-like: the
electric and magnetic fields are orthogonal and have equal magnitude.
Thus the invariants reveal physical properties that cannot be inferred from
one observer's values of $\bE$ and $\bBf$ alone.  Unlike the energy density
and flux below, they do not change with the observer.

\subsection{Lorentz force from the field and action}

Recall $F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$,
$\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$,
$U=\dd X/\dd s=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig)$,
$P=mU=E+\bp\cdot\bsig$, and $\bp=m\gamma\bv$, where $s$ is proper time,
$m>0$ is constant, and $q$ is the particle charge.
The Lorentz proper-force equation is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd P}{\dd s}=m\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}=q\repart{FU}
 =\frac q2\bigl(FU+U\rev F\bigr).
 \label{eq:lorentz}
\end{equation}
The Hermitian extraction removes the imaginary magnetic-dual components.
The remaining scalar and vector components are, respectively,
\begin{gather}
 \frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}=q\bv\cdot\bE\\
 \frac{\dd\bp}{\dd t}=q(\bE+\bv\times\bBf).
\end{gather}

The same result follows from the potential through the interacting-particle
action
\begin{align}
 \int L\dd t
 &=-m\int\dd s-q\int\scpart{\Phi^* U}\dd s\\
 &=\int\left(-\frac m\gamma
 -q\scpart{\Phi^* U/\gamma}\right)\dd t\\
 &=\int\left(-\frac m\gamma
 -q\scpart{(V-\bA\cdot\bsig)(1+\bv\cdot\bsig)}\right)\dd t\\
 &=\int\left(-\frac m\gamma-qV+q\bv\cdot\bA\right)\dd t.
\end{align}
Thus
\begin{gather}
 L=-\frac m\gamma-q(V-\bv\cdot\bA)\\
 \partial_{\bv}L=m\gamma\bv+q\bA=\bp+q\bA.
\end{gather}
The Euler--Lagrange terms are
\begin{align}
 \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\partial_{\bv}L
 &=\frac{\dd\bp}{\dd t}+q\partial_t\bA
   +q(\bv\cdot\pdr)\bA,\\
 \pdr L&=-q\pdr V+q\pdr(\bv\cdot\bA).
\end{align}
Using
$(\bv\cdot\pdr)\bA=\pdr(\bv\cdot\bA)
-\bv\times(\pdr\times\bA)$ gives
\begin{align}
 \frac{\dd\bp}{\dd t}
 &=-q\pdr V-q\partial_t\bA
   +q\bigl(\pdr(\bv\cdot\bA)-(\bv\cdot\pdr)\bA\bigr)\\
 &=q\bigl(-\pdr V-\partial_t\bA
          +\bv\times(\pdr\times\bA)\bigr)\\
 &=q(\bE+\bv\times\bBf).
\end{align}
The Hamiltonian follows with the velocity-dependent vector-potential terms
cancelling:
\begin{align}
 H&=\bv\cdot\partial_{\bv}L-L\\
  &=m\gamma\bv^2+q\bv\cdot\bA
    +\frac m\gamma+qV-q\bv\cdot\bA\\
 &=m\gamma(\bv^2+\gamma^{-2})+qV=m\gamma+qV.
\end{align}
This is the canonical energy and is gauge dependent under time-dependent
gauge changes; the kinetic energy $E=m\gamma$ is gauge invariant.  A
conserved canonical energy is obtained when the external potentials are time
independent in the chosen gauge.
Finally, $\dd H/\dd t=-\partial_tL$ gives
\begin{align}
 \frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}+q\partial_tV
 +q\bv\cdot\pdr V
 &=q\partial_tV-q\bv\cdot\partial_t\bA,\\
 \frac{\dd(m\gamma)}{\dd t}
 &=q\bv\cdot(-\pdr V-\partial_t\bA)
 =q\bv\cdot\bE.
\end{align}

To see equation~\eqref{eq:lorentz} componentwise, expand
\begin{equation}
 \frac{FU}{\gamma}=
 \bv\cdot\bE+\ii\bv\cdot\bBf
 +(\bE+\bv\times\bBf)\cdot\bsig
 +\ii(\bBf+\bE\times\bv)\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Taking the real paravector part removes the two imaginary components.

\subsection{Proper acceleration from the Lorentz force}
\label{sec:lorentz-proper-acceleration}

For a particle of constant mass $m>0$, recall
\begin{gather}
 \dd X=\dd t+\dd\br\cdot\bsig,\\
 U=\frac{\dd X}{\dd s}
 =\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig),\\
 P=mU=E+\bp\cdot\bsig,\\
 E=m\gamma,
 \qquad
 \bp=m\gamma\bv,\\
 \frac{\dd P}{\dd s}=m\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}.
\end{gather}
Also let $F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$ and
$\ba=\dd\bv/\dd t$.  Thus $\dd P/\dd s$ is the proper-force paravector,
while $\dd U/\dd s$ is the proper-acceleration paravector.

The Lorentz four-force is automatically orthogonal to the four-velocity.
Since $U^*U=UU^*=1$, cyclicity of the scalar part and
$\scpart F=\scpart{F^{\mathsf H}}=0$ give
\begin{align}
 \scpart{U^*\dd P/\dd s}
 &=q\scpart{U^*\repart{FU}}\\
 &=\frac q2\scpart{U^*FU+U^*UF^{\mathsf H}}\\
 &=\frac q2\left(\scpart F+\scpart{F^{\mathsf H}}\right)
 =0.
\end{align}
Because $\dd P/\dd s=m\dd U/\dd s$, this is equivalently
\begin{equation}
 \scpart{U^*\dd P/\dd s}
 =m\scpart{U^*\dd U/\dd s}=0,
\end{equation}
in agreement with the proper-acceleration development above.

The earlier kinematic result for $\dd U/\dd s$, multiplied by $m$, gives
\begin{align}
 \frac{\dd P}{\dd s}
 &=\gamma\left(
   \frac{\dd E}{\dd t}
   +\frac{\dd\bp}{\dd t}\cdot\bsig\right)\\
 &=m\gamma^4(\bv\cdot\ba)
 +m\left(\gamma^2\ba
 +\gamma^4(\bv\cdot\ba)\bv\right)\cdot\bsig.
\end{align}
The real paravector part of the field product is
\begin{equation}
 q\repart{FU}
 =q\gamma\left(
 \bv\cdot\bE
 +(\bE+\bv\times\bBf)\cdot\bsig\right).
\end{equation}
Equating the scalar and vector parts of
$\dd P/\dd s=q\repart{FU}$ and dividing by $\gamma$ gives
\begin{align}
 \frac{\dd E}{\dd t}
 &=m\gamma^3\bv\cdot\ba
 =q\bv\cdot\bE,\\
 \frac{\dd\bp}{\dd t}
 &=m\gamma\left(\ba+\gamma^2(\bv\cdot\ba)\bv\right)
 =q(\bE+\bv\times\bBf).
\end{align}
Eliminating $\bv\cdot\ba$ between these two equations yields
\begin{equation}
 \ba=\frac{q}{m\gamma}
 \left(\bE+\bv\times\bBf-(\bv\cdot\bE)\bv\right).
 \label{eq:lorentz-coordinate-acceleration}
\end{equation}
The magnetic term is perpendicular to $\bv$ and therefore changes momentum
without contributing to the coordinate-time power
$\dd E/\dd t=q\bv\cdot\bE$.

The scalar and vector parts of the proper force give its invariant squared
magnitude directly:
\begin{equation}
 -\det\left(\frac{\dd P}{\dd s}\right)
 =q^2\gamma^2
 \left((\bE+\bv\times\bBf)^2-(\bv\cdot\bE)^2\right).
\end{equation}
Since $\dd P/\dd s=m\dd U/\dd s$, the scalar-scaling rule gives
\begin{equation}
 \det\left(\frac{\dd P}{\dd s}\right)
 =m^2\det\left(\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\right).
\end{equation}
Therefore the corresponding invariant squared proper-acceleration magnitude
is
\begin{equation}
 -\det\left(\frac{\dd U}{\dd s}\right)
 =\frac{q^2}{m^2}\gamma^2
 \left((\bE+\bv\times\bBf)^2-(\bv\cdot\bE)^2\right).
 \label{eq:lorentz-proper-acceleration}
\end{equation}
The factor
$\gamma^2((\bE+\bv\times\bBf)^2-(\bv\cdot\bE)^2)$ is the squared
electric-field magnitude in the particle's instantaneous rest frame.  Thus
$\dd P/\dd s$ is the space-like proper force required by the fixed mass shell
$P^*P=m^2$; division by $m$ gives the proper acceleration required by
$U^*U=1$.

\subsection{Energy balance}

Since
\begin{gather}
 F=(\bE+\bBf\ii)\cdot\bsig\\
 F^{\mathsf H}=(\bE-\bBf\ii)\cdot\bsig,
\end{gather}
their ordered product is
\begin{equation}
 FF^{\mathsf H}
 =\bE^2+\bBf^2+2(\bE\times\bBf)\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Therefore the standard electromagnetic energy density $u$ and Poynting
vector $\bS$ are the scalar and vector coefficients of
$\tfrac12FF^{\mathsf H}$:
\begin{align}
 u
 &:=\tfrac12(\bE^2+\bBf^2)
 =\tfrac12\scpart{FF^{\mathsf H}},\\
 \bS
 &:=\bE\times\bBf
 =\tfrac12\vecpart{FF^{\mathsf H}}.
\end{align}
These quantities describe energy measured in the chosen frame; they are not
the two Lorentz invariants contained in $F^2$.  The product
$\tfrac12FF^{\mathsf H}$ packages energy density and energy flux into a
paravector, but is not by itself the full electromagnetic stress--energy
tensor.  The full tensor is represented without indices by the linear map
\begin{equation}
 T(N):=\tfrac12FN F^{\mathsf H},
 \label{eq:electromagnetic-stress-map}
\end{equation}
where $N$ is a real paravector.  In the frame whose time direction is $N=1$,
$T(1)=u+\bS\cdot\bsig$.  If
$F'=L^{-1}FL$ and $N'=L^{-1}NL^{-1}$, then
\begin{equation}
 T'(N')=L^{-1}T(N)L^{-1},
\end{equation}
so every energy--momentum flux paravector has the correct four-vector law.
Its time-direction value is future causal because
\begin{equation}
 \det(T(1))=u^2-\bS^2
 =\tfrac14\lVert\det(F)\rVert^2\geq0.
 \label{eq:electromagnetic-energy-causal}
\end{equation}
Equality holds for a null radiation field.  Thus the field invariants,
energy density, and Poynting flux meet in one determinant identity.
This product order gives the conventional sign of the Poynting vector
directly.  For comparison, reversing the order preserves the scalar
coefficient and changes the sign of the vector coefficient:
\begin{gather}
 \scpart{F^{\mathsf H}F}=\scpart{FF^{\mathsf H}}\\
 \vecpart{F^{\mathsf H}F}=-\vecpart{FF^{\mathsf H}}.
\end{gather}
Starting only from the paravector field equation $\partial F=D^*$, its
real-vector and imaginary-vector components give
\begin{gather}
 \partial_t\bE=\pdr\times\bBf-\bJ\\
 \partial_t\bBf=-\pdr\times\bE.
\end{gather}
Taking their dot products with $\bE$ and $\bBf$, respectively, yields
\begin{align}
 \partial_tu
 &=\bE\cdot\partial_t\bE+\bBf\cdot\partial_t\bBf\\
 &=\bE\cdot(\pdr\times\bBf)-\bE\cdot\bJ
   -\bBf\cdot(\pdr\times\bE)\\
 &=-\pdr\cdot\bS-\bJ\cdot\bE,
\end{align}
where
$\pdr\cdot(\bE\times\bBf)
=\bBf\cdot(\pdr\times\bE)-\bE\cdot(\pdr\times\bBf)$.
Substitution into $\partial_tu$ therefore derives Poynting's theorem, the
local electromagnetic energy-balance law:
\begin{equation}
 \partial_tu
 +\pdr\cdot\bS=-\bJ\cdot\bE.
 \label{eq:poynting}
\end{equation}
Here $\bJ\cdot\bE$ is the power density transferred from the field to
matter; the minus sign is the corresponding loss of field energy.
Equivalently, in paravector coefficients,
\begin{equation}
 \tfrac12\partial_t\scpart{FF^{\mathsf H}}
 +\tfrac12\pdr\cdot\vecpart{FF^{\mathsf H}}
 =-\bJ\cdot\bE.
\end{equation}

\section{Relativistic wave equations}
\label{sec:waves}

Recall that $U=\dd X/\dd s$ for a classical massive particle.  For a free
massive future mass-shell momentum, the corresponding normalized unit is
$U=P/m$.

In this section, $\psi$ denotes a scalar or paravector amplitude as stated
locally, $\bPsi$ denotes a two-paravector Dirac block, $m$ is the mass, and
$q$ is the charge.  Three uses of the algebra must be kept distinct.  Real
space--time quantities such as $X$, $P$, and a conserved current transform as
four-vectors.  Scalar and pure-vector fields carry spin zero and spin one
through their field transformation laws.  A spin-one-half state is instead a
minimal-ideal amplitude with a one-sided rotation law; a Dirac state contains
two such amplitudes with opposite boost laws.  The algebra element alone does
not determine the spin---its transformation law does.

The wave equations below describe classical fields and, where the conserved
density is positive, one-particle wave mechanics in a prescribed
electromagnetic field.  The negative-frequency solutions required by the
relativistic equations are also displayed.  Their interpretation as
positive-energy antiparticles is stated separately because that step belongs
to charge conjugation and field quantization, not to a negative probability.

\subsection{Paravector inner products and expectations}
\label{sec:paravector-inner-products}

For finite paravector amplitudes $\psi,\psi'$ and an algebraic paravector
$Z$, define the local pairing and matrix element intrinsically by scalar
extraction:
\begin{align}
 \langle\psi'\mid\psi\rangle
 &:=\scpart{\psi'^{\mathsf H}\psi},
 \label{eq:paravector-inner-product}\\
 \langle\psi'\mid Z\mid\psi\rangle
 &:=\scpart{\psi'^{\mathsf H}Z\psi}.
 \label{eq:paravector-matrix-element}
\end{align}
The first is the $Z=1$ case of the second, and
$\langle\psi\mid\psi\rangle=\lVert\psi\rVert^2$.  In particular,
\begin{equation}
 n:=\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}\geq0
\end{equation}
is the local norm density.  A finite spin amplitude is normalized by $n=1$,
not by $\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi=1$.  For spatial wavefunctions the same pairing
means
\begin{align}
 \langle\psi'\mid\psi\rangle
 &:=\int\scpart{\psi'^{\mathsf H}\psi}\,\dd^3\br,\\
 \langle\psi'\mid Z\mid\psi\rangle
 &:=\int\scpart{\psi'^{\mathsf H}Z\psi}\,\dd^3\br.
\end{align}
Thus wavefunction normalization is $\int n\,\dd^3\br=1$ whenever this
positive pairing is the conserved norm, as it is for Pauli and Dirac
amplitudes.  The Klein--Gordon scalar has a different conserved current and
is treated separately below.
For an observable $Z^{\mathsf H}=Z$ acting from the left,
\begin{equation}
 \langle Z\rangle_\psi
 :=\frac{\langle\psi\mid Z\mid\psi\rangle}
 {\langle\psi\mid\psi\rangle}.
 \label{eq:paravector-expectation}
\end{equation}
For a finite amplitude this is the local ratio
\begin{equation}
 \langle Z\rangle_\psi
 =\frac{\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}Z\psi}}
 {\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}},
\end{equation}
whereas for a spatial wavefunction both scalar pairings are integrated.
For normalized $\psi$, this reduces to
$\langle Z\rangle_\psi=\langle\psi\mid Z\mid\psi\rangle$.
If an observable contains derivatives, those derivatives act on the
amplitude to their right.  Hermiticity is then a statement about the
integrated pairing and follows by integration by parts with suitable boundary
conditions; it is not obtained by applying $^{\mathsf H}$ only to the
displayed algebra coefficient.
For unrestricted paravectors, the local pairing is one half of the usual
Hilbert--Schmidt pairing of their matrix images.  A single Pauli spinor is
obtained only after restricting the amplitude to a minimal left ideal as
described next.  Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-spinors}
then shows that these definitions become the standard two-component column
formulas.

For a paravector $T$ with $\psi'=T\psi$, a finite amplitude obeys
\begin{equation}
 \langle Z\rangle_{\psi'}
 =\frac{\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}T^{\mathsf H}ZT\psi}}
 {\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}T^{\mathsf H}T\psi}}.
 \label{eq:bilinear-congruence}
\end{equation}
For a spatial wavefunction, integrate the scalar numerator and denominator
before taking their ratio.
If $T^{\mathsf H}T=1$ and $\langle\psi\mid\psi\rangle=1$, then the
denominator is unity and
\begin{equation}
 \langle Z\rangle_{\psi'}
 =\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}T^{\mathsf H}ZT\psi}.
\end{equation}
Thus a unitary rotation preserves the normalization.  A finite boost is not
unitary, so this positive equal-time norm is not itself Lorentz invariant;
the covariant Dirac normalization instead comes from the conserved Dirac
current on a spacelike hypersurface.

\subsection{Physical spinors and minimal ideals}
\label{sec:physical-spinors}

In this subsection, $\Pi_3$ is the fixed column idempotent
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_3:=\tfrac12(1+\sigma_3).
\end{equation}
It satisfies $\Pi_3^2=\Pi_3=\Pi_3^{\mathsf H}$ and is held fixed; it selects a column
representation when it multiplies from the right.  When the same element
acts from the left it is also the positive-$\sigma_3$ measurement projector;
the side of multiplication distinguishes these two roles.  A physical
two-component Pauli amplitude has the form
\begin{equation}
 \psi=Z\Pi_3,
 \qquad \psi\Pi_3=\psi,
\end{equation}
where
$Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, with $S\in\C$ and $\bV\in\C^3$, is an unrestricted
complex paravector, not a scalar.  The representative $Z$ is not
unique; the product $Z\Pi_3$ contains the two complex components of $\psi$.
Let $Z'=S'+\bV'\cdot\bsig$, with $S'\in\C$ and $\bV'\in\C^3$, be any
unrestricted complex paravector.  These amplitudes form a minimal left ideal
because every such left action remains in the same set:
\begin{equation}
 (Z'\psi)\Pi_3=Z'(\psi\Pi_3)=Z'\psi.
\end{equation}
Right multiplication by the fixed $\Pi_3$ is therefore essential.  Left
multiplication by $\Pi_3$ would instead produce a right ideal, which is not
preserved by general observables acting from the left.

A normalized basis of this ideal is
\begin{equation}
 e_+:=\sqrt2\Pi_3,
 \qquad
 e_-:=\sqrt2\sigma_1\Pi_3.
 \label{eq:ideal-spin-basis}
\end{equation}
Direct use of the algebra operations gives
\begin{gather}
 \scpart{e_+^{\mathsf H}e_+}
 =\scpart{e_-^{\mathsf H}e_-}=1,\\
 \scpart{e_+^{\mathsf H}e_-}=0,\\
 \sigma_3e_+=e_+,
 \qquad
 \sigma_3e_-=-e_-.
\end{gather}
Every ideal amplitude can therefore be written with two complex scalars as
\begin{equation}
 \psi=c_+e_++c_-e_-.
\end{equation}
For a $\sigma_3$ measurement its two probabilities are simply
\begin{equation}
 p_+=\frac{\lVert c_+\rVert^2}
 {\lVert c_+\rVert^2+\lVert c_-\rVert^2},
 \qquad
 p_-=\frac{\lVert c_-\rVert^2}
 {\lVert c_+\rVert^2+\lVert c_-\rVert^2}.
\end{equation}
Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-spinors} maps $e_+$ and $e_-$ to the standard
columns $\{1,0\}^{\mathsf T}$ and $\{0,1\}^{\mathsf T}$.

For a physical spin measurement, let $\bu\in\R^3$ be a unit direction and
use the projectors from Section~\ref{sec:algebra-projectors},
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_\pm:=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
They satisfy the identities listed there, including
$\Pi_\pm^2=\Pi_\pm$, $\Pi_+\Pi_-=0$, and
$\Pi_\pm^{\mathsf H}=\Pi_\pm$.  They act from the left and give
\begin{equation}
 \psi_\pm:=\Pi_\pm\psi.
\end{equation}
The algebra element $\bu\cdot\bsig$ has eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$.  In
natural units the physical spin component is one half of this element, so
spin up and spin down mean respectively
\begin{align}
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)\psi&=\psi,
 &\Pi_+\psi&=\psi,
 &\Pi_-\psi&=0,\\
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)\psi&=-\psi,
 &\Pi_-\psi&=\psi,
 &\Pi_+\psi&=0.
\end{align}
The corresponding measured spin values are $+\tfrac12$ and $-\tfrac12$.
Because $\psi_\pm\Pi_3=\psi_\pm$, both outcomes remain in the same fixed
minimal left ideal.  Hermitian conjugation reverses the multiplied order, so
their norms and observable matrix elements are
\begin{align}
 \langle\psi_\pm\mid\psi_\pm\rangle
 &=\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm\psi}
 =\langle\psi\mid\Pi_\pm\mid\psi\rangle,\\
 \langle\psi_\pm\mid Z'\mid\psi_\pm\rangle
 &=\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm^{\mathsf H}Z'\Pi_\pm\psi}
 =\langle\psi\mid\Pi_\pm Z'\Pi_\pm\mid\psi\rangle.
\end{align}

The spin direction of a finite amplitude also has a compact intrinsic form.
For nonzero $\psi$, define the normalized Hermitian paravector
\begin{equation}
 B:=\frac{\psi\psi^{\mathsf H}}
 {\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}}
 =1+\bm{s}\cdot\bsig,
 \qquad
 \bm{s}:=\vecpart B.
 \label{eq:spin-state-paravector}
\end{equation}
It obeys $B^{\mathsf H}=B$ and $\scpart B=1$.  Multiplicativity gives the
entirely intrinsic identity
\begin{equation}
 \det(B)
 =\frac{\det(\psi)\det(\psi^{\mathsf H})}{n^2}
 =\frac{\lVert\det(\psi)\rVert^2}{n^2}
 =1-\bm{s}^2\geq0.
\end{equation}
A nonzero minimal-ideal amplitude has $\det(\psi)=0$, so
$\bm{s}^2=1$ and $\bm{s}$ is the unit Bloch direction of a pure Pauli state.
For an unrestricted amplitude, $\bm{s}^2\leq1$ and $B$ can also represent a
mixed state.  Thus pure states lie on the null boundary $\det(B)=0$, mixed
states lie inside it, and the Bloch ball is the unit-scalar slice of this
paravector cone.  This is state geometry, not the particle's spacetime
momentum.  In Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-spinors}, the matrix image of $B/2$
is the conventional normalized density matrix.  The two outcome
probabilities are already contained in $B$:
\begin{equation}
 p_\pm(\bu)=\scpart{\Pi_\pm B}
 =\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bm{s}).
 \label{eq:spin-probability-intrinsic}
\end{equation}
For a spatial Pauli wavefunction, the directly computable global
probabilities are instead the density-weighted integrals
\begin{equation}
 p_\pm(\bu)=
 \frac{\displaystyle\int
  \scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm\psi}\,\dd^3\br}
 {\displaystyle\int
  \scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}\,\dd^3\br}.
 \label{eq:integrated-pauli-spin-probability}
\end{equation}
More generally, let $Z'=a+\bb\cdot\bsig$ be Hermitian, with $a\in\R$,
$\bb\in\R^3$, and $\bb\ne0$, and take
$\bu=\bb/\lVert\bb\rVert$.  Its spectral form and expectation are
\begin{align}
 Z'&=(a+\lVert\bb\rVert)\Pi_+
   +(a-\lVert\bb\rVert)\Pi_-,\\
 \langle Z'\rangle_\psi
 &=\scpart{Z'B}=a+\bb\cdot\bm{s}\\
 &=(a+\lVert\bb\rVert)p_+(\bu)
  +(a-\lVert\bb\rVert)p_-(\bu).
\end{align}
Thus the same two projectors give the complete measurement rule for every
Hermitian paravector observable.  Cyclicity also gives the adjoint relation
\begin{equation}
 \langle\psi'\mid Z'\psi\rangle
 =\langle Z'^{\mathsf H}\psi'\mid\psi\rangle,
\end{equation}
so left multiplication by $Z'$ has adjoint left multiplication by
$Z'^{\mathsf H}$.
Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-spinors} shows that the fixed right projection gives
one conventional two-component Pauli column, while the left projections give
its ordinary spin-measurement components.  Applying the same fixed right
projection to both entries of the Dirac block gives the conventional four
complex components.

\subsection{Spin carried by paravector components}
\label{sec:paravector-spin-content}

Here $R$ is the rotation rotor defined in
Section~\ref{sec:transformations}, $a$ is a complex scalar amplitude,
$\psi\Pi_3=\psi$ is a minimal-ideal amplitude, and
$Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$ is an unrestricted complex paravector amplitude.
Spin is determined by how an amplitude transforms under rotations, not merely
by how many coefficients are used to write it.  The scalar, minimal-ideal,
and vector carriers transform differently.

A complex scalar amplitude has no spatial direction and is unchanged by the
rotor:
\begin{equation}
 a'=a.
\end{equation}
It therefore carries spin zero.  The Klein--Gordon field below is of this
type; its single complex coefficient is proportional to the algebra identity.

A minimal-ideal amplitude contains two complex coefficients.  Its
associated Pauli column transforms by one-sided rotor multiplication, which
is represented intrinsically by
\begin{equation}
 \psi'=R^{\mathsf H}\psi.
\end{equation}
This choice gives the same active rotation as the vector sandwich
$R^{\mathsf H}ZR$ used below.  It also preserves the fixed ideal because
$(R^{\mathsf H}\psi)\Pi_3=R^{\mathsf H}\psi$.  Since $R$ contains the
half-angle, a full $2\pi$ rotation gives
\begin{equation}
 \psi'=-\psi.
\end{equation}
This is the spin-one-half carrier.  A Dirac amplitude consists of two such
carriers coupled by the mass term; the pair still describes spin one-half,
not spin zero plus spin one.

A pure vector amplitude $\bV\cdot\bsig$ transforms by the two-sided sandwich
\begin{equation}
 \bV'\cdot\bsig
 =R^{\mathsf H}(\bV\cdot\bsig)R.
\end{equation}
The two rotor signs cancel under a $2\pi$ rotation, so this three-component
carrier returns to itself and carries spin one.  The electromagnetic field
$F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$ is a complex pure vector of this type.  The full
real Maxwell field has the two physical helicities.  After a frequency split,
the self-dual field $F$ carries one helicity on a positive-frequency branch,
while its Hermitian-conjugate sector supplies the other.  The three algebraic
vector coefficients should not be confused with three independent photon
polarizations.  The classical
Maxwell field carries energy density and Poynting flux as derived in
Section~\ref{sec:electromagnetism}; it is not by itself a normalized
single-photon position wavefunction.  More generally, a pure vector has the
rotation content of spin one but is not automatically a physical particle
field; a massive spin-one theory additionally requires its field equation
and constraint and has three physical polarizations.

Consequently, under the two-sided sandwich an unrestricted paravector
$Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$ is reducible: its scalar component carries spin zero and
its vector component carries spin one.  The transformation law is essential
to this statement.  Under one-sided multiplication, the matrix image of an
unrestricted paravector consists of two Pauli columns and carries two copies
of the spin-one-half representation.  The fixed right projection selects one
of those copies; it does not select scalar and vector coefficients.

Under Lorentz boosts the distinction becomes sharper.  A scalar remains a
scalar, the electromagnetic pure vector obeys $F'=L^{-1}FL$, and the two
minimal-ideal entries of a Dirac state use the opposite factors $L$ and
$L^{-1}$.  When $m=0$ the two Dirac equations decouple into the two chiral
spin-one-half equations.  For a positive-energy massless mode with
$\bu=\bp/\lVert\bp\rVert$, the free equation gives
\begin{equation}
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)\Psi_1=-\Psi_1,
 \qquad
 (\bu\cdot\bsig)\Psi_2=\Psi_2.
\end{equation}
Thus the two entries become opposite helicities in the massless limit.  They
are not, by themselves, the spin-up/down or particle/antiparticle entries.
The mass term couples them when $m\ne0$.

\subsection{Energy and momentum as differential operators}
\label{sec:quantum-energy-momentum}

Recall the real space--time paravector $X=t+\br\cdot\bsig$ and the real
four-momentum $P=E+\bp\cdot\bsig$.  Their invariant scalar pairing is
\begin{equation}
 f(X):=\scpart{\adj P\,X}=Et-\bp\cdot\br.
\end{equation}
The corresponding scalar plane wave is
\begin{equation}
 w(X):=\exp(-\ii f(X))
 =\exp(-\ii Et+\ii\bp\cdot\br).
\end{equation}
Its response to time and space translations gives
\begin{gather}
 \ii\partial_t w=Ew,\\
 -\ii\pdr w=\bp w.
\end{gather}
Thus energy and momentum are not separate substitutions added to the
algebra: they are the scalar and vector coefficients of one paravector
differential eigenvalue.  Indeed,
\begin{align}
 \scpart{\ii\partial^*}&=\ii\partial_t,
 &\vecpart{\ii\partial^*}&=-\ii\pdr,\\
 \ii\partial^*w&=P w,
 &\ii\partial w&=\adj P\,w.
 \label{eq:momentum-operator-eigenvalues}
\end{align}
Here coefficient extraction from a differential paravector is understood
before it acts.  On the spatially integrated pairing, the three components
of $-\ii\pdr$ are Hermitian when boundary terms vanish.  The reality of an
energy spectrum follows after the wave equation has been put in the
Hermitian evolution form $\ii\partial_t\bPsi=H\bPsi$.

The particle action gives canonical momentum $\bp+q\bA$ and canonical
energy $E+qV$.  Identifying these translation generators with
$-\ii\pdr$ and $\ii\partial_t$ respectively gives the kinetic operators
below and directly links minimal coupling to the classical action.
In an electromagnetic potential the gauge-covariant, or kinetic, operators
are
\begin{align}
 E&:=\ii\partial_t-qV,
 &\bp&:=-\ii\pdr-q\bA,\\
 E+\bp\cdot\bsig&=\ii\partial^*-q\Phi,
 &E-\bp\cdot\bsig&=\ii\partial-q\Phi^*.
 \label{eq:covariant-momentum-paravectors}
\end{align}
The last two expressions have the same coefficient pattern as $P$ and
$\adj P$.  They are nevertheless differential operators.  In a field their
components do not commute, so their ordered products must be retained; one
may not replace such a product by the ordinary paravector determinant.  For
the free operators all components commute and
\begin{equation}
 (\ii\partial)(\ii\partial^*)
 =(\ii\partial^*)(\ii\partial)=-\Box.
 \label{eq:free-momentum-product}
\end{equation}
The electromagnetic remainder produced when the covariant orderings are
interchanged is derived explicitly below in the interacting factorization.

\subsection{Klein--Gordon equation}

Let $\psi$ be a complex scalar amplitude.  For a free momentum
$P=E+\bp\cdot\bsig$, the mass shell is the single algebraic statement
\begin{equation}
 \det(P)=m^2.
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:momentum-operator-eigenvalues} and
\eqref{eq:free-momentum-product} turn this determinant polynomial into
\begin{equation}
 \left((\ii\partial)(\ii\partial^*)-m^2\right)\psi
 =-(\Box+m^2)\psi=0.
\end{equation}
Thus the Klein--Gordon equation is
\begin{equation}
 (\Box+m^2)\psi=0.
 \label{eq:kg}
\end{equation}

In an electromagnetic potential use the covariant operators in
equation~\eqref{eq:covariant-momentum-paravectors}.  The spin-zero equation
is the scalar part of either ordered paravector product:
\begin{equation}
 \left(-E^2+\bp^2+m^2\right)\psi
 =\left(-\scpart{(E-\bp\cdot\bsig)
                         (E+\bp\cdot\bsig)}+m^2\right)\psi=0.
 \label{eq:kg-field}
\end{equation}
The vector part is not discarded accidentally: it is absent from the scalar
equation but becomes the electromagnetic spin coupling when the Dirac
operator is squared.

For $m>0$, the conserved Klein--Gordon current has the intrinsic
paravector form
\begin{equation}
 J:=\frac1m\repart{\psi^{\mathsf H}
       (E+\bp\cdot\bsig)\psi}
 =j+\bm j\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:kg-current}
\end{equation}
The covariant equation and its Hermitian form give
\begin{equation}
 \scpart{\partial J}
 =\partial_tj+\pdr\cdot\bm j=0.
\end{equation}
For the free branches $\exp(-\ii f)$ and $\exp(+\ii f)$, respectively,
\begin{equation}
 J=+\frac{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}{m}P,
 \qquad
 J=-\frac{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}{m}P.
\end{equation}
Its time component is therefore positive on the positive-frequency branch
and negative on the negative-frequency branch.  Consequently $j$ is a
signed conserved density and
$qj$ is the electric charge density; $j$ is not a positive probability
density on an arbitrary Klein--Gordon solution.  A one-particle probability
interpretation requires restriction to a positive-frequency sector, where
the integrated Klein--Gordon pairing is positive; its local density need not
be pointwise positive for a superposition.  The complete particle--antiparticle
interpretation is the quantized scalar field.  This is why the positive
pairing used for Pauli and Dirac wavefunctions was not assigned to the
relativistic scalar field.

\subsection{Spin-zero low-energy limit}

Let $E=\ii\partial_t-qV$ and $\bp=-\ii\pdr-q\bA$, with
$\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$, and denote the relativistic scalar solution by
$\psi'$.  Separate its rest-energy phase by
$\psi'=\exp(-\ii mt)\psi$.  The exact equation for the slowly varying
amplitude becomes
\begin{gather}
 (-E^2-2mE+\bp^2)\psi=0\\
 E\psi=\frac{\bp^2-E^2}{2m}\psi.
\end{gather}
For a free positive-frequency particle the operators commute and the exact
residual-energy branch is
\begin{equation}
 E=\sqrt{\bp^2+m^2}-m.
\end{equation}
Its expansion gives
\begin{equation}
 E=\frac{\bp^2}{2m}-\frac{\bp^4}{8m^3}+\cdots.
\end{equation}
More generally, neglecting $E^2$ at leading nonrelativistic order in the
covariant equation gives the Schr\"odinger equation
\begin{equation}
 \ii\partial_t\psi=\left(\frac{(-\ii\pdr-q\bA)^2}{2m}+qV\right)\psi.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Dirac equation in block form}

Let $Z$ be a paravector, $\partial=\partial_t+\bsig\cdot\pdr$, and
$\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$ when a field is present.  Here
$\bPsi=\{\Psi_1,\Psi_2\}^{\mathsf T}$ is a two-entry block whose entries
belong to the same fixed minimal left ideal:
\begin{equation}
 \Psi_1\Pi_3=\Psi_1,
 \qquad
 \Psi_2\Pi_3=\Psi_2.
\end{equation}
The existing Hermitian operation extends to blocks by transposing the block
and applying $^{\mathsf H}$ to each entry.  Thus, if a block has entries
$Z_{nm}$, then its Hermitian block has entries $Z_{mn}^{\mathsf H}$.  This is
only the block extension of the operation already defined in
Section~\ref{sec:conjugate-operators}.  Sigma conjugation of a block acts
entrywise without transposing the block.  The componentwise Dirac pairing is
\begin{equation}
 \langle\bPsi'\mid\bPsi\rangle
 :=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\scpart{\Psi_k'^{\mathsf H}\Psi_k},
 \label{eq:dirac-inner-product}
\end{equation}
with a spatial integral for wavefunctions.  The corresponding local Dirac
norm density is
\begin{equation}
 \lVert\bPsi\rVert^2
 =\scpart{\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}\Psi_1}
 +\scpart{\Psi_2^{\mathsf H}\Psi_2}.
 \label{eq:dirac-norm}
\end{equation}
Define
\begin{equation}
 \Wop(Z):=
 \begin{Bmatrix}0&Z\\ \adj Z&0\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
It satisfies
\begin{gather}
 \Wop(Z)^2=
 \begin{Bmatrix}
  Z\adj Z&0\\
  0&\adj Z\,Z
 \end{Bmatrix}
 =\det(Z)\bI\\
 \Wop(Z)^*=\Wop(Z^*).
\end{gather}
For the free field, define the Dirac operator and its mass conjugate:
\begin{gather}
 \bDm:=\Wop(\ii\partial)-m\bI\\
 \bDm':=\Wop(\ii\partial)+m\bI.
\end{gather}
The Dirac equation is
\begin{equation}
 \bDm\bPsi=0.
 \label{eq:dirac}
\end{equation}
Since $\Wop(\ii\partial)^2=-\Box\bI$, multiplication by the mass conjugate
gives
\begin{equation}
 \bDm'\bDm
 =\Wop(\ii\partial)^2-m^2\bI
 =-(\Box+m^2)\bI.
\end{equation}
Consequently, $\bDm\bPsi=0$ implies
$(\Box+m^2)\bPsi=0$, so every component of the Dirac block satisfies the
Klein--Gordon equation.  This factorization is the direct connection between
the first-order Dirac equation and the relativistic mass-shell equation.  For
$\bPsi=\{\Psi_1,\Psi_2\}^{\mathsf T}$ it reads
\begin{gather}
 \ii\partial\Psi_2-m\Psi_1=0\\
 \ii\sconj\partial\,\Psi_1-m\Psi_2=0.
\end{gather}
Conversely, for $m\ne0$, any scalar solution $\psi'$ of
$(\Box+m^2)\psi'=0$ gives a Dirac solution
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi=
 \left\{\psi'\Pi_3,\frac{\ii}{m}(\sconj\partial\psi')\Pi_3\right\}^{\mathsf T}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the second component equation holds by definition, and the first
reduces to $-(\Box+m^2)\psi'/m=0$.
More generally, for constant complex scalars $a$ and $b$, the same scalar
solution gives
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi=
 \frac1m
 \begin{Bmatrix}
  ma+\ii b\partial\\
  \ii a\sconj\partial+mb
 \end{Bmatrix}\psi'\Pi_3.
 \label{eq:kg-dirac-general-lift}
\end{equation}
Substitution into the two component equations leaves respectively
$-a(\Box+m^2)\psi'/m$ and $-b(\Box+m^2)\psi'/m$, so both vanish.  This is a
two-constant family generated from one Klein--Gordon solution, not a claim
that one scalar solution parametrizes every Dirac solution.
With a potential, the gauge-covariant form is
\begin{equation}
 \bigl(\Wop(\ii\partial-q\sconj\Phi)-m\bI\bigr)\bPsi=0.
 \label{eq:dirac-field}
\end{equation}
It is invariant under
$\Phi'=\Phi+\sconj\partial\lambda$ and
$\bPsi=\exp(\ii q\lambda)\bPsi'$.

Isolating the time derivative in the two paravector equations gives the
ordinary evolution form
\begin{equation}
 \ii\partial_t\bPsi=H\bPsi,
 \qquad
 H:=qV\bI+\Wop(1)
       \left(m\bI+\Wop(\bp\cdot\bsig)\right)
 =qV\bI+
 \begin{Bmatrix}
  -\bp\cdot\bsig&m\\
  m&\bp\cdot\bsig
 \end{Bmatrix},
 \label{eq:dirac-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where $\bp=-\ii\pdr-q\bA$.  For real $V$ and $\bA$, suitable boundary
conditions make $\bp$ and hence $H$ Hermitian with respect to the integrated
Dirac pairing.  Therefore equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-hamiltonian} conserves the
spatial integral of the positive density in
equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-norm}.  This operator statement includes the
integration by parts implicit in spatial derivatives; it is stronger than
applying the algebraic $^{\mathsf H}$ pointwise.

In the rest of this subsection, $J$ denotes the Dirac probability-current
paravector rather than the electromagnetic source.  It has the intrinsic
form
\begin{equation}
 J:=\adj{\Psi_1\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}}
    +\Psi_2\Psi_2^{\mathsf H}
   =j+\bm j\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:dirac-current}
\end{equation}
Its scalar and vector components are
\begin{gather}
 j=\scpart{\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}\Psi_1}
  +\scpart{\Psi_2^{\mathsf H}\Psi_2},\\
 \bm j=-\vecpart{\Psi_1\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}}
       +\vecpart{\Psi_2\Psi_2^{\mathsf H}}.
\end{gather}
Here $j$ is the positive equal-time density.
The Dirac equation and its Hermitian form give
\begin{equation}
 \scpart{\partial J}
 =\partial_tj+\pdr\cdot\bm j=0.
\end{equation}
Thus the spatial integral of $j$ is conserved.  The ideal condition makes
each term in $J$ a future-null Hermitian paravector.  Their sum is therefore
future causal, and direct use of the determinant gives
\begin{equation}
 J^{\mathsf H}=J,
 \qquad
 \det(J)=j^2-\bm j^2
 =4\left\lVert\scpart{\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}\Psi_2}\right\rVert^2\geq0.
 \label{eq:dirac-current-causal}
\end{equation}
Consequently $\lVert\bm j\rVert\leq j$; where $j\ne0$, the ratio
$\bm j/j$ is the probability-flow velocity.  It is not the spin direction.

The full paravector $J$, not the scalar component by itself, has a
Lorentz-covariant transformation law.  For a wavefunction normalized on a
constant-time slice,
\begin{equation}
 \int j\,\dd^3\br=1.
\end{equation}
More generally, if $N$ is the future unit-normal paravector to a spacelike
hypersurface and $\dd A$ its invariant volume element, the covariant
normalization is
\begin{equation}
 \int\scpart{\adj N\,J}\,\dd A=1.
 \label{eq:dirac-hypersurface-norm}
\end{equation}
For a constant-time surface, $N=1$ and this reduces to the preceding spatial
integral.
The corresponding conventional column formulas are given in
Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-spinors}.

\subsection{Schr\"odinger--Pauli limit}

Recall $\bPsi=\{\Psi_1,\Psi_2\}^{\mathsf T}$,
$E=\ii\partial_t-qV$, $\bp=-\ii\pdr-q\bA$, and
$F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$.
Each relativistic Dirac component contains the rapid phase associated with
the rest energy.  For $n=1,2$, separate it from the slowly varying component
by
\begin{equation}
 \Psi_n'=\exp(-\ii mt)\Psi_n.
\end{equation}
For the covariant operators $E=\ii\partial_t-qV$ and
$\bp=-\ii\pdr-q\bA$, the exponential is spatially constant and therefore
\begin{align}
 E\Psi_n'&=\exp(-\ii mt)(E+m)\Psi_n,\\
 \bp\Psi_n'&=\exp(-\ii mt)\bp\Psi_n.
\end{align}
After cancelling the common exponential, the two Dirac equations become
\begin{align}
 (E+m-\bp\cdot\bsig)\Psi_2-m\Psi_1&=0,\\
 (E+m+\bp\cdot\bsig)\Psi_1-m\Psi_2&=0.
\end{align}
Eliminating $\Psi_1$ gives the exact second-order equation
\begin{equation}
 \left(E^2+2mE-\bp^2+q\bBf\cdot\bsig
 -\ii q\bE\cdot\bsig\right)\Psi_2=0.
\end{equation}
Under the usual low-energy, slowly varying-field power counting, the $E^2$
term and the electric-field term contribute beyond the leading
nonrelativistic approximation.  The expansion is in energy and momentum
relative to $m$; it is not an expansion in small $m$.  Retaining the leading
terms gives
\begin{equation}
 E\Psi_2=
 \frac{\bp^2-q\bBf\cdot\bsig}{2m}\Psi_2.
\end{equation}
At this order $\Psi_1$ and $\Psi_2$ have the same leading rest-frame
amplitude.  The conventionally normalized large component is their sum
divided by $\sqrt2$; either common component obeys the same leading equation
after a constant normalization.  Writing the faithful two-component column
as $\bpsi$ and substituting the covariant operators gives the Pauli equation
\begin{equation}
 \ii\partial_t\bpsi=\left(
  \frac{(-\ii\pdr-q\bA)^2}{2m}+qV
 -\frac{q}{2m}\bBf\cdot\bsig\right)\bpsi.
 \label{eq:pauli}
\end{equation}
In this column formula, each sigma-algebra element acts through its faithful
matrix image.
Since the physical spin is one half of $\bsig$, the magnetic term is
$-(q/m)\bBf\cdot(\bsig/2)$.  Thus minimal Dirac coupling gives magnetic
moment $q/m$ times the spin, or $g=2$, before radiative corrections.
Using the spin projectors,
$\bpsi=\bpsi_++\bpsi_-$ with $\bpsi_\pm=\Pi_\pm\bpsi$.  For a uniform static
magnetic field, $\bu=\bBf/\lVert\bBf\rVert$, the sectors decouple:
\begin{equation}
 \ii\partial_t\bpsi_\pm=\left(
 \frac{\bp^2\mp q\lVert\bBf\rVert}{2m}+qV\right)\bpsi_\pm.
\end{equation}
For a varying field direction the sectors are coupled.  With $H$ denoting the
Pauli Hamiltonian, direct differentiation gives
\begin{align}
 \ii\partial_t\bpsi_\pm
 &=\ii(\partial_t\Pi_\pm)\bpsi+\Pi_\pm H\bpsi\\
 &=H\bpsi_\pm+\left(\ii\partial_t\Pi_\pm
   +[\Pi_\pm,H]\right)\bpsi.
\end{align}
Because the field term commutes with its own instantaneous projectors, this is
\begin{gather}
 \ii\partial_t\bpsi_\pm=H\bpsi_\pm+\bm{S}_\pm\\
 \bm{S}_\pm=\left(\ii\partial_t\Pi_\pm
 +\frac{[\Pi_\pm,\bp^2]}{2m}\right)\bpsi.
\end{gather}

\subsection{Interacting operators and factorization}
\label{sec:interacting-factorization}

Recall $E=\ii\partial_t-qV$, $\bp=-\ii\pdr-q\bA$,
$\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$, $F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$, and
$S=\partial_tV+\pdr\cdot\bA$, with
$\bPsi=\{\Psi_1,\Psi_2\}^{\mathsf T}$.  The covariant energy and momentum
operators do not commute.  Direct
application to a test wavefunction gives
\begin{gather}
 [E,p_m]=\ii qE_m\\
 [p_m,p_n]=\ii q(\partial_mA_n-\partial_nA_m)\\
 \bp\times\bp=\ii q\bBf.
\end{gather}
Therefore
\begin{align}
 (E+\bsig\cdot\bp)(E-\bsig\cdot\bp)&=E^2-\bp^2-\ii qF,\\
 (E-\bsig\cdot\bp)(E+\bsig\cdot\bp)&=E^2-\bp^2-\ii qF^*,\\
 E+\bsig\cdot\bp&=\ii\partial^*-q\Phi,\\
 E-\bsig\cdot\bp&=\ii\partial-q\Phi^*.
\end{align}
Equivalently, scalar and vector extraction expose the two distinct pieces:
\begin{align}
 \scpart{(E+\bp\cdot\bsig)(E-\bp\cdot\bsig)}
 &=E^2-\bp^2,\\
 \scpart{(E-\bp\cdot\bsig)(E+\bp\cdot\bsig)}
 &=E^2-\bp^2,\\
 \vecpart{(E+\bp\cdot\bsig)(E-\bp\cdot\bsig)}\cdot\bsig
 &=-\ii qF,\\
 \vecpart{(E-\bp\cdot\bsig)(E+\bp\cdot\bsig)}\cdot\bsig
 &=-\ii qF^*.
 \label{eq:ordered-covariant-parts}
\end{align}
The Klein--Gordon equation retains the common scalar part.  Dirac
factorization retains the full ordered paravectors, so their vector
remainders are precisely the spin--field couplings.  This is the algebraic
reason spin appears when the first-order equation is squared.
Expanding the minimally coupled Klein--Gordon operator gives the fully explicit
result
\begin{equation}
 \left(\Box+\ii qS-q^2\Det\Phi+m^2
 +2\ii q(V\partial_t+\bA\cdot\pdr)\right)\psi=0.
\end{equation}
The two orderings satisfy
\begin{align}
 0&=\left((\partial+\ii q\Phi^*)
          (\partial^*+\ii q\Phi)+m^2\right)\Psi_1
   =(-E^2+\bp^2+m^2+\ii qF^*)\Psi_1,\\
 0&=\left((\partial^*+\ii q\Phi)
          (\partial+\ii q\Phi^*)+m^2\right)\Psi_2
   =(-E^2+\bp^2+m^2+\ii qF)\Psi_2.
\end{align}
These follow from the same mass-conjugate factorization used for the free
Dirac equation.  With the potential included, the two block operators become
\begin{align}
 \bDm&:=\Wop(\ii\partial-q\Phi^*)-m\bI,\\
 \bDm'&:=\Wop(\ii\partial-q\Phi^*)+m\bI.
\end{align}
Then
\begin{equation}
 \bDm'\bDm=
 -\begin{Bmatrix}
  -E^2+\bp^2+m^2+\ii qF^*&0\\
  0&-E^2+\bp^2+m^2+\ii qF
 \end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The electromagnetic field therefore prevents the interacting product from
being proportional to the block identity: its two components acquire the
distinct spin couplings $\ii qF^*$ and $\ii qF$.  In the free limit
$q=0$, both couplings vanish and $-E^2+\bp^2=\Box$, so
\begin{equation}
 \bDm'\bDm=-(\Box+m^2)\bI.
\end{equation}
Thus the free Dirac equation $\bDm\bPsi=0$ recovers the Klein--Gordon
equation on both components of the Dirac block.

The same formulas close the classical field--matter system without leaving
the paravector algebra.  For one charged Dirac field, take
\begin{equation}
 F^*=\partial\Phi-\scpart{\partial\Phi},
 \qquad
 D=qJ,
 \qquad
 \partial F=D^*,
 \label{eq:maxwell-dirac-source}
\end{equation}
together with the interacting Dirac equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-field}.  Then
$\scpart{\partial D}=q\scpart{\partial J}=0$ is exactly the source
consistency required by Maxwell's equation.  Replacing the Dirac current by
the Klein--Gordon current~\eqref{eq:kg-current} gives the corresponding
classical scalar system.  These are the Maxwell--Dirac and
Maxwell--Klein--Gordon equations; quantized photons, particle creation, and
radiative corrections require quantum field theory.

\subsection{Spin, energy sign, and probabilities}
\label{sec:dirac-spin-energy}

The Pauli-state paravector $B$ and the spin probabilities
$p_\pm(\bu)=\scpart{\Pi_\pm B}$ were constructed intrinsically in
equations~\eqref{eq:spin-state-paravector} and
\eqref{eq:spin-probability-intrinsic}.  For a Dirac block, the corresponding
local spin-state paravector is
\begin{equation}
 B:=\frac{\Psi_1\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}
          +\Psi_2\Psi_2^{\mathsf H}}{j}
 =1+\bm{s}\cdot\bsig,
 \qquad j\ne0.
 \label{eq:dirac-spin-state}
\end{equation}
It satisfies $B^{\mathsf H}=B$, $\scpart B=1$, and
$\det(B)=1-\bm{s}^2\geq0$.  A Dirac block can therefore have
$\bm{s}^2<1$ even when the full block is a pure state, because its two chiral
entries need not have the same spin direction.
It must not be confused with the current $J$: the adjugate on the first term
of $J$ makes its vector part probability flow, whereas the same-sign sum in
$B$ gives spin.  The spin projectors act identically from the left on both
entries,
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_\pm\bPsi
 :=\{\Pi_\pm\Psi_1,\Pi_\pm\Psi_2\}^{\mathsf T},
\end{equation}
and the local outcome fractions are
\begin{equation}
 \scpart{\Pi_\pm B}
 =\frac{\scpart{\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm\Psi_1}
       +\scpart{\Psi_2^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm\Psi_2}}{j}
 =\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bm{s}).
 \label{eq:dirac-spin-probability}
\end{equation}
For a spatial wavefunction, the directly computable probability is
\begin{equation}
 p_\pm(\bu)=
 \frac{\displaystyle\int\left(
  \scpart{\Psi_1^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm\Psi_1}
 +\scpart{\Psi_2^{\mathsf H}\Pi_\pm\Psi_2}\right)\dd^3\br}
 {\displaystyle\int j\,\dd^3\br}.
 \label{eq:integrated-dirac-spin-probability}
\end{equation}
For a normalized state the denominator is one.  If a pure state is aligned
with $\bu$ and
$\bu\cdot\bu'=\cos\alpha$, a measurement along $\bu'$ gives
$\cos^2(\alpha/2)$ and $\sin^2(\alpha/2)$.

For the free field, the Hamiltonian follows directly from the paravector
block function:
\begin{equation}
 H=\Wop(1)\left(m\bI+\Wop(\bp\cdot\bsig)\right)
 =\begin{Bmatrix}
  -\bp\cdot\bsig&m\\
  m&\bp\cdot\bsig
 \end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:free-dirac-hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
At fixed real momentum, let $E=\sqrt{\bp^2+m^2}>0$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 H^{\mathsf H}=H,
 \qquad H^2=E^2\bI.
\end{equation}
The Hermitian energy projectors are therefore
\begin{equation}
 \bQm_\pm:=\tfrac12\left(\bI\pm\frac{H}{E}\right).
 \label{eq:energy-projectors}
\end{equation}
They satisfy $\bQm_\pm^{\mathsf H}=\bQm_\pm$,
$\bQm_\pm^2=\bQm_\pm$, $\bQm_+\bQm_-=0$, and
$\bQm_++\bQm_-=\bI$.  Their directly computable probabilities
are
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\lVert\bQm_+\bPsi\rVert^2}{\lVert\bPsi\rVert^2},
 \qquad
 \frac{\lVert\bQm_-\bPsi\rVert^2}{\lVert\bPsi\rVert^2}.
 \label{eq:energy-probabilities}
\end{equation}

For a wave packet, let $\bPsi(\bp)$ denote its momentum-space block and use
$H(\bp)$ and $E(\bp)=\sqrt{\bp^2+m^2}$ in the preceding formulas.  For
example, the positive-energy probability is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\displaystyle\int
  \left\lVert\tfrac12\left(\bI+H(\bp)/E(\bp)\right)
  \bPsi(\bp)\right\rVert^2\dd^3\bp}
 {\displaystyle\int\lVert\bPsi(\bp)\rVert^2\dd^3\bp},
 \label{eq:wavepacket-energy-probability}
\end{equation}
with the sign reversed for the negative-energy probability.  In position
space $E=\sqrt{-\pdr^2+m^2}$, so these free energy projectors are nonlocal.

For $m>0$, the covariant mass-shell idempotents have a different role.  Put
\begin{equation}
 U=\frac{P}{m}=\frac{E+\bp\cdot\bsig}{m}.
\end{equation}
The mass shell gives $\det(U)=\det(P)/m^2=1$, and therefore
$\Wop(\adj U)^2=\bI$.  Hence the following expressions are idempotents.
For the phase $w=\exp(-\ii f)$, the free Dirac equation requires the constant
amplitude to lie in the $+1$ eigenspace of $\Wop(\adj U)$.  For the conjugate
phase $w^{\mathsf H}=\exp(+\ii f)$, it lies in the $-1$ eigenspace.  Hence
\begin{equation}
 \tfrac12(\bI+\Wop(\adj U)),
 \qquad
 \tfrac12(\bI-\Wop(\adj U))
\end{equation}
are the covariant positive- and negative-frequency amplitude idempotents.
The conjugate phase has canonical spatial momentum $-\bp$.  To compare its
amplitude with the Hamiltonian projector $\bQm_-(\bp)$ at a fixed momentum
label $\bp$, first replace $\bp$ by $-\bp$ in the future momentum and then
take the conjugate phase.  Equivalently, $\bQm_-(\bp)$ acts on the
negative-frequency phase $\exp(+\ii Et+\ii\bp\cdot\br)$, whereas the
displayed $w^{\mathsf H}$ corresponds directly to $\bQm_-(-\bp)$.
They are not Hermitian under the positive componentwise pairing away from
rest, because
\begin{equation}
 \Wop(\adj U)^{\mathsf H}=\Wop(U)\ne\Wop(\adj U)
 \qquad(\bp\ne0).
\end{equation}
Their squared component norms are therefore not probabilities; the
Hermitian projectors $\bQm_\pm$ are used for that purpose.

For a massive free field, the four joint spin--energy basis states are most
transparent at rest.  When $\bp=0$,
\begin{equation}
 H=m\begin{Bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Positive energy means $\Psi_2=\Psi_1$, while negative energy means
$\Psi_2=-\Psi_1$.  With the spin basis from
equation~\eqref{eq:ideal-spin-basis}, the four normalized rest states are
\begin{align}
 \frac1{\sqrt2}\{e_+,e_+\}^{\mathsf T},
 &\qquad
 \frac1{\sqrt2}\{e_-,e_-\}^{\mathsf T},\\
 \frac1{\sqrt2}\{e_+,-e_+\}^{\mathsf T},
 &\qquad
 \frac1{\sqrt2}\{e_-,-e_-\}^{\mathsf T}.
 \label{eq:dirac-rest-states}
\end{align}
Thus the choice $e_+$ or $e_-$ is spin up or down, while the relative sign
between the two chiral entries is the energy sign.

Negative energy is not by itself an antimatter label.  The existing sigma
conjugation gives a charge-reversed solution without introducing another
conjugation operation.  If $\bPsi$ has charge $q$, define
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi_c:=\{\Psi_2^*\sigma_1,-\Psi_1^*\sigma_1\}^{\mathsf T}.
 \label{eq:dirac-charge-reversal}
\end{equation}
Since $\Pi_3^*\sigma_1=\sigma_1\Pi_3$, both entries of $\bPsi_c$ remain in the fixed
minimal left ideal.  Applying $^*$ to the two coupled Dirac equations shows
that $\bPsi_c$ satisfies the same equations with $q$ replaced by $-q$.
Sigma conjugation also reverses the scalar plane-wave phase, so it maps a
negative-frequency charge-$q$ mode to a positive-frequency charge-$-q$
mode.  Since $\Pi_\pm^*=\Pi_\mp$, the raw Pauli-projector label also
reverses under this map; the physical antiparticle spin is determined by
applying the desired spin projector to the charge-reversed state.  After
field quantization the coefficients of the original
negative-frequency modes become creation amplitudes for positive-energy
antiparticles.  Thus $\bQm_-$ is a negative-frequency projector for the
one-particle charge-$q$ equation, not an antimatter-probability projector.
For a free field, proper orthochronous Lorentz transformations preserve the
future and past mass shells and do not mix these two frequency branches.

Finally, $\Pi_\pm$ describes the canonical Pauli spin in the chosen inertial
frame.  Spin and energy give simultaneous alternatives only when their
projectors commute.  For a fixed free momentum this occurs for any spin axis
at rest, and away from rest for $\bu$ collinear with $\bp$.  Taking
$\bu=\bp/\lVert\bp\rVert$ gives the usual helicity labels; reversing the axis
reverses those labels.  A covariant arbitrary rest-frame spin axis would
require a momentum-dependent boosted projector, so it is not represented by
simply holding $\bu$ fixed in $\Pi_\pm$.  For $a,b\in\{+1,-1\}$, write
\begin{equation}
 \bQm_a:=\tfrac12(\bI+aH/E),
 \qquad
 \Pi_b:=\tfrac12(1+b\bu\cdot\bsig).
\end{equation}
Here $\Pi_b$ acts componentwise on both Dirac entries, so
$[\bQm_a,\Pi_b]$ is a commutator of block operators.  When
$[\bQm_a,\Pi_b]=0$, the four joint probabilities are
\begin{equation}
 p_{ab}=\frac{\lVert\bQm_a\Pi_b\bPsi\rVert^2}
 {\lVert\bPsi\rVert^2},
 \qquad
 \sum_{a,b}p_{ab}=1.
 \label{eq:joint-spin-energy-probability}
\end{equation}
They describe positive or negative energy combined with spin up or down.
For a wave packet, a fixed axis gives joint projectors only when this
commutation condition holds throughout its momentum support.

If the projectors do not commute, the question must specify the measurement
order.  Energy followed by spin and spin followed by energy give respectively
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\lVert\Pi_b\bQm_a\bPsi\rVert^2}{\lVert\bPsi\rVert^2},
 \qquad
 \frac{\lVert\bQm_a\Pi_b\bPsi\rVert^2}{\lVert\bPsi\rVert^2},
 \label{eq:sequential-spin-energy-probability}
\end{equation}
and need not agree.  The conditional spin probability after an energy result
$a$ is
$\lVert\Pi_b\bQm_a\bPsi\rVert^2/\lVert\bQm_a\bPsi\rVert^2$, provided
$\lVert\bQm_a\bPsi\rVert^2\ne0$.
For a static external field, energy sectors are instead defined by the
spectrum of the full Hermitian Hamiltonian~\eqref{eq:dirac-hamiltonian}.  A
time-dependent field need not preserve any fixed positive--negative split;
its mixing of the two sectors requires field quantization for a complete
pair-production interpretation.

\section{Rotations and Lorentz boosts}
\label{sec:transformations}

Throughout this section, the transformation factor $T$ is defined from a
positive generator.  Thus $T=R$ for rotations and $T=L$ for boosts.  The
opposite action is written with $T^{-1}$; it is not introduced by redefining
$T$ with a negative exponential.

\subsection{Rotors}

Let $\theta\in\R$ be the rotation angle, $\bu\in\R^3$ a unit axis,
$X=t+\br\cdot\bsig$ a spacetime paravector, $Z$ a paravector, and
$\psi\Pi_3=\psi$ a minimal-ideal amplitude.  The rotation is generated by
\begin{gather}
 R:=\exp\!\left(\ii\frac\theta2\,\bu\cdot\bsig\right)
  =\cos\frac\theta2+\bu\cdot\ii\bsig\,\sin\frac\theta2\\
 R^{-1}=\rev R
 =\cos\frac\theta2-\bu\cdot\ii\bsig\,\sin\frac\theta2.
\end{gather}
The sandwich action $X'=\rev R X R$ fixes the scalar component and rotates
the spatial vector according to Rodrigues' formula,
\begin{equation}
 \br'=\br\cos\theta+(\bu\times\br)\sin\theta
       +(\bu\cdot\br)(1-\cos\theta)\bu.
\end{equation}
The half-angle explains the spinorial sign change
$R(\theta+2\pi)=-R(\theta)$.
The corresponding column-spinor and density-matrix transformations are
collected in Appendix~\ref{sec:matrix-spinors}.
For a spinor undergoing the same rotation, let
$\psi_R:=R^{\mathsf H}\psi$.  Then
\begin{gather}
 \psi_R^{\mathsf H}=\psi^{\mathsf H}R\\
 \langle Z\rangle_{\psi_R}
 =\frac{\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}RZR^{\mathsf H}\psi}}
 {\scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}}\\
 R^{\mathsf H}=R^{-1}.
\end{gather}
If the observable is rotated as $Z'=R^{\mathsf H}ZR$, cyclicity gives
\begin{equation}
 \langle Z'\rangle_{\psi_R}=\langle Z\rangle_\psi.
\end{equation}
In particular, $R^{\mathsf H}(\br\cdot\bsig)R=\br'\cdot\bsig$ gives the
same Rodrigues rotation displayed above.  Both entries of a Dirac block
transform by this same factor:
\begin{equation}
 \Psi_1'=R^{\mathsf H}\Psi_1,
 \qquad
 \Psi_2'=R^{\mathsf H}\Psi_2.
\end{equation}
The current transforms by the same vector sandwich:
\begin{equation}
 J'=R^{\mathsf H}JR.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Boosts}
\label{sec:boosts}

Let $\bu\in\R^3$ be a unit boost direction,
$X=t+\br\cdot\bsig$ a spacetime paravector,
$F=(\bE+\ii\bBf)\cdot\bsig$ an electromagnetic field, and
$\Phi=V+\bA\cdot\bsig$ the potential,
$D=\rho+\bJ\cdot\bsig$ the electromagnetic source, and
$\bPsi=\{\Psi_1,\Psi_2\}^{\mathsf T}$ a Dirac block.  Let rapidity $\theta$
satisfy
$\beta=\tanh\theta$ and $\gamma=\cosh\theta$.  The boost is
\begin{gather}
 L:=\exp\!\left(\frac\theta2\,\bu\cdot\bsig\right)
 =\cosh\frac\theta2+\bu\cdot\bsig\,\sinh\frac\theta2\\
 L^{\mathsf H}=L\\
 L^{-1}=L^*
 =\cosh\frac\theta2-\bu\cdot\bsig\,\sinh\frac\theta2.
\end{gather}
Unlike a rotation, a boost acts oppositely on the two chiral entries of a
Dirac block.  In the following field laws the primed field is evaluated at
$X'=L^{-1}XL^{-1}$ and the unprimed field at $X$.  With the ordering used in
the Dirac equation,
\begin{equation}
 \Psi_1'(X')=L\Psi_1(X),
 \qquad
 \Psi_2'(X')=L^{-1}\Psi_2(X).
 \label{eq:dirac-boost}
\end{equation}
Thus $L^{-1}\psi$ is the transformation of the second chiral carrier, while
$L\psi$ is the transformation of the first.  A single minimal-ideal
amplitude has no unique boost law until its chirality is specified.  The two
chiral representations use the same rotation factor but opposite boost
generators, as required by the mass coupling.

The similarity $L^{-1}ZL$ is a different operation: it preserves
$\scpart Z$ and is not the spacetime-vector boost used here.
The spacetime paravector therefore transforms by the Hermitian congruence
$X'=L^{-1}XL^{-1}$, which yields
the components
$\br_{\parallel}=(\br\cdot\bu)\bu$, $\br_{\perp}=\br-\br_{\parallel}$,
$\br'_{\parallel}=(\br'\cdot\bu)\bu$, and
$\br'_{\perp}=\br'-\br'_{\parallel}$:
\begin{align}
 t'&=\gamma(t-\beta\bu\cdot\br),\\
 \br'_{\parallel}&=\gamma(\br_{\parallel}-\beta t\bu),\\
 \br'_{\perp}&=\br_{\perp}.
\end{align}
It preserves $\Det X=t^2-\br^2$.  Four-momentum has the same vector law,
\begin{equation}
 P'=L^{-1}PL^{-1},
 \qquad
 \scpart{\adj{P'}X'}=\scpart{\adj P\,X},
\end{equation}
so the plane-wave phase is Lorentz invariant.  The electromagnetic field
instead obeys
$F'(X')=L^{-1}F(X)L$ for
$F=(\bE+\bBf\ii)\cdot\bsig$.  Define
$\bE_{\parallel}=(\bE\cdot\bu)\bu$,
$\bE_{\perp}=\bE-\bE_{\parallel}$ and likewise
$\bE'_{\parallel}=(\bE'\cdot\bu)\bu$,
$\bE'_{\perp}=\bE'-\bE'_{\parallel}$; define the four corresponding
components of $\bBf$ and $\bBf'$ by the same formulas.  Then
\begin{align}
 \bE'_{\parallel}&=\bE_{\parallel},\\
 \bE'_{\perp}&=\gamma(\bE_{\perp}+\beta\bu\times\bBf),\\
 \bBf'_{\parallel}&=\bBf_{\parallel},\\
 \bBf'_{\perp}&=\gamma(\bBf_{\perp}-\beta\bu\times\bE).
\end{align}
With the same convention,
\begin{gather}
 \Phi'(X')=L^{-1}\Phi(X)L^{-1}\\
 \partial'=L\partial L\\
 \partial'^*=L^{-1}\partial^*L^{-1}\\
 D'=L^{-1}DL^{-1}\\
 D'^*=LD^*L.
\end{gather}
Since $L$ is constant and $LL^{-1}=1$, the field equation is covariant:
\begin{equation}
 \partial'F'=L(\partial F)L=LD^*L=D'^*.
\end{equation}
The potential and derivative transformations likewise give
\begin{align}
 \ii\partial'-q\Phi'^*
 &=L(\ii\partial-q\Phi^*)L,\\
 \ii\partial'^*-q\Phi'
 &=L^{-1}(\ii\partial^*-q\Phi)L^{-1}.
\end{align}
Together with equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-boost}, these identities make both
coupled Dirac equations covariant.  The current in
equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-current} transforms with the spacetime-vector law,
\begin{equation}
 J'(X')=L^{-1}J(X)L^{-1}.
\end{equation}
If the hypersurface normal obeys the same vector law,
$N'=L^{-1}NL^{-1}$, then
\begin{equation}
 \scpart{\adj{N'}J'}=\scpart{\adj N\,J}
\end{equation}
by adjugation and cyclicity.  This is the local invariance behind the
hypersurface normalization~\eqref{eq:dirac-hypersurface-norm}.

\section{Flat spacetime in generalized coordinates}
\label{sec:generalized}

This section changes coordinates within flat Minkowski spacetime; it does not
introduce spacetime curvature or a gravitational field.  For a time-like
curve, recall that $U=\dd X/\dd s$ represents proper velocity.  Let the real
scalar function $f$ and real three-vector function $\bg$ define a locally invertible
map from $(t,\br)$ to the inertial components of $X$.  If $x_0=t$ and
$x_n=r_n$ for $n=1,2,3$, write
$\partial_n:=\partial/\partial x_n$.  For
$n,m\in\{0,1,2,3\}$, the pulled-back metric coefficients are
\begin{equation}
 g_{nm}:=\partial_n f\,\partial_m f
 -\partial_n\bg\cdot\partial_m\bg.
 \label{eq:flat-pullback-metric}
\end{equation}
The determinant of the Jacobian of $(f,\bg)$ must be nonzero for these
functions to form a local coordinate chart.  Although $g_{nm}$ may vary with
the coordinates, it is the pullback of the flat metric.

Let
\begin{gather}
 X=f(t,\br)+\bg(t,\br)\cdot\bsig\\
 \dd X=\dd f+\dd\bg\cdot\bsig.
\end{gather}
Writing $\dd X=A\dd t+\bB\cdot\dd\br$ gives
\begin{gather}
 A=\partial_tf+\partial_t\bg\cdot\bsig\\
 \bB=\pdr f+\pdr(\bg\cdot\bsig),
\end{gather}
and the interval remains
\begin{equation}
 \dd s^2=\dd X\,\dd X^*=\dd f^2-\dd\bg^2.
\end{equation}
For a future-directed time-like curve with $\dd f>0$,
\begin{gather}
 \dd s=\sqrt{1-\bv^2}\,\dd f\\
 \bv^2<1.
\end{gather}
Define the geometric velocity and Lorentz factor by
\begin{gather}
 \bv=\frac{\dd\bg}{\dd f}\\
 \gamma=\frac{\dd f}{\dd s}=\frac1{\sqrt{1-\bv^2}}\\
 \frac{\dd X}{\dd s}=U=\gamma(1+\bv\cdot\bsig).
\end{gather}
With geometric acceleration
$\ba=\dd\bv/\dd s=\gamma\dd^2\bg/\dd f^2$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\dd U}{\dd s}
 =\gamma^3(\bv\cdot\ba)
 +\left(\gamma\ba+\gamma^3(\bv\cdot\ba)\bv\right)\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
For inertial motion, $\dd U/\dd s=0$.  Its scalar component gives
$\bv\cdot\ba=0$, and its vector component then gives $\ba=0$.  Hence
$\dd^2\bg/\dd f^2=0$, so $\bg=c_1f+c_2$ and the geometric velocity is
constant.
If $f'=\dd f/\dd t$ and $\bg'=\dd\bg/\dd t$, then
\begin{gather}
 \frac{\dd\bv}{\dd t}
 =\frac{\bg''f'-\bg'f''}{(f')^2}\\
 [U,\dd U/\dd s]=2\gamma^2(\bv\times\ba)\cdot\ii\bsig.
\end{gather}

\section{Pauli-matrix representation}
\label{sec:matrix}

Throughout this appendix, $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$ is a complex paravector and
$\bM=\operatorname{mat}(Z)$ is its faithful $2\times2$ matrix image.

\subsection{Faithful representation}

Every complex element of the displayed algebra basis can be collected as
\begin{equation}
 Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig.
\end{equation}
Here $S\in\C$ and $\bV\in\C^3$.
Such an element is called a complex paravector.  Its faithful $2\times2$
complex representation begins with
\[
 \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_1)=\begin{Bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{Bmatrix},\quad
 \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_2)=\begin{Bmatrix}0&-\ii\\\ii&0\end{Bmatrix},\quad
 \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_3)=\begin{Bmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{Bmatrix}.
\]
Thus
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(S+\bV\cdot\bsig)=
 \begin{Bmatrix}
  S+V_3&V_1-\ii V_2\\
  V_1+\ii V_2&S-V_3
 \end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:matrix}
\end{equation}
It preserves identity, addition, and multiplied order:
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}(1)&=\bI_2,\\
 \operatorname{mat}(Z'Z)
 &=\operatorname{mat}(Z')\operatorname{mat}(Z).
 \label{eq:matrix-product}
\end{align}

\subsection{Matrix operations and induced algebra operations}

Let $\bM=\operatorname{mat}(Z)$ with entries
$m_{jk}=a_{jk}+b_{jk}\ii$, where $a_{jk},b_{jk}\in\R$.  Define transpose by
\begin{equation}
 (\bM^{\mathsf T})_{jk}:=m_{kj}.
\end{equation}
Define entrywise complex conjugation independently by
\begin{gather}
 m_{jk}^{\mathsf C}:=a_{jk}-b_{jk}\ii\\
 \bM^{\mathsf C}:=
 \begin{Bmatrix}
  m_{11}^{\mathsf C}&m_{12}^{\mathsf C}\\
  m_{21}^{\mathsf C}&m_{22}^{\mathsf C}
 \end{Bmatrix}.
\end{gather}
The Hermitian transpose is then
\begin{equation}
 \bM^{\mathsf H}:=(\bM^{\mathsf C})^{\mathsf T}
 = (\bM^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf C}.
 \label{eq:matrix-operations}
\end{equation}
Define the algebra operation $^{\mathsf H}$ through the faithful
representation by
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(Z^{\mathsf H}):=\bM^{\mathsf H}.
\end{equation}
For $n=1,2,3$, its action on every basis type is
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}(1)^{\mathsf H}&=\bI_2,\\
 (\ii\bI_2)^{\mathsf H}&=-\ii\bI_2,\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}
 &=\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n),\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\ii\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}
 &=-\operatorname{mat}(\ii\sigma_n).
\end{align}
Hence $1^{\mathsf H}=1$, $\ii^{\mathsf H}=-\ii$,
$\sigma_n^{\mathsf H}=\sigma_n$, and
$(\ii\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}=-\ii\sigma_n$.
Hermitian conjugation changes matrix-product order.  Therefore faithfulness
gives
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}((\sigma_n\sigma_m)^{\mathsf H})
 &=\left(\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n)
          \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_m)\right)^{\mathsf H}\\
 &=\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_m)^{\mathsf H}
   \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n)^{\mathsf H}
 =\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_m\sigma_n),
\end{align}
and hence
\begin{equation}
 (\sigma_n\sigma_m)^{\mathsf H}
 =\sigma_m^{\mathsf H}\sigma_n^{\mathsf H}
 =\sigma_m\sigma_n.
 \label{eq:hermitian-product-order}
\end{equation}
Thus $^{\mathsf H}$ is precisely the Hermitian operator defined in
Section~\ref{sec:conjugate-operators}.
To define the distinct sigma conjugation, let
$\bm{S}:=\begin{Bmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{Bmatrix}$ and
$\bm{S}^2=-\bI_2$, so that $\bm S^{-1}=-\bm S$, and set
\begin{align}
 \bM^*&:=\bm{S}^{-1}\bM^{\mathsf C}\bm{S},\\
 \operatorname{mat}(Z^*)&:=\bM^*.
 \label{eq:matrix-sigma-conjugation}
\end{align}
For $n=1,2,3$, this gives the required action on every basis type:
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}(1)^*
 &=\bm S^{-1}\bI_2^{\mathsf C}\bm S=\bI_2,\\
 (\ii\bI_2)^*
 &=\bm S^{-1}(\ii\bI_2)^{\mathsf C}\bm S=-\ii\bI_2,\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n)^*
 &=\bm S^{-1}\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n)^{\mathsf C}\bm S
 =-\operatorname{mat}(\sigma_n),\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\ii\sigma_n)^*
 &=\bm S^{-1}\operatorname{mat}(\ii\sigma_n)^{\mathsf C}\bm S
 =\operatorname{mat}(\ii\sigma_n).
\end{align}
The operation also preserves multiplied order.  Since entrywise conjugation
preserves order,
\begin{align}
 (\bM\bN)^*
 &=\bm S^{-1}\bM^{\mathsf C}\bN^{\mathsf C}\bm S\\
 &=(\bm S^{-1}\bM^{\mathsf C}\bm S)
   (\bm S^{-1}\bN^{\mathsf C}\bm S)
 =\bM^*\bN^*.
\end{align}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
 (\bM\bN)^*=\bM^*\bN^*.
 \label{eq:matrix-star-product}
\end{equation}
The four operations must remain distinct:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c l l}
 \toprule
 symbol & matrix operation & relation to the algebra \\
 \midrule
 $\bM^{\mathsf T}$ & transpose & changes matrix-product order \\
 $\bM^{\mathsf C}$ & entrywise complex conjugation & sends $\ii$ to $-\ii$ \\
 $\bM^{\mathsf H}$ & Hermitian transpose
   & $\operatorname{mat}(Z^{\mathsf H})$ \\
 $\bM^*$ & sigma conjugation
   & $\operatorname{mat}(Z^*)$ \\
 \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Their product-order rules are
\begin{align}
 (\bM\bN)^{\mathsf T}&=\bN^{\mathsf T}\bM^{\mathsf T},\\
 (\bM\bN)^{\mathsf C}&=\bM^{\mathsf C}\bN^{\mathsf C},\\
 (\bM\bN)^{\mathsf H}&=\bN^{\mathsf H}\bM^{\mathsf H}.
\end{align}
Sigma conjugation preserves order by equation~\eqref{eq:matrix-star-product}.
Thus the induced algebra operations satisfy
\begin{align}
 (Z^{\mathsf H})^{\mathsf H}&=Z,\\
 (ZZ')^{\mathsf H}&=Z'^{\mathsf H}Z^{\mathsf H},\\
 (Z^*)^*&=Z,\\
 (ZZ')^*&=Z^*Z'^*,\\
 Z^{*\mathsf H}&=Z^{\mathsf H*}.
 \label{eq:algebra-operator-laws}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Trace, determinant, and adjugate}
Recall $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$ and $\bM=\operatorname{mat}(Z)$.  Define the
matrix trace and determinant by
\begin{gather}
 \operatorname{tr}(\bM):=m_{11}+m_{22}\\
 \det(\bM):=m_{11}m_{22}-m_{12}m_{21}.
\end{gather}
The corresponding paravector operations are
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{tr}(Z)&:=\operatorname{tr}(\bM)=2S,
 \label{eq:paravector-tr-det}
 \\
 \det(Z)&:=\det(\bM).
 \label{eq:paravector-matrix-det}
\end{align}
Faithfulness transfers the matrix identities to the algebra:
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{tr}(Z+Z')&=\operatorname{tr}(Z)+\operatorname{tr}(Z'),\\
 \operatorname{tr}(cZ)&=c\operatorname{tr}(Z),\\
 \operatorname{tr}(ZZ')&=\operatorname{tr}(Z'Z),\\
 \det(ZZ')&=\det(Z)\det(Z'),\\
 \det(cZ)&=c^2\det(Z).
\label{eq:trace-det-properties}
\end{align}
The last identity holds for $c\in\C$.
For a $2\times2$ matrix and its paravector preimage, define
\begin{align}
 \adj{\bM}&:=\operatorname{tr}(\bM)\bI_2-\bM,\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\adj Z)&:=\adj{\bM}.
\end{align}
Direct comparison with the induced algebra operators gives
\begin{align}
 \adj Z&=Z^{*\mathsf H}
 \label{eq:matrix-adjugate}\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\adj Z)=\adj{\bM}=(\bM^*)^{\mathsf H}.
\end{align}
Equivalently,
\begin{gather}
 \adj Z=\operatorname{tr}(Z)-Z\\
 \operatorname{tr}(Z)=Z+\adj Z=2S.
\end{gather}
The adjugate changes product order and obeys
\begin{align}
 \adj{ZZ'}&=\adj{Z'}\adj Z,\\
 \adj{\adj Z}&=Z,\\
 \adj Z\,Z&=Z\adj Z=\det(Z),\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\adj Z\,Z)&=\det(\bM)\bI_2.
 \label{eq:matrix-adj-det}
\end{align}
Consequently, when $\det(Z)\ne0$,
\begin{equation}
 Z^{-1}=\frac{\adj Z}{\det(Z)}.
\end{equation}
Finally, Cayley--Hamilton gives
\begin{align}
 \bM^2-\operatorname{tr}(\bM)\bM+\det(\bM)\bI_2&=0,\\
 Z^2-\operatorname{tr}(Z)Z+\det(Z)&=0.
 \label{eq:cayley-hamilton}
\end{align}

\subsection{Algebra--matrix crosswalk}

For $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, define
\begin{gather}
 \scpart Z:=S\\
 \vecpart Z:=\bV.
\end{gather}
Thus $\vecpart Z$ is the coefficient vector, while its algebra element is
$\bV\cdot\bsig$.

For $\bM=\operatorname{mat}(Z)$, the extraction formulas are
\begin{align}
 \scpart Z&=S=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bM),\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\scpart Z)&=S\bI_2,\\
 (\vecpart Z)_n&=V_n=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\sigma}_n\bM),\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\vecpart Z\cdot\bsig)
 &=\bM-\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bM)\bI_2.
 \label{eq:matrix-sc-vec}
\end{align}

Define real and imaginary paravector extraction through Hermitian splitting:
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}(\repart Z)&:=\tfrac12(\bM+\bM^{\mathsf H}),\\
 \operatorname{mat}(\impart Z)&:=\frac{\bM-\bM^{\mathsf H}}{2\ii}.
 \label{eq:matrix-re-im}
\end{align}
By construction, the algebra and matrix operators agree through
\begin{gather}
 \operatorname{mat}(Z^{\mathsf H})=\bM^{\mathsf H}\\
 \operatorname{mat}(Z^*)=\bM^*\\
 \operatorname{mat}(Z^{*\mathsf H})=(\bM^*)^{\mathsf H}.
\end{gather}
The matrix-only operations $^{\mathsf C}$ and $^{\mathsf T}$ are related to
the algebra operations by
\begin{align}
 \bM^{\mathsf C}&=\bm S\bM^*\bm S^{-1}
 \label{eq:matrix-c-t-crosswalk}
 \\
 \bM^{\mathsf T}&=\bm S\operatorname{mat}(Z^{*\mathsf H})\bm S^{-1}.
\end{align}

Finally, scalar extraction of a product and the paravector norm
are
\begin{align}
 \scpart{Z'Z}&=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bM_{Z'}\bM_Z),\\
 \lVert Z\rVert^2&:=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bM^{\mathsf H}\bM)
 =\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\bM\bM^{\mathsf H}).
 \label{eq:matrix-norm}
\end{align}

\subsection{Matrix amplitudes and column spinors}
\label{sec:matrix-spinors}

Let $\psi$ and $\psi'$ be paravector amplitudes and $Z$ a paravector
observable, and define
\begin{gather}
 \bM_\psi:=\operatorname{mat}(\psi)\\
 \bM_{\psi'}:=\operatorname{mat}(\psi').
\end{gather}
This subsection translates the intrinsic paravector spinor development of
Section~\ref{sec:physical-spinors} into the familiar matrix and column
languages.
Faithfulness and Hermitian compatibility give
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(\psi'^{\mathsf H}Z\psi)
 =\bM_{\psi'}^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(Z)\bM_\psi.
\end{equation}
Scalar extraction is half the matrix trace, so the intrinsic definitions
\eqref{eq:paravector-inner-product} and
\eqref{eq:paravector-matrix-element} become
\begin{align}
 \langle\psi'\mid\psi\rangle
 &=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}
   (\bM_{\psi'}^{\mathsf H}\bM_\psi),\\
 \langle\psi'\mid Z\mid\psi\rangle
 &=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}
   (\bM_{\psi'}^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(Z)\bM_\psi).
\end{align}
Thus $\operatorname{mat}(\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi)
=\bM_\psi^{\mathsf H}\bM_\psi$ is generally Hermitian rather than scalar;
the scalar inner product is obtained by taking half its trace.

An unrestricted matrix $\bM_\psi$ contains two independent columns.  Under
left multiplication those columns transform as two equivalent
two-component carriers, so the full matrix is not yet one conventional Pauli
column.  The fixed right projection below selects one carrier.

Recall the fixed idempotent from Section~\ref{sec:physical-spinors}:
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_3=\tfrac12(1+\sigma_3).
\end{equation}
Here $\Pi_3$ is the fixed column selector and is distinct from the matrix
superscript $^{\mathsf C}$ used for entrywise complex conjugation.
With
\begin{gather}
 \bm v:=\{1,0\}^{\mathsf T}\\
 \bm v^{\mathsf H}\bm v=1,
\end{gather}
its matrix image is
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(\Pi_3)=\bm v\bm v^{\mathsf H}
 =\begin{Bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Any other fixed primitive Hermitian idempotent gives an equivalent column
basis, but $\Pi_3$ is kept fixed throughout the following formulas.

For unrestricted complex paravectors $Z$ and $Z'$, let
$\psi=Z\Pi_3$ and $\psi'=Z'\Pi_3$ be two ideal amplitudes.  Define their
conventional columns by
\begin{gather}
 \bpsi:=\frac1{\sqrt2}\bM_\psi\bm v
 \label{eq:ideal-column-map}
 \\
 \bpsi':=\frac1{\sqrt2}\bM_{\psi'}\bm v.
\end{gather}
The inverse relations are
\begin{gather}
 \bM_\psi=\sqrt2\,\bpsi\bm v^{\mathsf H}
 \label{eq:ideal-matrix-map}
 \\
 \bM_{\psi'}=\sqrt2\,\bpsi'\bm v^{\mathsf H}.
\end{gather}
For the normalized ideal basis in
equation~\eqref{eq:ideal-spin-basis}, this map gives explicitly
\begin{equation}
 \frac1{\sqrt2}\operatorname{mat}(e_+)\bm v
 =\begin{Bmatrix}1\\0\end{Bmatrix},
 \qquad
 \frac1{\sqrt2}\operatorname{mat}(e_-)\bm v
 =\begin{Bmatrix}0\\1\end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:ideal-basis-column-map}
\end{equation}
Thus each ideal amplitude and its column contain the same two complex
components.  Its matrix has rank one for a nonzero state and rank zero for
the zero state.  The fixed right factor records the chosen ideal, while all
state information is contained in the column.

Substitution into the trace pairing gives exactly the conventional formulas:
\begin{align}
 \langle\psi'\mid\psi\rangle
 &=\bpsi'^{\mathsf H}\bpsi
 \label{eq:minimal-ideal-spinor}
 \\
 \langle\psi'\mid Z\mid\psi\rangle
 &=\bpsi'^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(Z)\bpsi.
\end{align}
In particular,
\begin{gather}
 \scpart{\psi^{\mathsf H}\psi}=\bpsi^{\mathsf H}\bpsi\\
 \langle Z\rangle_\psi
 =\frac{\bpsi^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(Z)\bpsi}
 {\bpsi^{\mathsf H}\bpsi}.
\end{gather}
The factor $1/\sqrt2$ in the column map compensates for scalar extraction
being one half of the matrix trace.  Left multiplication needs no further
adjustment: $T\psi$ maps to $\operatorname{mat}(T)\bpsi$.
In particular, the rotor law $\psi'=R^{\mathsf H}\psi$ becomes
\begin{equation}
 \bpsi'=\operatorname{mat}(R)^{\mathsf H}\bpsi,
\end{equation}
which rotates the column and its Bloch vector in the same direction as the
Rodrigues formula in Section~\ref{sec:transformations}.

For a measurement along a unit direction $\bu$, let
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig),
 \qquad
 \psi_\pm=\Pi_\pm\psi.
\end{equation}
The corresponding matrices and columns are
\begin{align}
 \bM_{\psi_\pm}
 &=\operatorname{mat}(\Pi_\pm)\bM_\psi,\\
 \bpsi_\pm
 &=\operatorname{mat}(\Pi_\pm)\bpsi.
\end{align}
Consequently, the probability in
equation~\eqref{eq:spin-probability-intrinsic} is
\begin{align}
 p_\pm(\bu)
 &=\frac{\bpsi_\pm^{\mathsf H}\bpsi_\pm}
 {\bpsi^{\mathsf H}\bpsi}\\
 &=\frac{\bpsi^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(\Pi_\pm)\bpsi}
 {\bpsi^{\mathsf H}\bpsi}\\
 &=\frac{\operatorname{tr}
   (\bM_\psi^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(\Pi_\pm)\bM_\psi)}
  {\operatorname{tr}(\bM_\psi^{\mathsf H}\bM_\psi)}.
\end{align}
For an ideal amplitude, its normalized density matrix may therefore be
written in any of the equivalent forms
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\bpsi\bpsi^{\mathsf H}}{\bpsi^{\mathsf H}\bpsi}
 =\frac{\bM_\psi\bM_\psi^{\mathsf H}}
 {\operatorname{tr}(\bM_\psi\bM_\psi^{\mathsf H})}
 =\tfrac12\operatorname{mat}(B).
\end{equation}
For an unrestricted matrix amplitude, the matrix-amplitude expression remains
a valid normalized positive density matrix and may describe a mixed state.
It comes from a single Pauli column precisely when the matrix amplitude has
rank one.

For the Dirac block, apply the same column map to each ideal entry and write
$\bpsi_1,\bpsi_2$ for the resulting columns.  The faithful block action is
\begin{equation}
 \Wop(Z)\bPsi
 \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
 \begin{Bmatrix}
  0&\operatorname{mat}(Z)\\
  \operatorname{mat}(\adj Z)&0
 \end{Bmatrix}
 \begin{Bmatrix}\bpsi_1\\\bpsi_2\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For $Z=S+\bV\cdot\bsig$, this block matrix is
\begin{equation}
 \begin{Bmatrix}
  0&S\bI_2+\bV\cdot\bsig\\
  S\bI_2-\bV\cdot\bsig&0
 \end{Bmatrix},
\end{equation}
which is the standard chiral form used by the Dirac equation.  In this
ordering the conventional Hermitian row entering the Dirac current is
$\{\bpsi_2^{\mathsf H},\bpsi_1^{\mathsf H}\}$.  The normalized spin state in
equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-spin-state} becomes
\begin{equation}
 \tfrac12\operatorname{mat}(B)
 =\frac{\bpsi_1\bpsi_1^{\mathsf H}
        +\bpsi_2\bpsi_2^{\mathsf H}}
       {\bpsi_1^{\mathsf H}\bpsi_1
        +\bpsi_2^{\mathsf H}\bpsi_2}.
\end{equation}
The charge-reversal rule also has a direct column form.  Entrywise
conjugation of a column is denoted by the already defined matrix superscript
$^{\mathsf C}$.  The ideal map gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac1{\sqrt2}\operatorname{mat}(\psi^*\sigma_1)\bm v
 =\bm S^{-1}\bpsi^{\mathsf C},
\end{equation}
so equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-charge-reversal} becomes
\begin{equation}
 \bPsi_c\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
 \begin{Bmatrix}
  \bm S^{-1}\bpsi_2^{\mathsf C}\\
  -\bm S^{-1}\bpsi_1^{\mathsf C}
 \end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:charge-reversal-column-map}
\end{equation}
This is the familiar antisymmetric two-component charge-reversal map in the
chiral ordering used here.
By contrast, the matrix image of
equation~\eqref{eq:dirac-current} is
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}(J)
 ={}&(\bpsi_1^{\mathsf H}\bpsi_1
     +\bpsi_2^{\mathsf H}\bpsi_2)\bI_2\\
 &+\sum_{n=1}^{3}
 \left(-\bpsi_1^{\mathsf H}\bm{\sigma}_n\bpsi_1
       +\bpsi_2^{\mathsf H}\bm{\sigma}_n\bpsi_2\right)\bm{\sigma}_n.
\end{align}
The rotation and boost laws of the two columns are correspondingly
\begin{align}
 \begin{Bmatrix}\bpsi_1'\\\bpsi_2'\end{Bmatrix}
 &=\begin{Bmatrix}
   \operatorname{mat}(R)^{\mathsf H}&0\\
   0&\operatorname{mat}(R)^{\mathsf H}
  \end{Bmatrix}
  \begin{Bmatrix}\bpsi_1\\\bpsi_2\end{Bmatrix},\\
 \begin{Bmatrix}\bpsi_1'\\\bpsi_2'\end{Bmatrix}
 &=\begin{Bmatrix}
   \operatorname{mat}(L)&0\\
   0&\operatorname{mat}(L)^{-1}
  \end{Bmatrix}
  \begin{Bmatrix}\bpsi_1\\\bpsi_2\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{align}
Thus the two columns have the same rotation generator and opposite boost
generators, which is the standard chiral Dirac transformation.

\subsection{Quaternion subalgebra}
\label{sec:quaternions}

The real span of the scalar and bivector basis elements is a quaternion
subalgebra of the complex paravectors.  Define its three imaginary units by
\begin{gather}
 q_1=-\sigma_2\sigma_3=-\ii\sigma_1\\
 q_2=-\sigma_3\sigma_1=-\ii\sigma_2\\
 q_3=-\sigma_1\sigma_2=-\ii\sigma_3,
\end{gather}
or, collectively, $\bq=-\ii\bsig$.  For $n,m\in\{1,2,3\}$, the sigma
multiplication rule gives
\begin{equation}
 q_nq_m=-\delta_{nm}+\sum_{k=1}^{3}\epsilon_{nmk}q_k,
 \label{eq:quaternion-product}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{gather}
 q_1^2=q_2^2=q_3^2=-1\\
 q_1q_2=q_3\\
 q_1q_2q_3=-1,
\end{gather}
with the remaining products fixed by anticommutation.  For $a\in\R$ and
$\bA\in\R^3$, define the real paravector
\begin{equation}
 Q=a+\bA\cdot\bq
 =a-\ii\bA\cdot\bsig.
 \label{eq:quaternion-paravector}
\end{equation}
These are precisely the quaternions embedded in the paravector algebra.  For a
second quaternion $Q'=a'+\bA'\cdot\bq$, equation
\eqref{eq:quaternion-product} gives the familiar product
\begin{equation}
 QQ'=(aa'-\bA\cdot\bA')
 +(a\bA'+a'\bA+\bA\times\bA')\cdot\bq.
\end{equation}

Hermitian conjugation reverses every bivector, while sigma conjugation fixes
it:
\begin{gather}
 q_n^{\mathsf H}=-q_n\\
 q_n^*=q_n.
\end{gather}
Quaternion conjugation is therefore already the Hermitian operation,
\begin{gather}
 Q^{\mathsf H}=a-\bA\cdot\bq\\
 Q^*=Q\\
 \adj{Q}=Q^{\mathsf H}.
\end{gather}
It follows that
\begin{gather}
 QQ^{\mathsf H}=Q^{\mathsf H}Q=a^2+\bA^2=\det(Q)\\
 Q^{-1}=\frac{Q^{\mathsf H}}{a^2+\bA^2}
\end{gather}
for nonzero $Q$.

The faithful matrix representation sends the quaternion units to
\begin{gather}
 \operatorname{mat}(q_1)=
 \begin{Bmatrix}0&-\ii\\-\ii&0\end{Bmatrix}\\
 \operatorname{mat}(q_2)=
 \begin{Bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{Bmatrix}\\
 \operatorname{mat}(q_3)=
 \begin{Bmatrix}-\ii&0\\0&\ii\end{Bmatrix}.
\end{gather}
Hence equation~\eqref{eq:quaternion-paravector} becomes
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{mat}(Q)
 =a\bI_2-\ii\bA\cdot\bsig
 =\begin{Bmatrix}
   a-\ii A_3&-A_2-\ii A_1\\
   A_2-\ii A_1&a+\ii A_3
  \end{Bmatrix}.
 \label{eq:quaternion-matrix}
\end{equation}
The algebraic conjugation and norm become the usual matrix identities
\begin{align}
 \operatorname{mat}(Q^{\mathsf H})
 &=\operatorname{mat}(Q)^{\mathsf H},\\
 \operatorname{mat}(Q)^{\mathsf H}\operatorname{mat}(Q)
 &=\operatorname{mat}(Q)\operatorname{mat}(Q)^{\mathsf H}
 =(a^2+\bA^2)\bI_2,\\
 \det(\operatorname{mat}(Q))&=a^2+\bA^2.
\end{align}
Thus a unit quaternion is represented by a unitary matrix of determinant one.

Finally, for a unit axis $\bu$, let
\begin{equation}
 Q=\cos\frac\theta2+\bu\cdot\bq\,\sin\frac\theta2.
\end{equation}
Since $\bq=-\ii\bsig$, this quaternion is $R^{\mathsf H}$ for the rotor $R$
defined in Section~\ref{sec:transformations}.  The quaternion sandwich
\begin{equation}
 \br'\cdot\bq=Q(\br\cdot\bq)Q^{\mathsf H}
\end{equation}
therefore reproduces the same Rodrigues rotation.

\section{Conventions and cautions}
\label{sec:conventions-cautions}

\begin{itemize}
 \item The same symbol $\ii=\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3$ is both the algebra's
 pseudoscalar and, in the Pauli representation, the ordinary complex unit.
 \item Sigma conjugation $(\cdot)^*$, the Hermitian operator $(\cdot)^{\mathsf H}$,
 and their composition are distinct.  In the Pauli representation,
 the Hermitian operator corresponds to Hermitian adjunction.
 \item $\det(Z)$ is an indefinite quadratic form and may be
 complex for complex $Z$.
 \item Differential operators act on everything to their right unless
 parentheses state otherwise.  This matters in products involving fields.
 \item Vectors and matrices are bold, and only vectors and matrices are bold.
 In particular, the electromagnetic fields formerly written as calligraphic
 or italic \(E\) and \(B\) are the upright bold vectors \(\bE\) and \(\bBf\),
 while scalar energy remains the unbold symbol \(E\).  Blackboard bold is
 retained only for the number sets \(\R\) and \(\C\).

 \item A unit paravector $U$ is defined by the existence of a two-sided
 inverse, $UU^{-1}=U^{-1}U=1$.  In particle applications the local recall
 $U=\dd X/\dd s$ specializes this general notation to proper velocity; that
 real unit additionally satisfies $\det(U)=1$ and $U^{-1}=U^*$.

 \item The action is written as \(\int L\dd t\), avoiding conflict with the
 scalar part \(S\).  The Lagrangian \(L\) and Hamiltonian \(H\) use the regular
 math font.  The Dirac block function is the upright bold \(\Wop(\cdot)\), and
 a vector square is explicitly defined by
 \(\bA^2:=\bA\cdot\bA\).

 \item Curly braces delimit explicit vector-entry lists and matrices, while
 commutators alone use square brackets.  Parentheses delimit functions,
 algebraic grouping, and order-sensitive operator products.
 Redundant grouping parentheses were removed.

 \item The extraction functions \(\operatorname{sc}(\cdot)\),
 \(\operatorname{vec}(\cdot)\), \(\operatorname{re}(\cdot)\), and
 \(\operatorname{im}(\cdot)\) replace \texttt{scalar}, \texttt{vector}, and
 their capitalized variants.  They are defined before use.  The unused
 expectation abbreviation \texttt{Exp} was removed.

 \item The original metric notation
 \(\langle\!\langle Z\rangle\!\rangle^2\) and determinant bars were replaced
 by \(\det(Z)\).  Double bars are reserved for squared norms, with
 \[
  \lVert Z\rVert^2=\lVert S\rVert^2+\lVert\bV\rVert^2.
 \]
 Absolute-value bars are not used for paravector norms.  Entrywise
 complex-conjugation notation is confined to the matrix appendix.

 \item Sigma conjugation retains the splat \(^{*}\).  The operation originally
 written \(^{R}\), and elsewhere as a dagger, is written
 \(^{\mathsf H}\) because it reverses multiplied sigma order and becomes
 Hermitian adjunction in the matrix representation.  The abbreviation
 \(\operatorname{adj}(Z):=Z^{*\mathsf H}\) is defined early and used where it
 shortens formulas.

 \item The matrix appendix uses matching upright sans-serif capitals
 \(^{\mathsf T}\), \(^{\mathsf C}\), and \(^{\mathsf H}\) for transpose,
 entrywise complex conjugation, and Hermitian transpose.  It distinguishes
 these operations from sigma conjugation and states which operations reverse
 product order.

 \item The fixed idempotent $\Pi_3=\tfrac12(1+\sigma_3)$ is multiplied on the
 right to select one minimal left ideal and one Pauli column.  The variable
 projectors $\Pi_\pm=\tfrac12(1\pm\bu\cdot\bsig)$ act from the left and
 represent physical spin measurements.  When $\bu$ is the third-axis
 direction, $\Pi_3$ and $\Pi_+$ are the same algebra element, but right ideal
 selection and left measurement remain different actions.

 \item The adjugate--determinant identity applies directly to algebraic
 paravectors and to free differential paravectors whose coefficient
 operators commute.  Gauge-covariant energy and momentum components do not
 commute; their products remain ordered and their vector remainders are the
 electromagnetic field couplings.

 \item On Dirac blocks, $^{\mathsf H}$ transposes the block and applies the
 same algebraic $^{\mathsf H}$ to every entry, while $^*$ acts entrywise
 without transposing the block.  For spatial differential operators,
 Hermiticity additionally includes the operator domain, boundary conditions,
 and integration by parts.

 \item Plus/minus labels are subscripts
 \(({}_\pm,{}_{+},{}_{-})\), not superscripts or parenthesized labels.
 Parallel and perpendicular components use \({}_{\parallel}\) and
 \({}_{\perp}\) rather than \texttt{par} and \texttt{prp}; their projection
 formulas are restated where needed.

 \item Spatial derivatives use \(\partial_{\br}\) rather than \(\nabla\), and
 no overbars are used.  Literal or malformed source commands such as
 \texttt{quad} and \texttt{frac} were converted to formatted \LaTeX.

 \item Long product expansions were consolidated while retaining their
 distinct components and results.  Expressions otherwise preserve the compact
 ordering of the source, for example
 \(\operatorname{sc}(\Phi^*U)\) with $U=\dd X/\dd s$, without an extra pair
 of grouping parentheses.
\end{itemize}
